# The Segal Method — Extracted Notes

**Source:** *The Architects' Journal*, Special Issue: "The Segal Method", 5 November 1986, pp. 31–68. Written by Jon Broome (self-builder and joint architect with Walter Segal on the Lewisham self-build housing schemes). Extracted from `THE SEGAL METHOD - color.pdf`.

**Scope of source:** Describes the essential features and implications of Walter Segal's timber-frame self-build method, following the self-build sequence from planning through to finishes. Cover photo: two-storey Segal house with pitched roof, exposed dark timber frame, white infill panels, external spiral stair and raised deck.

## Walter Segal's approach

- **Origin (1962):** Segal built a temporary house in his Highgate garden for his family while the main house was rebuilt. Completed in **two weeks** for a materials cost of **£800** (~£6500 at 1986 prices). It still stood in 1986.
- Private clients then commissioned **two dozen or so private houses** in England and Ireland over the next 15 years. A students' residence was designed/built by students in **Stuttgart, 1983**. A house was built by this method in **Australia in 1980** after two long telephone calls. Other architects (e.g. **David Lea** — vicarage at Melling near Liverpool; housing at Churt, Surrey) applied Segal's ideas.
- The method uses **widely available building materials put together in an open-ended way** — the speed and economy of open-system building without the monotony/inflexibility of a closed system.
- Segal **rethought common technical problems from first principles** (e.g. flat-roof failures, condensation), avoiding the defects that afflict much system-built housing.

### A humane architecture
- The houses are not monumental or massive; they show concern for comfort and utility, respond to occupants, and **can be easily altered** — "an architecture of liberation."
- Not to be confused with "prefab": quality as harmony of form, fitness, purpose (per Christopher Alexander), together with being ordinary and unremarkable.
- Essentially an **updated version of medieval timber-framing** using basic products of industry and commonly understood skills. Echoes of a pre-industrial age; could also demonstrate a post-industrial one.

### A potential for self-build
- Simplification lets non-experts build a house and non-architects control design.
- **1971:** first self-build — a school teacher near **Woodbridge, Suffolk** built his own Segal-designed house after watching the carpenter and deciding he could do it himself.
- **1978 Lewisham (south-east London):** 14 families from the council's housing waiting/transfer lists built their own houses. A second Lewisham scheme was nearing completion in 1986. No one excluded by circumstances — houses built by people in their 60s and by a single mother.
- Advantages of self-build under this method:
  - Each family builds its own house **individually from start to finish**, setting its own programme (avoids the pressure of communal rota schemes; parents and children take part — a family enterprise).
  - Self-builders do nearly all operations themselves (no subcontractors) — saves money, avoids coordination problems.
  - Houses need not be identical; the method allows design amendment **during construction** (e.g. move a window to catch a view).
  - Houses can be extended: one family added a bedroom over **three weekends for £1200**.
  - Progressive development can cut initial cost and extend useful life beyond the **60-year design life**.

### Housing standards
- Essential simplicity ≠ highest conventional performance (e.g. sound insulation measures) — but houses are **detached**, so no neighbour noise anyway (typical Segal lateral thinking).
- Thermal insulation standards improved over the method's development. Segal understood there is a point beyond which elaboration negates the advantages of simplicity.
- Segal experimented with new materials/techniques only when based on knowledge or experience — no untried materials of unproven performance.
- The method developed from temporary to permanent houses, from one-off contractor-built designs to self-built houses with high user control.

## The essential method (principles list)

- An **attitude of mind rather than a system of construction**; rigorous simplification of the whole building process from design to completion.
- Process rethought from **first principles** to handle fundamentals: tolerances, thermal and moisture movement.
- Elements reduced to essentials — maximum economy of material and effort.
- Uses **readily available materials and techniques** (not special manufacture) → market competition on price and availability; the method can absorb improvements in techniques and materials.
- Standard materials assembled on site **in their market sizes** → minimal on-site production, minimal waste.
- Building boards (plasterboard, woodwool slabs) form the enclosure within a **framework of standard timber sections**. (The approach could also apply to steel for frame and infill.)
- Materials chosen for performance and cost; workable with **simple hand power tools**; easily handled size and weight.
- Organised on a **modular grid determined by the sizes of standard materials**.
- **Dry jointing only** (bolts and screws) — wet trades (bricklaying, plastering) eliminated.
- Foundations and groundworks reduced to a minimum.
- Buildable by **one person with basic carpentry skills**, except possibly services and roofing.
- Quick and economical construction.
- **Simple documentation:** small freehand A4 diagrams of layout and structure, standard junction details, schedule of materials, set of instructions.
- The **schedule of materials** combines bill of quantities, specification and order list → accurate ordering and cost control from the outset.
- Architect's role includes quantity surveyor and, ideally, engineer.
- The process is **"transparent"** — understandable and controllable by someone without experience; a novice can plan the layout and do the work.
- Buildings feel light and optimistic, are **very adaptable and easy to extend**.
- Produces one- and two-storey buildings with flat or pitched roofs, courtyards, split levels, double-height spaces.

## The planning process

### 1 General arrangement (fig. 18)
Cutaway isometric of a two-storey Segal building showing all numbered elements in place: foundations (10) as discrete pads below posts; structural frame (11) of posts and beams; roof (12); floors (13); external walls (14); windows (15); partitions (16); ceilings (17); stairs (18); services (19); modular grid (2); structural layout (4). Order of description ≈ sequence of execution.

### 2 Modular grid
- Both structural frame and infill walls/partitions planned on a **modular "tartan" grid**: wide bands = panel width (**600 mm or 1200 mm**, from standard board sizes), narrow bands = structural thickness (**commonly 50 mm**).
- Tartan grid lets **corners and columns (generally 50 mm thick)** occur at any grid position **without cutting panels**. Trade-off: a 50 mm gap is left at every joint position in a straight wall (more jointing material) — accepted because grid planning + uncut standard panels outweigh it.
- **Columns are deeper than 50 mm**, so they stand partly outside the grid: placed outside the building where possible, or projecting from partitions; never set longways within the grid (would require cutting panels).
- Client can plan on **squared paper**; drawings can be simple tools (self-builder's floor plan shown drawn on graph paper, fig. 20).
- **Walls carry no load** → walls, doors, windows can go anywhere on either floor and can be changed at any stage, even during construction (fig. 21).
- Vertical dimensions controlled the same way: infill panel height + structural depth of frame. **Ceiling panels fit between joists without cutting** (fig. 22; ~200 mm structural depth shown with 50/600/50 tartan spacing).

### 3 Layout drawings
- Two or three simple **A4 freehand diagrams**: plan and elevations showing positions of walls and openings (figs. 23–24, Lewisham self-build example). Superfluous information omitted.
- Used together with calculations, framing drawings, schedule of materials, catalogue of elements, and building instructions.
- In practice layout drawings are referred to only occasionally once the frame is up and the modular arrangement is legible in the building itself.

### 4 Structural layout
- Structure is a **post and beam frame** (fig. 25a), *not* stud-wall panel construction (fig. 25b).
- A frame is less timber-efficient than panels but: **far smaller/cheaper foundations**, and great planning freedom and adaptability.
- Design order (figs. 26a–c): **plan first** (partition layout decided first), then frame designed to suit the plan; column layout follows partition layout; columns may sit partially outside the tartan grid.
- Columns spaced **up to six modules apart in either direction** — optimum span before beam deflection becomes critical. Gives a structural bay of up to **3.85 m** in either direction (fits most domestic rooms). Composite beams or trusses possible for larger spans.

### 5 Calculations
- Once the structural layout is fixed, the structure is calculated for adequacy and the most economical form, integrating structure with plan and constructional discipline. **Joints get particular attention** (most critical part).
- Building Regulations submission typically includes: dead/imposed load tabulation, wind loads, bending and deflection of critical beams and joists at floor and roof levels, critical columns (axial + bending), joints, wind overturning, soil bearing pressures.
- A set is about **a dozen pages** (example shown: floor joist bending calculation, fig. 27).

### 6 Framing drawings
- Prepared after calculation check; again **A4 freehand diagrams** (ground floor framing plan and sections A-A, B-B shown, figs. 28–29) with the dimensional information needed to construct the frame.
- Typical set: foundation layout, plans at roof levels, sections, joint details — **about eight sheets altogether**.

### 7 Schedule of materials
- Detailed list of all materials for the particular building: each component described with **size, length, finish, and location** in the building, with diagrammatic annotations (fig. 30 example ~10 pages for a house).
- Combination of bill of quantities + specification, prepared by the architect; describes each piece of material accurately.
- Also used as the **ordering list and delivery checklist**; yields a very accurate cost at the outset that will not vary significantly on completion.

### 8 Catalogue of elements
- Standard document per building: series of simple **A4 diagrams of standard details** of each element and how they go together to form the basic shell (fig. 31 shows typical partition/wall junction details: battens, woodwool, plasterboard, screw sizes, e.g. Ex 25×25 battens, 50×50 blocks, 12.7 mm plasterboard, 75 mm No 8 screws).
- With the typical junction arrangements, buildings of many shapes and sizes can be formed.

### 9 Building instructions
- Final documentation element: simple **step-by-step description of the building process**, annotated with diagrams (fig. 32 example: making the main cross-frames — "BE METHODICAL, BE CAREFUL": mark all posts and beams, mark out all joints, mark out notches, cut to length, check actual depth of planed timber, lay out posts and beams for each frame).
- Inexperienced self-builders attend a short course of ~**12 evening sessions**: method + documentation (slides, models, drawings) and workshop sessions building confidence with hand and power tools, making typical joints, basic plumbing and wiring.
- On site people learn by example and pass on information; at Lewisham regular meetings monitor progress. Architect available by phone in the evening and visits weekly (weekends). Mindset: careful, methodical, think operations through, plan ahead.

## The building process

### 10 Foundations
- Essential feature: **the building stands above the ground on posts** rather than being built *in* the ground.
- Foundations are **separate in-situ concrete bases below each post position**: dig hole (building inspector approves bottom), fill with concrete, cap with a **concrete paving slab set at a slight fall**. Posts stand on the slabs **on a piece of lead** forced into the end grain by the building's weight — seals the post against moisture.
- Pad size: about **600 × 600 mm** (or to suit soil bearing pressure), **~900 mm deep** or to suit local ground conditions (fig. 33).
- Topsoil below the building is removed and replaced with **gravel within a border of paving slabs**; **no concrete oversite slab**.
- Concrete quantities small → dug by hand, placed by wheelbarrow (fig. 34): avoids ready-mix and machine excavation costs, suits restricted sites, keeps groundworks self-buildable.
- Foundations built **at whatever ground level exists — no site levelling**. Works on sloping sites without significant cost increase; trees are not damaged by nearby buildings.
- Typical count: **3-bed bungalow ≈ 18 bases; two-storey house ≈ 12**. Isolated pads are less extensive/expensive than continuous foundations under loadbearing walls.
- **Levels are taken and each post cut to length** so floors come out precisely level when frames are erected. The undercroft below the building is a useful storage "cellar" (fig. 35).
- **Posts are not anchored to the foundations** — the building's weight keeps them in place. Once frames are erected, post positions can be adjusted for distance and square **independently of the foundations** (fig. 36: check modular dimensions between frames, check diagonals for square; lead between posts and foundations). Accurate setting-out of foundations is therefore unnecessary — removing an operation inexperienced builders find difficult.
- **Short bored piles** are an economical alternative (no ground beams needed); quick, good for poor soils.

### 11 Structural frame
- Like medieval timber framing: a number of **cross-frames with rigid joints, assembled flat on the ground, raised into position, braced together** (fig. 37: frame assembled flat on the oversite deck).
- Erection sequence (figs. 38–41): frames assembled flat, stacked in order of erection; first frame erected and secured with **struts into the ground**; remaining frames raised one at a time and **temporarily braced to the first with timbers to be used later in construction**, until permanent bracing is fixed. Temporary braces removed one at a time to adjust frames for square and position.
- Timber: **slender sections of stress-graded, imported softwood, pressure treated against rot**.
- **Rigid joints formed with galvanised steel bolts** (in place of medieval mortice-and-tenon with pegs). Form/proportion of the frame has more in common with steel than traditional timber framing; slender sections keep frames light enough to handle by hand.
- **Portal frame behaviour** (fig. 44): rigid joints resist wind in the plane of the frame, but unlike a conventional portal there is a **tie beam at floor level** that eliminates horizontal reactions at the column base — this is what allows the building to stand on the ground unanchored, with no critical foundation tolerances.
- Frames are rigid in one direction; in the other direction they are **braced together**: the floor is fixed to form a **rigid horizontal plate** transferring wind forces to the bracing position (fig. 45). (The roof could act as a plate too.)
- **Bracing:** generally one cross-brace bay per floor level near the centre of the building (figs. 41, 42, 45). Single-storey houses can use **knee braces below floor level** (fig. 43), avoiding the planning limitation of a fixed braced bay above floor.
- **Bolt sizing:** bolt size from joint load and timber type; bolt sizes dictate minimum spacing between bolts and to timber edges (fig. 46).
- **Beams:** centre frames get **double beams**, edge frames **single beams**, so all beams carry a similar load (fig. 49).
- **Joists** fixed between erected frames at roof and floor levels; **spacing and thickness conform to the modular grid** (fig. 47: joists of modular thickness, fixed at modular spacing).
- **Beams and joists have the same depth**: joists fixed with a bearer on the side of beams so that **undersides of beams and joists are at the same level** (figs. 50–51) → nothing projects below ceiling level, all wall panels the same height. Possible because beam timber is much stronger than joist timber.
- Later-use timbers are fixed temporarily to posts as a **working platform** for fixing upper joists — **no scaffolding needed** (fig. 48).
- Frames can cantilever (fig. 52); deflection then governs.

### 12 Roof
- Roof built **immediately after the frame** so all later work happens under cover (fig. 54).
- **Flat roof preferred**: quick, easy, economical, fits any plan shape, allows extension in any direction. Segal rethought flat-roof construction from first principles to avoid its classic failures:
  - **Membrane laid loose** ("tablecloth principle", fig. 55): waterproof layer draped over the roof, **not fixed to deck and not fixed at edges** → structural/deck movement is not transferred to the membrane (no cracks/leaks); membrane free to expand/contract without wrinkles or edge tears. (Many large flat roofs now use this 'loose lay' principle.)
  - **Generous overhang all round** protects the building — creates a "microclimate" of shelter (fig. 53). The **roof outlet is the only penetration**, positioned in the overhang so the joint is fail-safe.
  - **Deck:** woodwool slabs **laid loose on the joists, without falls**; dimensioned to avoid cutting slabs; laid level (fig. 56).
  - **With a perimeter upstand the roof is wet at all times** — keeps the membrane at a relatively even temperature.
  - **Membrane:** conventional **three-layer bituminous felt build-up** (economical), layers hot-bitumen bonded **to one another but not to the deck**. Hot-bonding is a job for a professional roofer; cold bitumen or single-layer butyl rubber membrane could make it fully self-build (eliminating all subcontractors).
  - **Ballast:** membrane weighted with **40 mm of 20 mm shingle**, which also keeps direct sunlight off the felt (fig. 56).
  - **Edge detail (fig. 57):** felt carried up and over a perimeter upstand and turned down over the fascia; if the roof outlet blocks, water simply overflows the upstand harmlessly (and signals the blockage). Edge wedged by a **two-piece timber capping fixed through spacer blocks below the felt edge** — no fixings through the felt itself, so it can expand/contract freely.
  - **Insulation** commonly at ceiling level; void above ceiling ventilated via the loose construction (avoids interstitial condensation). Also very easy to insulate on top as an **upside-down (inverted) roof**.

### 13 Floors
- Fixed after the roof. Generally conventional **tongued-and-grooved softwood boarding on joists** (fig. 58). (Chipboard would be vulnerable to weather since walls aren't up yet.)
- Ground floor is **raised above ground** → insulated against extra heat loss; the raised floor gives a full-extent storage **undercroft**; services run easily in this space.
- **Insulation laid between joists on panels which can be removed** for service access (fig. 59: t&g flooring on joists; panels and insulation on battens fixed to joists, liftable out for access).

### 14 External walls
- Fixed floors provide the working platform. External walls are **non-structural infill clamped into the structural frame with bolts** (bolt heads visible outside, fig. 60).
- Wall = **sandwich of readily available building boards**: insulating and structural core + weatherproof external finish + decorative internal finish.
- **Segal's clothes analogy (fig. 61):** vest (comfortable inside) + sweater (warmth) + raincoat (dry outside). Like loose-fitting clothes, the layers fit **loosely** so air circulates between them and condensation cannot build up. **No reliance on a vapour barrier** (whose integrity can't be guaranteed) — safe, at the cost of higher ventilation heat losses.
- **Typical build-up:** fully compressed **fibre-cement panel with enamel finish (e.g. Glasal) outside**, **painted plasterboard inside**, **50 mm woodwool slab core** between. Materials selected per situation (thermal insulation, design life); panel sizes selected to eliminate cutting and waste.
- Optional extra layers: **urethane foam** for thermal performance; **plastic laminate** inside for washable splashback around a bath.
- **Assembly (fig. 62):** panels clamped together with **timber battens bolted through with three spun galvanised steel bolts** per joint. Battens engage a **sole plate at the bottom** and the **structural joist at the top**; **blocks fixed top and bottom establish the tartan grid position** (fig. 63).
- **Joint is not sealed** (fig. 65): a **weep hole at the bottom of each joint** drains it and ventilates the wall. Elements simply lap over one another → conventional tolerances irrelevant; large tolerance makes the wall easy to build (fig. 64 plan of junction: structural column 50; weatherproof external finish; insulation and structural core; decorative internal finish; corners formed with battens screwed into blocks; infill panels clamped into frame with battens bolted together; 50/600/50 tartan dimensions).
- **Finish as you go:** plasterboard painted and external battens stained before/during assembly — decorating is one of the first operations, not the last.

### 15 Windows
- Fixed after wall panels to make the building weathertight (fig. 66).
- Philosophy: **assemble easily available materials on site to make a window** rather than buying a factory component — avoids tolerance/modular coordination problems, single-source dependence, delivery delays; cuts costs (no transport or factory overheads).
- **Construction (fig. 69):** windows are sheets of glass with **polished edges sliding horizontally in a track of two aluminium angles**, held by timber beads, all within a timber window lining. One angle is fixed **through a spacer washer (tap washer)** to form a **drainage slot** in the track. The angles provide the sliding surface and act as spacer between fixed and sliding panes (**1 mm clearance** so panes slide past one another). Glass **lubricated by running a wax candle** along the pane edge before fixing. Rain falling on the sliding pane runs behind the angle and out through the slot; dry glazing tape seats fixed glass.
- Very adaptable: fixed and opening panes **up to about one metre square** in great variety — storey-height glazing (fig. 67), horizontal strip glazing (fig. 70), varied shapes (Melling vicarage by David Lea, fig. 68). **Double windows** = a second set constructed inside the first.
- Chief disadvantage: **not airtight**. Remedy: clear plastic channel pushed onto the edge of one pane closes the 1 mm gap; foam strip seals the part of the drainage slot not needed below the fixed pane. Conventional windows can also be incorporated.

### 16 Partitions
- Built once weathertight (fig. 71). Same clamping principle as external walls: panels clamped into the frame with timber battens.
- Build-up: **structural core with internal finish both sides** — commonly **50 mm woodwool slab + painted plasterboard each side** (fig. 72). Extra layers possible (e.g. **softboard as pinboard**).
- Engage a **sole plate at bottom** and **structural joist at top**, blocks top and bottom set the tartan grid. Partition running perpendicular to joists needs a **filler piece at its top**.
- **Corners/junctions** formed with timber battens as for external walls. Because the wall is overlapping elements clamped together, boards forming the finish can be **offset off the modular grid at corners** to accommodate their thickness — avoiding cut panels (fig. 73).
- **Door linings made on site** and incorporated in the wall. Fixings for shelving/fittings available at **every batten position**.

### 17 Ceilings
- Fixed after partitions (fig. 74). Commonly **painted plasterboard laid between the joists on battens**; boards **600 mm wide**, only trimmed in length (1200 mm boards cut in half on site). Rooms ≥ 6 modules wide need two boards end-to-end with a **cover strip** at the joint.
- Ceiling below the roof can hold **extra insulation**; boards **lift out for access to services**.
- **Fire resistance:** ceiling below a first floor achieves a **modified half-hour fire resistance** via **sacrificial timber linings fixed to the joists** (fig. 75) — the linings char in a fire, leaving structural joists unaffected. Satisfies Building Regulations; relies on timber's predictable burn rate (thickness ↔ time to burn through).

### 18 Stairs and other features
- Stairs/steps designed to **avoid complicated setting-out, angle cutting, and jointing**: treads supported on **hangers** (fig. 76), **posts** (fig. 77), or **cantilever beams** (fig. 78). Timber members **lap over one another at right angles**.
- Externally, **hardwood — generally keruing —** for treads and walkways (decay resistant).
- The basic structure adapts to many features: **pergola, porch, seat (fig. 80), bay window (fig. 79), trellis, veranda (fig. 81), walkway, balustrade**. Great variety of form, colour and detail is possible within the modular discipline.

### 19 Services
- Construction incorporates **voids in roofs, floors and walls** for services, all accessible by **unscrewing a batten or lifting out a panel** (fig. 83).
- **Lighting** wiring in the **ceiling/roof void**; switch drops run **in the 50 mm spaces between wall panels at joints**.
- **Power ring main** in the **floor void**, rising to socket outlets **at batten positions**; switches and sockets can be positioned on any batten by cutting a cabling hole in it (fig. 82).
- **Pipework** (heating flow/return, gas, hot and cold water) runs in the **floor void**; **waste pipes** suspended in the space **below the ground floor**.

## The future (implications)

- Roles of tradesmen and professionals altered by rigorous simplification of the process.
- Buildings become genuinely adaptable/flexible — "**tools**" to be put to different uses rather than fixed edifices.
- Maintenance attitudes change (easy replacement of worn parts).
- Financing could change: build a **small basic initial house capable of easy upgrading and extension**.
- Kit-of-parts approach → far greater resident choice in public and private developments.
- Many more people could build their own homes with control, variety and satisfaction. (Stockholm City Council has run self-build departments since the 1920s; 10,000-family waiting list in 1986.)
- Choice: an alternative to subsidised mass housing and anonymous mass-produced private housing. Potential for unemployed people to build (self-confidence → employment); small-scale self-regulating communities of self-built houses on vacant urban sites.
- Walter Segal became the only living architect (to the author's knowledge) named in the London A–Z: **Segal Close, SE23** (figs. 84–85); **Walter's Way** to be listed the following year.

## Construction sequence summary (for procedural modelling)

1. **Plan on tartan grid** (600 or 1200 mm modules, 50 mm structural bands); partitions first, then columns (≤6 modules apart, bay ≤3.85 m); columns outside grid/building or projecting from partitions.
2. **Foundations:** discrete concrete pads (~600 sq × 900 deep) capped with paving slabs at post positions only; gravel oversite; no levelling of site.
3. **Posts cut to length** per pad level; stand on lead pads, unanchored.
4. **Cross-frames** (post + beam + floor-level tie, bolted rigid joints) assembled flat, raised, plumbed, braced; joists (same depth as beams, modular spacing) on bearers; central cross-braced bay per floor; double beams at centre frames, single at edges.
5. **Roof immediately:** loose woodwool deck (no falls) → 3-layer felt loose-laid, edge upstand + overhang, single outlet in overhang → 40 mm shingle ballast.
6. **Floors:** t&g softwood on joists; liftable insulation panels between joists.
7. **External walls:** non-structural sandwich panels (Glasal / 50 mm woodwool / plasterboard) clamped by bolted battens to sole plate and joist; blocks set grid; unsealed weep-hole joints; finish as you go.
8. **Windows:** site-made horizontal-sliding glass in aluminium-angle tracks within timber linings; panes ≤ ~1 m².
9. **Partitions:** plasterboard/woodwool/plasterboard clamped panels, any grid position.
10. **Ceilings:** 600 mm plasterboard between joists on battens; sacrificial fire linings under first floors.
11. **Stairs/features:** lapped right-angle timber members on hangers/posts/cantilevers; keruing outside.
12. **Services** last: all in accessible voids (roof, wall joints, floor, undercroft).
