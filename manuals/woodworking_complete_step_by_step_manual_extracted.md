# Woodworking, The Complete Step By Step Manual: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Woodworking, The Complete Step By Step Manual.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://archive.org/download/woodworking-the-complete-step-by-step-manual/Woodworking%2C%20The%20Complete%20Step%20By%20Step%20Manual.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** Woodworking, The Complete Step By Step Manual (previously published as Woodwork), by DK, with contributions from Alan and Gill Bridgewater, Glyn Bridgewater, Colin Eden-Eadon, Sally Francis, John Lloyd, Jonathan Tibbs, and Chris Ward. Published by DK Publishing, a division of Penguin Random House LLC, New York. This American Edition 2020 (first American edition 2010). 400 pages as counted by the PDF page tool. ISBN 978-1-4654-9111-4.

**Scope of source:** A hand-tool and light-machine woodworking course for furniture making: tool identification, wood preparation, a catalogue of 35 named joints with step by step cutting instructions, turning, veneering, finishing, restoring old furniture, a directory of about 100 softwood, hardwood, and veneer species, and 28 furniture and household projects with cutting lists and dimensioned drawings. It does not cover structural carpentry, framing, roofing, stairs, or any building-code span or load table. It gives no nailing or fastening schedules beyond generic screw, nail, and hinge types, and no lumber grading standard beyond a few sentences on softwood "clear" grading. Where the book gives a joint proportion (a ratio or a fraction of stock thickness) that value is transcribed here; where it only shows a step of a hand technique with no rule attached, that is compressed or skipped.

**Most useful for:** the geometry, proportions, and cutting sequence of hand-cut timber joints (mortise and tenon, dovetail, housing, halving, miter, bridle, comb, scarf) for procedurally modelling furniture-scale joinery, and for named furniture project dimensions and cutting lists as reference geometry for small wooden objects and joined assemblies.

## Chapter 1: Tools (pp.12-75)

One paragraph, as this chapter is reference material rather than a source of geometry. Hand tools (pp.14-37): saws are described by teeth geometry (points per inch, or PPI, and "set," the alternating bend of the teeth that cuts a kerf wider than the blade) rather than by a single standard size; panel saws are all-purpose, back saws stiffen the blade for joinery cuts, and Japanese saws cut on the pull stroke instead of the push stroke used by Western saws. Bench planes are numbered by size and pitch (p.18): a no. 4 is a small smoothing plane, a no. 5 1/2 ("jack" plane) is general purpose, a no. 6 ("fore" or "try" plane) is about 18in (450mm) long, and a no. 7 ("jointer" plane) is about 22in (550mm) long, both used for the longest, straightest edges; standard blade pitch is 45 degrees, raised above 45 degrees ("York pitch") for hardwoods, or lowered to about 42 degrees on low-angle planes for end grain. Chisels are sharpened to a 30-degree bevel and come in bevel-edged (general use), paring (thin, never struck), and mortise (thick, struck with a mallet) patterns, in large/medium/small width sets. Measuring and marking tools include steel rulers to 36in (91.4cm), tape measures of 12ft (3.7m) or 25ft (7.6m), dividers, vernier calipers, squares, marking/mortise gauges, and marking knives, with a 2H pencil recommended for crisp lines. Power tools (pp.38-49) cover drills and drivers, routers, handheld power saws, and sanders and nailers; stationary machines (pp.50-62) cover table saws, band saws, planers and thicknessers, lathes, mortisers (capacities and setup detailed on pp.58-59, see quick reference), and drill presses. Commercial joining systems (biscuit, domino, dowel, and pocket-screw jigs) are introduced on pp.60-61 and detailed again in the joinery chapter on pp.138-145. Fixings (screws, nails, pins, hinges, knockdown fixings) are on p.63. Workshop setup (pp.64-75) gives a sample layout built on a 1 x 1ft (305 x 305mm) grid, plus workbenches, sharpening, gluing and clamping, scrapers and abrasives, and personal protective equipment. The numeric values worth pulling out of this chapter are already listed in the quick reference table above; open the relevant page only to check what a named tool looks like.

## Chapter 2: Techniques, introduction and preparing wood (pp.78-87)

Page 80 to 87 describes converting rough-sawn planks into square, accurately sized components, and this is the closest the book comes to a general dimensioning rule outside the joint catalogue itself.

Selecting wood: choose planks flat, straight, and matched in color and grain for glued-up panels; avoid sapwood in visible work; use quarter-sawn stock for stability or figure (p.80).

Rough sizing (p.81): stock must be about 1/4in (6mm) thicker than the finished component to allow for planing (for example, choose 1in/25mm board for a 3/4in/19mm finished part). Add 2in (50mm) extra length per component. Add 1/2 to 1 1/2in (12 to 40mm) extra width, more for long components. Components under 16in (400mm) are not rough sized individually; they are grouped, planed together, then cut to length later. Crosscut before ripping when cutting short pieces.

Facing and edging (pp.82-85): plane a face side flat first (with a no. 5 1/2 jack plane, sole about 15in/380mm, or a no. 7 jointer plane, sole about 24in/600mm, for boards over 6in/150mm wide), then plane a face edge square to it, check with winding sticks and a straightedge, mark thickness and width with a marking gauge from the two reference faces, then plane or saw to the marks. On a planer, remove no more than 1/24in (1.5mm) per pass. On a thicknesser, reduce thickness in 1/32in (1mm) steps for hardwood or 1/16in (2mm) for softwood, and never feed a piece shorter than 18in (450mm) (the rollers cannot grip it).

Cutting to final size (pp.86-87): crosscut with the blade raised about 3/8in (10mm) above the stock. Sheet materials (plywood, MDF) are commonly sold at 96 x 48in (2,440 x 1,220mm); cut oversize by 1/32 to 1/8in (1 to 3mm) and trim to the line with a router and guide to control breakout.

## Chapter 2.1: Joinery, the joint catalogue (pp.88-145)

This is the single most useful chapter for CraftBot. Each joint entry below gives the geometry and proportions stated in the source; the step-by-step hand-tool cutting sequence (marking gauge settings, saw cuts, chisel paring) is compressed since it is a fabrication method, not a modelling rule. Page 88 to 89 is a two-page reference table, "Joints and their uses," listing every joint's strengths, weaknesses, and typical applications; open it directly for that comparison rather than reading it here. It also defines the vocabulary used throughout the chapter: tenon (cheeks, shoulders, haunch), mortise, rebate, groove, tongue, dovetail, and pin (Fig., p.88, "Joint terms" diagram).

Joint stresses (p.88): four kinds of stress affect a joint through its life: tension (pulls a joint apart, as on a drawer front), racking or bending (chairs, tables, unsupported frames), shear (glue line loaded across the joint, as with a scarf or miter), and compression (downforce, as on a sideboard plinth).

### Edge-to-edge joint (pp.90-91)
Two planed, square boards glued face to face along their edges. No mechanical joint; strength comes entirely from the glue line. For long joints, a slight hollow of about 1/32in (0.5mm) is left in the middle of each edge before gluing, giving a "sprung" joint that clamps tight at the ends and resists the joint opening as the wood shrinks. This is the joint used for cutting boards, tabletops, and panels.

### Loose tongue-and-groove joint (pp.92-93)
A groove is cut in each of the two joining edges; a separate loose tongue (usually plywood, since its thickness is uniform) is glued into both grooves. Tongue thickness should be less than one third of the board thickness, or the grooves weaken the board. Tongue width equals the combined depth of both grooves minus about 1/16in (2mm) for clearance. Groove depth is set a little more than the groove width. Used for aligning edge-glued panels and for a decorative contrasting-wood detail.

### Fixed tongue-and-groove joint (pp.94-95)
The tongue is integral, formed by a double rebate cut into one piece with a rebate plane (or router), rather than a separate insert. The groove is cut about 1/32in (1mm) deeper than the tongue length to leave room for glue. Used for paneled backs, floorboards, and cabinet-door panels.

### Cross-halving joint (pp.96-97)
Two crossing pieces each have exactly half their thickness removed, so the four resulting shoulders interlock flush. The fit must be exact or the joint is sloppy. Used for X-frames, stretcher rails, and shoji-style lattice screens. Related joints: the simple lap joint (a rebate with the second piece butted in, glued only, used for trays and boxes), the "T" halving joint (a T-shaped brace, as in a sideboard plinth stretcher), and an oblique or angled halving marked out with a combination square or adjustable bevel.

### Dovetail half-lap joint (pp.98-99)
The tailpiece is cut to half the stock thickness, same as a plain half-lap, but shaped as a dovetail for pullout and racking resistance; it is the strongest of the lap joints. The socket piece is usually the same width and thickness as the tailpiece, though it can differ. The tailpiece end grain is left protruding up to 1/16in (2mm) over the socket piece, then planed flush after gluing. The dovetail angle is set with a dovetail marker positioned at least 1/8in (3mm) from the shoulder edge. Used for top drawer rails and back rails let into an existing frame; can be doubled as a twin dovetail on a wide rail.

### Corner-halving joint (pp.100)
Both pieces are the same thickness; each is reduced to half its width so the two mirror-image laps interlock at a corner, with an end-grain shoulder on each. No mechanical lock, relies entirely on glue (can be reinforced with screws or dowels). Ends are left about 1/16in (2mm) proud and planed flush after gluing. Used for backs of carcasses and internal dust frames.

### Scarf joint (p.101)
Two identical pieces are each cut with a shallow-angle bevel, roughly four times the stock width in length, then butted together face to face. The long, shallow bevel gives a large gluing area on end grain (which absorbs more glue, so priming first helps) and greater shear strength than a plain butt joint. Used to lengthen carpentry members (dado rails, architraves, skirting), not usually in furniture; can be reinforced with a loose tongue, screws, nails, or a fixing plate.

### Full (through) housing joint (pp.102-103)
A groove (the housing) is cut across the full width of one piece to receive the square end of another (the tenon piece). Housing width equals the tenon-piece thickness exactly. Housing depth is one third to one half of the housing-piece thickness, and never more than half or the housing piece is weakened. The tenon end grain is visible on both face edges, so this joint is used where that does not matter (shelves in bookcases, plinths) or is hidden.

### Shouldered and stopped housing joint (pp.104-105)
A variant of the full housing that hides the joint from the front. The tenon piece is cut with a shoulder on one side (housing width is narrower than the tenon-piece's full thickness), and the housing itself is stopped short of the front edge of the housing piece (the "stop"). Housing depth can be up to half the housing-piece thickness. Tenon length equals the housing depth minus about 1/32in (1mm) for glue clearance. Used for drawer divisions and dividers where the front edge stays clean and can be shaped (rounded, chamfered).

### Dovetail housing joint (pp.106-107)
Like the full housing joint, but shaped as a dovetail so it resists both pullout and racking. Housing depth is one third to one half of the stock thickness. The dovetail angle should be a standard 1 to 8 ratio for hardwood or 1 to 6 ratio for softwood (cut with a router and dovetail cutter, following a fence). Housing width is set about 1/4in (6mm) less than the tailpiece thickness before the dovetail profile is routed in. The tailpiece is usually driven in from the back of a carcass, so the housing is commonly stopped at the front. Used for open bookshelves, arm-to-side-rail joints on carver chairs, bed frames, and knockdown assemblies.

### Butt-miter joint (p.108)
Two pieces of equal width and thickness are each cut to 45 degrees on the end grain and glued face to face, with no mechanical lock at all; strength depends entirely on end-grain glue, the weakest gluing surface, so the miter angle should be kept as shallow (small) as practical. Used for picture frames and applied frames on tabletops or panels. Commonly reinforced with a loose tongue in a slot (a "loose-tongue miter," effectively the p.110 joint), veneer keys (p.111), biscuits or dominoes (pp.138-141), or dowels (pp.141-144).

### Mitered half-lap joint (p.109)
Combines a half-lap (each piece cut to half its thickness) with a 45-degree miter cut on the top face of each piece, so only one end-grain face shows. The two halves (referred to as piece A and piece B) are cut slightly differently from each other. About 1/16in (2mm) extra length is left at the end grain and planed flush after gluing.

### Loose-tongue miter joint (p.110)
A mortise is cut into each mitered end grain (triangular in section: a vertical edge from the top, a horizontal edge from the bottom) to hold a loose tongue, ideally 1/4in (6mm) birch plywood, square in section, sized to match the mortise depth. Functions like a floating tenon (p.136) across a miter.

### Keyed miter joint (p.111)
Starting from a glued butt-miter, saw cuts are made straight through the mitered corner (kerf width matched to the veneer thickness) and veneer strips are glued into the kerfs as decorative, structural keys. Keys are usually evenly spaced along the miter and can be a contrasting wood or color.

### Basic mortise-and-tenon joint (pp.112-115)
The mortise is a slot (stopped, as described here, or cut fully through the piece); the matching tenon's side faces are called cheeks and its end faces are shoulders. Tenon thickness should be between one third and one half of the mortise-piece thickness: too thick weakens the mortise piece, too thin weakens the tenon. About 1/16in (2mm) of clearance is left between the tenon end and the base of the mortise for glue. This is the joint used in the Bedside Table project (pp.296-307). Named variants: the double or twin tenon (two tenons side by side in matching mortises, larger gluing area, strong against racking, especially with a shoulder), the stub tenon (a smaller, generally square tenon for fitting drawer rails to carcasses), and the carcass tenon added to a housing joint for extra strength.

### Haunched-tenon joint (p.116)
A basic mortise-and-tenon variant used where a groove for a panel runs the full length of a frame stile. The mortise is cut to reduced depth near one end (leaving a shallower section, the haunch position), and a matching haunch is cut into the tenon to fill and strengthen that end of the panel groove. Haunch length is about one third of the mortise length.

### Secret haunched-tenon joint (p.117)
Differs from the standard haunched tenon in that the haunch is sloped (tapered), chiselled at about 45 degrees from the outer edge down to a point, so the haunch is hidden once assembled rather than visible at the edge. The mortise is usually blind. Used where a haunch would otherwise show, such as a chair's front rail-to-leg joint or a low cabinet's door top rail.

### Wedged through mortise-and-tenon joint (pp.118-120)
The tenon extends fully through the mortise piece. On the far (exit) face, the mortise is flared or splayed outward with a chisel, to almost its full depth, leaving the near-side edges square; the flare width matches the intended wedge thickness. Two diagonal slots are sawn into the end grain of the protruding tenon, starting about 1/8in (3mm) from the shoulder. Tapered wedges, cut from stock the same thickness as the mortise width, are driven into the slots so the tenon spreads to fill the flared mortise and lock permanently; no glue is required, though it can be added for extra long-term strength. Used for chair back legs and any carcass joint needing maximum pullout resistance; a decorative feature if contrasting-wood wedges are used (as in the Bookcase project, pp.234-239).

### Knockdown tenon joint (p.121)
A tenon protrudes far enough past the mortise piece to carry a second, smaller mortise for a removable peg. The peg/mortise width should be no more than one third of the tenon width. The peg is tapered on one edge so it fits loosely into its mortise, then is driven tight; pulling the peg lets the whole joint be knocked down. No glue is used. Common in Asian furniture and Arts and Crafts pieces, and for large knockdown items such as bookcases and beds.

### Draw-bore tenon joint (pp.122-123)
Predates reliable glue. A peg hole ("bore") is drilled through both cheeks of the mortise, positioned at least 1/2in (12mm) from the tenon end. The tenon is assembled dry, then a bradawl or drill mark is used to record the bore position on the tenon, and the tenon-side hole is redrilled about 1/16in (2mm) closer to the shoulder than the mortise-side hole. Driving a tapered peg (dowel) through the offset holes pulls the shoulders tight against the joint. Assembled dry; the wedging action alone holds it, though glue can add strength.

### T-bridle joint (pp.124-126)
An open-mortise-and-tenon variant: the mortise is a U-shaped open slot that completely straddles ("bridles") the tenon piece, giving racking resistance even without glue, unlike other bridle joints. The mortise gauge is set to just over one third of the mortise-piece thickness to mark both the mortise width and the matching tenon (bridle) thickness. Used mid-rail, such as carcass panel frames, or to join a leg to a table's top rail.

### Corner bridle joint (p.127)
The same construction as the T-bridle but with the open mortise cut at the very end of the mortise piece, forming a corner rather than a T. Mortise gauge set to one third of the wood thickness. Both end grains stay exposed, giving a large glued face-grain area but no mechanical lock; strengthened variants add a square peg or two diagonal pegs. Used for chair leg-to-seat joints, sometimes tripled for extra gluing area.

### Comb (finger) joint (pp.128-129)
A simplified through-dovetail: interlocking square pins ("fingers") on both pieces, cut parallel rather than angled. Marking gauge is set slightly more than the wood thickness so pins come out very slightly oversize, then are planed flush after assembly. Strongest when the pins on both pieces are the same size. Little mechanical strength on its own; relies on the large glued surface area. Used for boxes and carcasses, often with contrasting woods for decoration.

### Through-dovetail joint (pp.130-133)
The classic interlocking tails-and-pins joint, strong in tension, used at box and carcass corners. Layout: a half-pin length is set at both ends of the tailpiece end grain with dividers; a combined tail-plus-pin-socket length is chosen and "walked" with dividers between the two half-pin marks, adjusting until a whole number of repeats exactly fills the span (or the spacing can be calculated with a ruler). Shoulder depth is set with a marking gauge to a fraction more than the tailpiece thickness. The dovetail angle is drawn with a dovetail marker (a fixed-angle template tool); the source does not state a numeric angle or ratio for the through-dovetail specifically (compare the 1 to 8 / 1 to 6 ratio given for the dovetail housing joint, p.106). Variants named in the text: lapped dovetails (below), double-lapped dovetails (hide the tails on carcass work), secret-mitered dovetails (fully hidden, difficult), single- and half-lap dovetails (drawer-opening rails), and dovetail keys or splines (contrasting-wood inserts on box sides or tabletop butt joints).

### Lapped dovetail joint (pp.134-135)
Like a through-dovetail, but the pin sockets stop short of the socket piece's front face, leaving an uncut "lap." Lap-to-dovetail-depth ratio is usually 1 to 3, and can go as thin as 1 to 4, but no thinner or the lap risks being cut through while cleaning the sockets. The half pin is made thicker than the full pins for strength. Used for drawer fronts (front stays visibly unbroken) and for hidden carcass joints such as chest-of-drawers sides.

### Floating tenon joint (pp.136-137)
Also called a loose or slip tenon: both pieces get a mortise (there is no true tenon), connected by a loose tongue, usually plywood, no more than two thirds the width of the tenon piece, sized square to the mortise gauge setting. Mortise depth is one third of the piece's thickness plus about 1/32in (1mm) for glue. Functionally equivalent to a mortise-and-tenon but faster to cut since there are no shoulders to mark, making it efficient wherever many small tenons are needed. This joint is the hand-tool ancestor of biscuit and domino connectors (below).

### Joints using commercial connectors (pp.138-145)
- Biscuits: oval, compressed-beech plates fitted into matching elliptical slots cut by a biscuit jointer; three standard sizes, 0 (smallest), 10, and 20 (largest, p.61). Can be doubled for extra strength; works on edge-to-edge, face-to-edge, corner, and miter joints.
- Dominoes: a compressed-beech, fluted, rounded-lozenge loose tenon cut by a dedicated domino jointer; standard machine cuts slots 3/16 to 3/8in (4 to 10mm) wide, the larger Domino XL machine cuts 5/16 to 9/16in (8 to 14mm); can be doubled side by side as a twin "tenon."
- Dowels: round pegs acting as mini loose tenons in drilled holes; commercial dowels are fluted to relieve glue/air pressure on insertion. Hole depth is usually up to half the piece thickness; alignment uses a center point (single dowel) or a dowel jig with guide bushes (multiple dowels, spaced with an E-clamp).
- Pocket-screw joint (Kreg system): an acute-angle, counterbored hole drilled with a stepped bit through a dedicated jig, housing a self-tapping screw; used where a mortise-and-tenon is impractical, such as attaching rails to a tabletop or kitchen-cabinet carcass work.

## Chapter 2.2: Jigs, turning, veneering, finishing, restoring (pp.146-171)

### Jigs and templates (pp.146-147)
MDF is recommended for jigs and templates because it does not move with humidity. Named examples: a drilled-hole jig for repeat shelf-peg holes (used in the Glass-Fronted Cabinet project, pp.334-341); a tapering-thickness jig for feeding an angled workpiece through a thicknesser (only for pieces over 18in/450mm long, reducing thickness 1/32 to 1/16in/1 to 2mm per pass); a tapering-width jig (an L-shaped sled) for a table saw; and a template for cutting and routing a smooth curve with a bearing-guided cutter.

### Turning techniques (pp.148-152)
Spindle turning between centers: stock is mounted between headstock and tailstock, the tool rest set about 3/8in (10mm) from the stock. A profile (square sections, beads, coves, tapers, cylinders) is planned on a cardboard template and transferred to the spinning stock with a pencil. Beads and coves are roughed out with a parting tool cut to slightly over the final diameter (about 3/16in/5mm over for beads, 1/16in/2mm over for coves), then finished with spindle gouges, skew chisels, and a beading tool, checking diameters with calipers throughout. A friction wax-polish can be applied while the piece still spins.

### Veneering (pp.153-159)
Veneer sheets are commonly available about 8in (200mm) wide and are joined side by side (slip-matched, book-matched, or quarter-matched) to cover larger areas. A veneered panel needs both a face veneer and a balancing (backing) veneer glued to a core, normally MDF, or it will warp regardless of thickness. Gluing uses PVA spread on both core faces, with tapered clamping cauls (about 1/16in/2mm taper) placed every 6in (150mm) along the piece, or historically hot animal glue rubbed down with a veneer hammer. Exposed board edges are covered either with lipping (solid wood strip, cut 1/32in/1mm wider than the core thickness, mitered at the corners) or with a strip of matching face veneer glued and clamped, then trimmed flush.

### Finishing techniques (pp.160-167)
One paragraph, since this chapter is procedural rather than dimensional. Order of work is preparation (sanding progressing through 120 to 240 grit, filling holes with wood filler or a plugged peg, scraping with a cabinet scraper or scraper plane, optionally raising the grain by dampening then sanding again) followed by a final finish. A table on p.163 compares nine finish types (danish oil, tung oil, wax polish, hard wax oil, french polish, polyurethane varnish, acrylic varnish, wax finish varnish, cellulose lacquer, acid-catalyst lacquer) by sheen, durability, and application method. Staining, liming, oiling, and waxing each get a short illustrated sequence; none carry dimensional rules.

### Restoring furniture (pp.168-171)
One paragraph. Two guiding principles: every repair should be reversible, and original surfaces should be preserved. Old joints are dismantled by softening animal glue with methanol, steam, or heat, not by force; modern resin-glued joints usually have to be sawn apart instead. A false tenon can be spliced in to replace one that was cut away, matched to the original mortise angle (the source gives an example angle range of 30 to 40 degrees for one repair, p.169). Loose veneer is reglued by "hot blocking" (a heated MDF or plywood block clamped over the area) or by injecting warm animal glue underneath. French polish (shellac dissolved in alcohol) is the traditional antique finish. White water rings are cut back with fine abrasive or ash-and-oil paste; black stains are treated with oxalic acid (wear full protective gear, p.171).

## Rules for modelling

This source is a reference and technique guide, not a single procedural build; use these as rules to apply when a modelling task calls for a specific joint or furniture-scale geometry, rather than as one linear sequence.

1. Pick a joint by function, not by habit: use the "Joints and their uses" table (pp.88-89) to match required strength (tension, racking, shear, or compression resistance, p.88) to a joint, then open that joint's page for its proportions.
2. Mortise-and-tenon family: set tenon thickness to one third to one half of the mortise-piece thickness (p.112); add a haunch (about one third of the mortise length, p.116) wherever a tenon sits at the end of a stile groove; taper the haunch at about 45 degrees if it must stay hidden (p.117); through-tenons meant to be locked mechanically get wedges driven into diagonal end-grain slots after the mortise is flared on the exit face (pp.118-120).
3. Housing joints: set housing depth to one third to one half of the housing-piece thickness, never more (p.102); narrow the housing and add a stop if the joint must not show at the front edge (p.104); use a 1 to 8 (hardwood) or 1 to 6 (softwood) dovetail angle on the housing walls wherever pullout resistance is also needed (p.106).
4. Half-lap and halving joints: remove exactly half the stock thickness from each crossing member so the shoulders meet flush (pp.96, 98, 100); shape one member as a dovetail if the joint also needs pullout resistance (p.98).
5. Miter joints: cut both mating faces at 45 degrees on the end grain for a plain butt-miter (p.108); add a loose tongue, keyed veneer inserts, or commercial connectors whenever the miter must carry real load, since a plain miter has no mechanical lock (p.108, p.110, p.111).
6. Dovetails: set the lap-to-depth ratio to 1 to 3 (down to 1 to 4 minimum) for a lapped dovetail hiding one face (p.134); space tails and pins evenly by dividing the joint width into an integer number of equal tail-plus-socket units (pp.130-131); make the half pin thicker than the interior pins on a lapped dovetail for strength (p.134).
7. Bridle joints: set the mortise-gauge width to just over one third of the mortise-piece thickness (pp.124, 127); use the T-bridle (mortise straddles the tenon on both sides) wherever the joint sits away from an end and needs racking resistance without relying on glue (p.124).
8. Loose-tenon and commercial connectors: when substituting a floating tenon, biscuit, domino, or dowel for a cut mortise-and-tenon, keep the loose element no more than two thirds of the piece width (floating tenon, p.136), or use the standard biscuit sizes 0/10/20 (p.61) or domino widths of 3/16 to 3/8in (4 to 10mm), 5/16 to 9/16in (8 to 14mm) on the XL machine (p.61).
9. Stock preparation: size rough lumber about 1/4in (6mm) over finished thickness, plus 2in (50mm) extra length and 1/2 to 1 1/2in (12 to 40mm) extra width, before planing to the marked face and edge (p.81).
10. Furniture-scale reference: when a project needs plausible overall dimensions for a named furniture type (table, chair, chest, cabinet, bed), use the project dimensions table above as a scale reference; it covers the same range of household furniture CraftBot is likely to model.
