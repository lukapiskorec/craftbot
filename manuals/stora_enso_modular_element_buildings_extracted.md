# Stora Enso design manual for modular element buildings: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Stora Enso Design Manual - Modular Element Buildings.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://www.storaenso.com/-/media/Documents/Download-center/Documents/Product-brochures/Wood-products/Design-Manual-A4-Modular-element-buildings20161227finalversion-40EN.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** Building Systems by Stora Enso. 3 to 8 Storey Modular Element Buildings. Design manual, version 4.0. Stora Enso, Division Wood Products, Building Solutions, published 26 December 2016. No ISBN. 96 pages including covers, landscape A4.

**Scope of source:** A preliminary design manual for Stora Enso's CLT (cross laminated timber) modular building system for wood multi-storey residential buildings, 3 to 8 storeys. Covers the anatomy of a modular building and of a single modular element, architectural design guidelines, structural principles (acoustics, fire, deformation, HVAC routing, bracing, seismic design), a catalogue of wall, floor, roof and stair build-ups with fire and acoustic ratings, a full set of module-to-module and module-to-ground connection drawings, and transportation, erection and site protection instructions. It does not give span tables, load calculation worksheets, nailing or screw schedules, exact panel dimensions, or country-specific transport envelopes: fastener sizing is repeatedly deferred to "the structural engineer" and module size is repeatedly described as limited by production, transport and lifting capacity without a stated number. All solutions are explicitly preliminary and subject to full engineering verification.

**Most useful for:** modelling a CLT panel-and-slab modular residential building, its module-to-module and module-to-ground joints (vertical tension, horizontal shear, acoustic separation layers), a floor plan of stacked room and technical modules around a central corridor and stair, and the erection sequence of modules floor by floor.

## Chapter notes

### 1. Introduction and disclaimer

The manual targets designers, contractors, owners and developers. The core of the system is Stora Enso's CLT wall panels and floor slabs, which are simultaneously the load-bearing structure and the finished envelope of the apartment; elements are built in factory conditions and delivered to site with finishing materials, technical installations and fixtures already installed. The manual explicitly covers preliminary design only. All structural solutions and reference values need review and approval by responsible structural, acoustic, fire and building physics designers before use; conformance with local codes is the designer's responsibility. Detailed drawings can be downloaded from the Stora Enso Building Solutions web pages (referenced but not included here).

### 2. Anatomy of the Stora Enso modular building system

Fig., p. 7: axonometric cutaway of a modular building, labelling from top to bottom: roof (prefabricated and/or site-built), room modules (prefabricated), technical modules (prefabricated), elevator shaft (massive wood, check local fire rules), central corridor (assembled on site: massive wood slabs, beams and stairways), facade (factory or site assembled), HVAC/vertical shafts (site assembled), balcony zone (prefabricated, various types possible), surface structures (finished on site after technical installation), additional facade panels, and a concrete ground floor/basement/foundation (commercial or storage space, cast on site).

Fig., p. 8: axonometric of a single modular element exploded into layers, top to bottom: module roof slab (CLT), wall surface structure, non-load-bearing partition walls, load-bearing wall panels (CLT, high load-bearing capacity), floor surface structure, HVAC installations and covers, fixed furniture, doors and windows, insulation, facade (installed in factory and/or on site). Basic module types are the room module (living room, bedroom, dining room, workspace) and the technical module (kitchen, bathroom, toilet).

Table, p. 9, compares three construction technologies (Building components / Prefabrication / Dimensions / Complexity / Construction): building on site (low prefabrication, no size limit, long construction phase), element construction (factory finished 2D panels, wide range of dimensions, shorter phase), modular element (factory finished 3D elements, extremely high prefabrication, limited dimensions, repetition of modular units, shortest construction phase). Stated benefits of the massive wood structure: good load-bearing capacity (even 8 to 10 storeys possible), stable and clear structure with easy and secure connections, tight and massive cross-layer structure with high technical performance, and easy openings.

### 3. Architectural design guidelines

Repetition and variation (p. 11): a spectrum from maximum repetition with a single module type A (typical for hotels, small apartment housing, student housing, elderly homes) through an optimal mix of a handful of module types A to G (typical for normal multi-storey housing, the recommended sweet spot for project economics and architectural quality) to unlimited variation with minimal repetition (exclusive housing, special projects).

Building typology (p. 12): footprint, unit distribution and vertical circulation are the basic parameters. A symmetrical layout with a central core optimises load-bearing structure and shear walls. Diagrams show footprint variants (rectangular block, L-shape, point block, slab with wings) and corresponding unit layout variants around single or double access cores. Case study: an 8-storey central corridor block, and a central corridor block with six units per floor.

Structural principles (p. 13): modular elements, core walls and apartment-dividing walls are the usual shear and load-bearing structure; even long shear walls may carry openings subject to calculation. Load-bearing outer walls and slabs are sized for optimal span and to avoid load-bearing elements inside the unit; the orientation of the modules controls which facade can carry larger or more frequent openings. Wet zones (baths, toilets, kitchens) sit next to vertical installation shafts, avoiding long horizontal drain runs or shafts penetrating through elements. Additional elements built on site (corridors: slabs, beams, columns, stairways; roof; balconies) are installed simultaneously with the module erection phase.

Modular system variations (p. 14): standard modules vs cantilevered/recessed modules (cantilevers may need supporting columns depending on opening size and cantilever length) vs cantilevered balconies. Balcony types: balcony zone (continuous), suspended balconies, recessed balconies, free-standing balcony towers. Roof types over the upper floor: flat, pitched, shed, rooftop terrace.

Apartment layout (p. 14): single-module apartment = one technical/wet module containing sanitary unit and services. Double-module apartment = technical module plus one room module. Multi-module apartment = technical module plus multiple room modules, for larger units with more bedrooms.

Case study, pp. 15 to 16: a floor plan with six units per floor around a central stairway, room modules (blue outline) and technical modules (green outline) shown on a floor plan; rendered elevations; four labelled cross-sections a-a through d-d showing the vertical stack of modules over a taller ground floor.

### 4. Building System by Stora Enso

**4.1 Structural components** (p. 18). The CLT panels are simultaneously load-bearing structure and envelope, and in most cases visible interior surface. Module size is limited by: maximum production size of the massive wood panels, national road transport limits, spatial limits of the factory, and maximum lifting weight (heavier for elements with sanitary facilities). No numeric envelope is given (see Quick reference). The exploded axonometric on this page (massive wood walls, massive wood slabs) shows a technical module with a sanitary unit and an adjoining room module, with call-outs for "max width", "max length" and "max height" all annotated as "note the production dimensions of panels" rather than as numbers; a wall-to-wall connection gap of 60 to 100 mm and a slab thickness note of 100 to 180 mm appear on the same figure. An open (partially assembled) modular element needs temporary supports during lifting and assembly.

**4.2 Manufacturing process** (p. 19). Three-stage flow, each shown as a triptych of factory photos/renders: (1) slab elements: flat massive wood panels ordered with cut-outs and edge forming, then fitted with insulation and sheathing, ready to connect. (2) wall elements: same process, panels equipped with insulation, sheathings and frame structures. (3) modular elements: wall and slab elements are connected to form the module, building services and interior fixtures are installed, then the finished module is wrapped and transported to site.

**4.3 Principles of building acoustics** (p. 20). Four acoustic challenges are addressed: airborne sound (reduced by the layered wall/floor structure along the direct sound path), impact sound (floating surface structure plus insulation reduces impact sound at source), flanking transmission (separated load-bearing structures per apartment, i.e. double-wall and double-floor structures at apartment boundaries, cut the indirect sound path through the frame), and plumbing noise (main plumbing lines run in the corridor rather than through apartments, and are isolated from the frame with insulated hangers). All connections between separate apartments use vibration pads to control flanking transmission.

**4.4 Principles of fire design** (p. 21 to 22). Three exposure principles for massive wood layups: Principle 1, exposed massive wood (no protective layers, full fire resistance from the char layer of the massive wood itself); Principle 2, limited encapsulation (protective layers on the wood, wood allowed to char); Principle 3, complete encapsulation (protective layers, wood not allowed to char). Verification is by EN13501-1 (reaction to fire) and EN13501-2 or EN1995-1-2 charring calculation (fire resistance). Design procedure shown as a 4-step worked example on a 4 vs 7 storey wall, floor span 4.4 m: (1) define fire design loads per EN1990/1991/1995, (2) define fire resistance of protective layers, (3) define charring depth for the required duration, (4) verify load-bearing capacity of the residual cross-section under the loads from step 1. Reference wall design loads given: walls A/B (4 storeys) 117/217 kN/m, walls C/D (7 storeys) 194/357 kN/m; residual section fire resistance 60/90 minutes. Horizontal fire barriers in the ventilating slot at each floor prevent vertical fire spread on the facade; all joints between modules need fire sealing.

**4.5 Principles of controlling deformations and cracking** (p. 23 to 24). Deformation sources: moisture-driven swelling/shrinkage of timber, creep, and compression. CLT dimensional change: 0.02 percent length change per 1 percent MC change parallel to grain in the panel plane, 0.24 percent per 1 percent MC change perpendicular to the panel layer (i.e. through the panel's cross-layers, which matters where a CLT ceiling panel sits between two wall panels and adds to the stack-up settlement). Manufacture MC: CLT 10 to 14 percent, LVL 8 to 10 percent; service MC 7 to 13 percent; ambient RH 20 to 60 percent. Modulus of elasticity: CLT 12,500 MPa parallel / 370 MPa perpendicular to grain; LVL 10,000 to 13,800 MPa parallel / 130 to 2,400 MPa perpendicular; vibration pad E approx. 0.5 to 10 MPa. Worked settlement example for one storey under F = 30 kN/m loads from the two modular elements above plus the roof: wall panel elastic deformation with creep u = 1.6 mm, ceiling panel (perpendicular to grain) u = 1.0 to 3.0 mm depending on position, vibration pad u = 0.2 mm; total settling quoted as 4.8 mm for one storey. Connections: vertical settlement requires slotted holes in steel parts so tension rods can be retightened after settling; vertical HVAC piping needs a connection detail that allows for settling. Cracking: wood cracks when tension stress perpendicular to grain is exceeded; main causes are uncontrolled on-site drying and moisture cycling (summer to winter); rapid drying can cause visible cracks on exposed CLT surfaces, and this is treated as a normal, designed-for phenomenon.

**4.6 Principles of HVAC design** (p. 25). Centralised ventilation: main ducts run horizontally through suspended ceilings and vertically through plumbing cavities located in the corridors (to keep plumbing noise out of apartments), aiming for short, simple routes and avoiding difficult penetrations through structure. Three pipe location options are diagrammed: (A) pipes in the suspended ceiling with the fire compartment border at the ceiling, (B) pipes between modules with the fire compartment border in the ceiling, (C) pipes between modules with the fire compartment border in the floor. Any penetration through a beam needs careful design; use fireproof sealing at compartment borders.

**4.7 Bracing systems** (p. 26). Bracing is based on modular elements; local loads, conditions and codes govern. Guidelines: consult local wind/seismic conditions and codes; use symmetrical floor layouts to reduce torsional vibration; provide enough shear walls for overall stiffness; plan openings in shear walls carefully (size, location, number). Calculations must consider design loads (including fire, progressive collapse and seismic accident cases per current EN standards), ultimate limit state (equilibrium loss, fracture), service limit state (deformation, vibration), and the load-bearing capacity/stiffness of members and connections. Two bracing schemes are diagrammed: bracing with shear walls (horizontal shear-transfer connections at each floor, a vertical shear connection, tension/compression connections resisting wind and geometric-imperfection loads, with module ceilings fastened to each other and anchored to the core), and bracing with a concrete core (shear connection and uplift connection from the timber structure into the concrete core, with a pinned connection that allows settlement).

**4.8 Principles of seismic design** (p. 27). The system can be designed to Eurocode 8. Wood advantages in seismic areas: low density (lower seismic mass), high strength-to-weight ratio, better damping than concrete due to the material and the joints used. Principles for modular elements: modules stay intact as modules, i.e. no brittle fracture in internal connections; connections between modules must have sufficient yielding capacity (ductile, ideally not brittle); forces are directed to stiffening structures with enough capacity, and then to foundations with sufficient anchorage. Ductility class DCM or DCH per EN1998-1 table 8.1, behaviour factor q approx. 2 to 3 for a multistorey timber building. Design steps: conceptual design, seismic action, then connection details; seismic actions depend on site hazard, soil quality, building class (residential = class II) and structural system.

**4.9 Erection procedure sequence** (p. 28). The erection sequence affects structural, architectural, HVAC and electrical design and must be considered in floor layout and pipe/cable routing. In the case study, erection proceeds one apartment at a time, starting from the technical module (situated next to the corridor for easy service connections through the corridor shafts); centralising services in the technical modules keeps pipe and cable runs short and maintenance easy, and avoids complex vertical shafts inside apartments. A numbered plan (modules 1 to 13) shows the sequence across a floor. Key erection notes: connection hardware in the gap between two neighbouring modules can only be fixed to the module installed first, because the second module's installation blocks access to the gap, so the erection order determines where connection hardware can go and which walls can act as shear walls; overhanging components must be checked so they do not obstruct positioning of the next module; in the worked example the technical module must be installed before the adjoining room module because the room module's floor slab rests on a support on the side of the technical module. Referenced connection drawings for this sequence: VD09 (staircase floor slab placed between modules), VD05 (vertical connection plates fastening each module), VD07 (horizontal plates between ceiling panels connecting neighbouring modules), VD08 (drain/pipe/cable connections through the staircase shaft, closed with lightweight walls), VD15 (sealing the joint between two modules forming one room), VD01 (finishing the seam after the vertical connection plates are fastened).

### 5. Structural design

**5.1 Structural types** (pp. 30 to 50): a build-up catalogue keyed to a "List of Drawings" and an orientation chart (p. 30) that locates each type on a building section. Naming convention: US = load-bearing external wall, VSK = load-bearing partition wall, VP = CLT slab intermediate floor, YP = roof structure, AP = base floor, VS = non-load-bearing partition wall, E-VP/E-VSK = extension types (recessed balcony variants). For every type the catalogue gives lettered material options (A, B, C...), an axonometric or section diagram with a labelled layer stack from outside to inside, and a table of insulation thickness, total thickness, minimum CLT cross-section (for both R60 and R90, at 4 and 7 storeys), U value where relevant, surface reaction to fire, and airborne/impact sound ratings Rw and Ln,w. See the Quick reference table above for the numeric highlights of each type; the full 20-plus build-up sheets (US1, VSK1 to 5, VP11, VP2, VP3, VP41, VP5 stairs, YP1, YP2, AP1, VS1, VS2, E-VP12, E-VP42, E-VSK12) are catalogue pages best read directly in the PDF when sizing a specific wall, floor or roof assembly; note their common structure: outer layer, insulation, one or two CLT layers, inner finish, with a vibration isolation pad or air gap wherever the layer stack crosses a module boundary.

Stairs (VP5, p. 41 to 42): five options; A, CLT stairs with CLT steps and load-bearing CLT handrails or gluelam beams; B, nail-plate structured stairs (steps on nail-plate-connected beams); C, CLT steps on gluelam supporting beams with 100 mm insulation and double gypsum board, REI30, total 226 mm; D, plywood steps on nail-plate-connected beams, same insulation and lining, REI30, total 198 mm; E, precast concrete stairs and beams, 110 mm concrete steps.

Roofs (YP1/YP2, p. 42 to 44): A, timber truss roof, ventilated, insulation 450 mm rock wool, CLT 80 mm ceiling, gypsum 18 mm, REI60, U 0.076; B, flat roof, bitumen sheet over plywood over rock wool with a ventilation channel, polyurethane board, moisture barrier, CLT 180 mm, battens, gypsum 13 mm. YP2 (over the corridor) uses a timber truss with an LVL bottom chord, sheathing board, 450 mm rock wool, vapour barrier, battens, 2 layers of 15 mm gypsum, REI60.

**5.2 Structural details** (pp. 51 to 85): 17 VD (vertical/module) details, 6 FD (foundation) details and 3 HD (horizontal, plan-view corner) details, each a labelled section drawing with an orientation thumbnail locating it on a building elevation, floor plan or 3D module diagram. See Figure index and Rules for modelling below for the geometry of each; in every VD sheet the joint is drawn both as a vertical section and, in a small circular inset, as a set of four labelled corner conditions (1 to 4) showing how the same detail turns a corner or a T-junction. A recurring note on nearly every sheet: "exact amount, size and spacing of fastener according to structural engineer" and "note that all final solutions need to be reviewed and approved by responsible designer" (the source never gives screw/nail counts or spacing).

### 6. Transportation and instructions for on-site assembly

**6.1 Transportation** (p. 87). Finished modules are wrapped in a plastic outer cover in the factory, then fastened to the trailer to prevent movement and damage in transit. Before transport the route must be checked for low bridges and tight corners; local traffic law may cap module width and height. No numeric transport envelope is given in this manual (contrast with producer-specific literature such as the Arkemi "How to CLT" handbook, which quotes 13.6 x 2.45 x 2.7/3.0 m semi/mega trailer limits and up to 4.15 x 4.5 x 30 m for whole 3D modules).

**6.2 Principles of erection** (p. 87). 6.2.1 General: an erection scheme, approved to local regulations, must specify lifting equipment (mobile or tower crane, slings, chains, eyebolts), and confirm site space and weather are adequate for the erection period before starting. Modules are lifted from 4, 6 or 8 lifting points depending on the element; lifting points are planned to balance the module while lifted, and large-opening modules may need temporary reinforcement before lifting to prevent damage. Working-at-height safety and platform live load limits apply throughout. Typical erection speed is 6 modular units per shift. 6.2.2 Installation: the erection surface (straightness, tolerances) is checked before starting; modules are unloaded in assembly order or installed directly from the truck; plastic covers are removed where not needed and a visual quality check made before installation; each module is lifted onto its spot using guiding steel parts to locate it correctly and keep it within installation tolerances (no numeric tolerance value is given); once position and tolerance are confirmed, the module is permanently fastened, with concealed steel-part fastening documented during installation; joints between adjacent modules are then sealed; once a full floor of modules is erected, remaining structural elements not part of the modules (beams, posts, CLT slabs) are installed; the whole floor gets a visual check and the structure is stabilised before starting the next floor.

**6.3 Protection on-site, moisture control** (p. 87). Moisture control runs through every project phase. A named person or team is assigned to monitor and control moisture. A moisture control plan estimates risk, sets a measuring/monitoring schedule, and a control scheme; site staff are trained in the plan's requirements, which also feed into estimates, quotes, contracts and site meetings. Any moisture damage is documented and the drying actions monitored with moisture measurements and recorded results.

**6.4 Protection of structures and material on-site** (p. 88 to 89). All modules stay protected during transport and after arrival, and are not stored in direct ground contact; every module is inspected on arrival and any damage documented and repaired immediately. The erection sequence is planned so modules can be protected right after erection, and all modules under one roof module should be erected in one continuous push (Fig. 1, p. 88). Walls: factory plastic coating is partly removed on site just before erection; joints on external walls are taped and a divider plank installed over the facade boarding at the joint; external walls not part of the final facade must stay protected until the adjacent module is installed. Roofs: temporary roof modules (covering one or several apartments) protect the top of the stack until the permanent roof is built; they carry their own lifting beam, are typically gang-nailed timber trusses with chipboard and a bitumen layer, and their joints are lapped with the neighbouring roof module's bitumen; roof module sides are protected with boards or plastic against rainwater ingress, and they can include drainage channels; unprotected cantilever modules need a tarpaulin or other cover. Indoor conditions: management of the indoor climate begins once load-bearing structures are erected and openings in the envelope are covered; the heating system is switched on in every space enclosed by load-bearing walls to dry the structure; two recording condition loggers (relative humidity and temperature) per building are recommended, read out weekly; target relative humidity is under 75 percent during assembly and 45 to 55 percent after thermal insulation is installed, with air temperature kept above +10 C for effective drying. Inspections before finishes (areas that are not prefabricated modules): check and chart moisture content and condition of all internal wood surfaces before coating; use the moisture data to judge coating readiness; damaged materials are replaced before internal finishing begins; measurements can be confirmed by drying/weighing tests or by measuring the relative humidity of the pore-air of the timber.

### 7. Sustainability (short)

Stora Enso frames sustainability around responsibly sourced wood (80 percent of wood at Stora Enso mills PEFC/FSC certified as of 2015, chain-of-custody tracked) and energy-efficient, low-carbon homes (EU buildings use approx. 40 percent of total EU energy; nZEB from 2021 under the EPBD; CLT structures can reach U values down to 0.1 W/m2K without moisture risk). 7.2 covers indoor climate and thermal comfort (airtight envelope, optimised surface temperatures, low-emission natural wood materials). 7.3 covers life cycle design: buildings are typically designed for a 50 to 100 year service life, with shorter-life components (fans, pumps, piping, coatings, facades, window frames) at 25 to 50 years, so a life-cycle approach plans for component replacement, maintenance, condition surveys and protecting load-bearing CLT on the inside of the insulation from outdoor climate impacts. 7.4 lists third-party certification schemes referenced by the market (BREEAM, LEED, DGNB, HQE, Miljobyggnad, Minergie) without endorsing a specific one. No modelling-relevant geometry in this chapter.

### 8. Stora Enso (company profile, short)

One page of company background: Stora Enso is described as a renewable-materials company (paper, packaging, biomaterials, wood products and wood construction), 2015 sales of EUR 10 billion, about 26,000 employees in more than 35 countries, listed in Helsinki and Stockholm. No modelling content.

## Rules for modelling

The source is a reference/design guide (a build-up and connection catalogue plus an erection procedure), not a single procedural recipe, so the following are rules to apply when generating a modular CLT building rather than one fixed sequence. Numbers are cited above; where the source gives no number, pick a project value and note the assumption.

1. Treat the building as a stack of factory-made 3D modules (room modules and technical modules) plus site-assembled additional elements (central corridor, stairs, elevator shaft, roof, balconies). Do not model load-bearing structure inside a technical module beyond its own CLT walls and slab.
2. Size each module by production, transport and lifting limits, not by a value from this document. The source gives no numeric envelope; if a concrete module footprint is needed, take it from the project brief or from a producer-specific source (e.g. semi/mega trailer 13.6 x 2.45 x 2.7 to 3.0 m, or a 3D module up to about 4.15 m wide, 4.5 m high, 30 m long, as quoted in the Arkemi "How to CLT" handbook).
3. Every module is a closed box of CLT wall panels and a CLT floor/roof slab, each fabricated as flat panels then assembled (4.2). Model wall and floor panels as flat CLT plates with a nominal single-layer thickness picked from the relevant build-up type (US1 120 to 140 mm, VSK 120 to 140 mm, VP floor 80 to 200 mm, YP roof 80 to 180 mm) rather than deriving thickness from a span calculation, which this source does not provide.
4. Where two modules meet side by side or stacked, insert a joint gap and a stack of separation layers rather than a direct panel-to-panel contact: typical stack is mineral wool 30 mm at the module perimeter, a separation strip for moisture and sound isolation, and a 12 mm vibration isolation pad between the panels that actually bear load. Two modules forming a single room (VD15) share one continuous gluelam beam the length of the module and an installation gap sealed with an EPDM rubber sealant on both sides.
5. Give every module-to-module vertical joint a tension connection (continuous tension rod through one or two shear walls, scarfed into a notch in the CLT, VD4) and a shear connection (either a notched wood-to-wood joint or a steel plate with screws, over the acoustic elastomer, VD5). Between stacked floors add a separate shear connection (VD6, steel plate in a notch) and, where needed, a tension/compression connection (VD7, same principle with a denser fastener pattern) as distinct joints, not combined into one connector.
6. Model the corridor, stairs and elevator shaft as site-built elements assembled after or alongside module erection, not as prefabricated modules: the central corridor is described as massive wood slabs, beams and stairways assembled on site, and the stair-to-floor connection (VD11) is an angle bracket into a gluelam beam carrying a CLT stair flight.
7. Anchor the ground floor of external and partition walls with a distinct uplift tie (tension rod plus anchor bolt into a steel part notched into the CLT, FD2/FD4) and a separate shear connection (steel angle plus anchor bolt, FD3/FD5), both sitting on a bitumen felt layer over the concrete with a 0 to 50 mm infill cast beneath the module for site-installation tolerance.
8. Give plan-view corners (salient corner, T-connection, re-entrant corner, HD1 to HD3) the same tension-rod-plus-EPDM-sealant logic as the straight wall joints; do not model a corner as a simple mitred panel intersection.
9. Sequence erection floor by floor: place the technical module first (it sits next to the corridor for short service runs), then its adjoining room module (whose floor slab bears on a support at the side of the technical module, so order matters structurally, not just logistically). Only fasten a module's connection hardware into the gap facing the side that will be installed next, since access is blocked once the neighbour is placed. Stabilise and visually check a full floor before starting the next one.
10. If the model needs to show construction-phase state, add the temporary protective layer: modules keep a partial plastic wrap, module-to-module facade joints get taped with a divider plank over the boarding, and the topmost storey gets a temporary roof module (gang-nailed truss, chipboard, bitumen) until the permanent roof is installed.
11. Do not model exact fastener counts, screw sizes or spacings from this source: every connection drawing defers "exact amount, size and spacing of fastener" to the structural engineer. Represent fasteners schematically (a plate, a rod, a bracket) rather than as an engineered pattern.
