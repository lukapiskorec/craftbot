# Introduction to timber frame construction: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/TRADA WIS 0-3 Introduction to timber frame construction 2016.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://turnertimber.co.uk/introduction.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** Introduction to timber frame construction, Wood Information Sheet WIS 0-3, subject "Timber frame", reviewed April 2016 with minor corrections. Prepared by Exova BM TRADA and published by the Timber Research and Development Association (TRADA), Chiltern House, Stocking Lane, High Wycombe. No ISBN, 8 pages.

**Scope of source:** A single, short overview sheet on UK platform timber frame construction. It covers the benefits of the method, a reading list of more detailed TRADA and British Standard publications, the platform frame erection method, external wall build-up and cladding options, internal and party walls, floors and roofs in outline, services routing, and preservative treatment. It states typical member sizes and spacings but does not contain span tables, load calculations, nailing schedules, U-value figures, or full working drawings. For those, the sheet points to other named publications (Timber frame construction 5th edition, Timber Frame: Standard details for houses and flats, Timber Frame Housing: UK Structural Recommendations, and others listed in the references).

**Most useful for:** orienting a modelling agent to UK platform-frame terminology, typical stud and joist sizes and spacings, and the layer order of an external wall or party floor build-up, before it builds a wall panel, floor platform, or party wall assembly. Not a source for span tables or connection design values.

## Title page and key points (PDF p. 1)

The sheet frames the whole document around platform frame, the method used for about 95 percent of UK timber frame buildings (stated later, p. 3). Key points on p. 1 summarise the rest of the sheet: open panels (studs, rails, lintels, sheathing, breather membrane) are factory made and craned onto a platform formed by the foundations and sole plate; installed floor decks form the next working platform; closed panels add insulation, services, vapour control layer, linings and sometimes joinery and cladding in the factory; the 2010 regulations pushed nominal wall depth from 140 mm to 190 mm by adding a service zone; wall panels are braced by nailed or stapled sheathing; moisture control through membranes is called out as critical. Fig. 1, p. 1: photo of an open panel timber frame structure mid erection, showing exposed studs, top and bottom rails and floor joists before sheathing and services.

## Benefits of timber frame construction (PDF p. 2)

Short paragraph each for designer, contractor and occupant. Designer: flexible planning, energy efficiency options, economic material use, wide range of external finishes, lighter overall dead weight than masonry which can permit lighter foundations. Contractor: reduced site work and faster completion from prefabrication, no wet trades so no masonry drying-out time, less remedial snagging. Occupant: high insulation reduces heating cost, dry construction lets decoration start soon after completion without cracking risk, easy to customise and extend. No numeric values in this section.

## Method of construction (PDF p. 2 to 3)

Platform frame is named as the method used for about 95 percent of UK timber frame buildings. Sequence described in prose: foundations and sole plate form the first platform; factory-made open panels (studs, rails, lintels, sheathing, breather membrane, sometimes pre-fitted rigid insulation in stud voids) are craned onto it and nailed into position; floor cassettes or loose joists (increasingly engineered joists) with a wood-based structural deck are installed to form the next platform; the process repeats storey by storey; the roof, normally trussed rafters, is positioned, fixed and braced last; open-panel structures are then dry-lined on site after services, insulation and vapour control layer are installed. Closed panels raise prefabrication further by adding insulation, services, vapour control layer, internal linings and sometimes joinery and cladding in the factory, but weather exposure of finished materials, construction speed and site erection detail need more care.

Load-bearing wall design should follow Eurocode 5. A boxed variation note gives the BS 5268 equivalents: BS 5268-6.1 for dwellings not exceeding 7 storeys, BS 5268-6.2 for buildings other than dwellings not exceeding 4 storeys.

Fig. 2, p. 3: photo of open panels being installed on site (Castleoak Group), panels standing on a floor platform mid erection.

Fig. 3, p. 3: "Externally sheathed timber frame wall with brick cladding", an isometric cutaway through an external wall and its foundation, the most detailed build-up drawing in the document. From outside to inside at wall level: brickwork cladding, ventilated cavity (open perpends above for drainage, spaced at max 1.5 m centres), breather membrane, sheathing, studs with thermal insulation between them, vapour control layer (vcl), internal wall lining. At the head: steel lintel with clips where required (or timber lintel), head binder, jack stud, top rail, cavity tray with open perpends above for drainage, breather membrane cut and tucked under the cavity tray by a minimum of 100 mm. At the sill and window: window bottom rail, flexible wall tie nailed to studs, stud marker tape. At the base: bottom rail, sole plate, dpm lapped with dpc and turned up to lap with vcl, inner leaf of brickwork or concrete block, finished ground level 150 mm below the lowest timber, vertical dpc lapped with vcl.

## External walls (PDF p. 4 to 5)

Framed wall panels carry all vertical and horizontal loads to the foundations. Brick or block cladding is supported directly off the foundations, not off the wall panels, and carries only its own weight; lighter claddings (timber boarding, tile hanging, render on mesh) are supported directly off the wall panels, so the structural engineer needs to know the cladding type.

Studs and rails form the vertical load-bearing skeleton, must be strength graded timber, normally preservative treated. Studs for buildings are most commonly 38 mm x 140 mm; larger sizes accommodate more insulation. The 2010 regulation change (25 percent heat loss reduction versus 2006) meant 140 mm was often not enough insulation depth, so the sheet recommends an insulated service zone nominally 50 mm deep on the inner face of the external wall, taking nominal wall depth from 140 mm to 190 mm. The service zone keeps the vapour and air control layer free of service penetrations; laps and junctions must be mechanically fixed and clamped behind battens to studs and rails. Studs are butt jointed and nailed to rails of the same section, normally spaced at 600 mm centres (400 mm centres or closer where needed), a spacing chosen to use 1200 mm x 2400 mm sheathing boards economically.

Sheathing (most commonly OSB, also plywood or bitumen-impregnated fibreboard) is nailed or stapled to the framing in a precise pattern justified by the structural engineer, and provides the racking stiffness against wind loads. Window and door openings are framed with additional cripple studs supporting loadbearing lintels. Panel size and prefabrication level decide manual versus crane lifting on site: single elevation panels if crane erected, smaller panels nailed together otherwise.

Sheathing is most often on the outside of the stud frame, in which case a vapour control layer is needed on the warm side of the insulation; if sheathing is fitted inside the studs instead, erection sequence and service installation need more care, and a service void formed with internal battens can be used. A plasterboard lining on the inside contributes to the wall's fire resistance. Insulation fills the stud cavities. The outer face (of the sheathing, or of the framing if sheathing is internal) is covered by a breather membrane, which protects the panel during construction and resists wind-driven rain; reflective breather membranes also improve thermal performance if installed correctly.

Fig. 4, p. 4: "Typical external wall construction", a two-storey elevation section through a party or gable-type wall showing, from top: horizontal cavity barrier, head binder, top rail of wall frame, lintel, trussed rafter ceiling tie, stud, cripple stud supporting lintel, sheathing, window bottom rail; then at the intermediate floor: bottom rail of wall frame, flooring, floor joist, header joist, top rail of wall frame (upper storey), lintel, head binder, cripple stud, horizontal cavity barrier with dpc over; at the base: bottom rail of wall frame, sole plate. This is the clearest single reference for member stacking order through a two-storey platform frame wall.

External cladding is frequently brick or blockwork tied to the timber frame with flexible stainless steel wall ties, which transfer wind load from masonry to the panels but must accommodate differential vertical movement from shrinkage, settlement and timber compression (this movement matters more with building height, and at projecting elements such as soffits, verges, sills and balconies). Lightweight claddings (clay tiles, slates, brick slips, timber boarding, cement render, proprietary render systems) are supported on preservative-treated softwood battens fixed to the studwork, forming a drained and vented cavity. Light combustible claddings are permitted subject to the unprotected area rule in building regulations; brick or blockwork gives adequate external fire resistance regardless of boundary distance. Brick or blockwork clad timber frame gives sound insulation similar to masonry cavity walls, though overall performance depends on openings in the wall.

Fig. 5, p. 5: "External wall with vertical board-on-board cladding": breather membrane, then treated horizontal battens fixed to studs at max 600 mm centres, top edge of each batten angle cut to a minimum of 15 degrees to throw water away from the sheathing face.

Fig. 6, p. 5: "External wall with proprietary render system on backing board": treated battens fixed to studs, dpc, board, fibreglass scrim, proprietary render, with a drained and vented cavity behind the render system.

## Internal walls (PDF p. 6)

Internal loadbearing and non-loadbearing walls use timber framing with plasterboard both sides. A loadbearing internal wall needs loadbearing lintels over openings on cripple studs, and must match the fire performance of the external walls and floors it supports. Where better acoustic performance is needed between rooms (bathrooms, bedrooms), mineral wool goes between the studs or the plasterboard lining specification is improved. No sizes given beyond this.

## Party walls (PDF p. 6)

Party walls are normally two separate timber framed walls with a cavity between. Insulation fills each frame; plasterboard in two layers with staggered joints lines each room side, for fire resistance and sound insulation. Any gap between the underside of roof tiling and the party wall must be fire stopped. Services and wiring in party walls must be detailed to maintain fire and acoustic performance where regulations permit them there at all. A further note: under England and Wales Approved Document L 2010, all cavities within a party wall must be filled with insulation to assume zero heat loss through the party wall, which changes sequencing and construction for sheathed timber party walls (referred back to Timber frame construction, 5th edition for detail).

Fig. 7, p. 6: "Party floor with solid timber joists", an isometric cutaway of a two-sided party floor: cavity barrier, seal, double joist (joists may be solid timber or a proprietary joist system), resilient bar, mineral wool, structural floor deck, plasterboard, gypsum based board, resilient batten, T&G chipboard, resilient flanking strip at the perimeter, load transfer blocking.

Fig. 8, p. 6: "Strip foundation with masonry cladding", a section through a party wall foundation: brickwork cladding set out from the sheathing face to ensure a correct 50 mm cavity width, wall tie, dpc, open perpends at max 1.5 m centres, internal lining, vcl, dpc, dpm lapped with dpc and vcl, batten, exterior ground level, concrete fill. Dimensions given: cavity width 50 mm, ground clearance 150 mm minimum (shown at two points), foundation depth variable but not less than 900 mm in shrinkable clay, foundation width variable but wide enough for bricklayers to work (no figure). A note on the figure says gaps in the construction are shown only to illustrate membrane laps, not actual construction gaps.

## Other structural elements (PDF p. 7)

Floors and roofs are similar to those in masonry construction. Ground floors are an insulated concrete slab or a suspended type (precast concrete or timber). Intermediate floors are prefabricated floor cassettes or site-built floors. Floor joists are normally at 400 mm or 600 mm centres, constant depth so wall panels stack to equal storey heights. In platform frame, floor joists sit directly on the wall panel's top rail or on a head binder. Head binders connect wall panels together and support floor joists that fall between stud positions; an additional header joist runs round the building perimeter to support upper wall panels, and at party walls to maintain fire and acoustic performance.

Trussed rafter roofs should comply with Eurocode 5 (BS 5268 Code of practice for trussed rafter roofs in the variation box). They fix directly to wall panels, or to head binders where trusses don't coincide with studs. Party floors combine a structural timber element with ancillary components for fire and acoustic performance, detailed in fig. 7 above.

## Services (PDF p. 7)

Services run in the cavity between wall panel studs. Wet services in an external wall should sit on the warm side of the insulation to avoid condensation, and the vapour control layer must be sealed around penetrations. An increasingly common approach uses a service cavity formed by battens between the vapour control layer and the plasterboard lining, so services don't penetrate the vapour control layer, improving airtightness; this zone can also be insulated to improve thermal performance. TRADA and BSRIA jointly publish Services in timber frame construction, Guidance for a defect-free interface [34] for best practice on this interface.

## Preservative treatment (PDF p. 7)

Need for treatment depends on timber durability and decay or insect attack risk. Members normally treated: sole plates, bottom members of wall frames resting on the dpc, external wall panel framing (optional or desirable under BS 8417 but usually done as insurance), timber cavity barriers in external cavity walls, timber in cold design flat roofs, timber cladding unless it is heartwood of Durability Class 2 (durable) or better, cladding battens, tiling battens. Housing warranty organisations set their own specific treatment requirements. Micro-emulsion based wood preservatives applied by low pressure methods have mostly replaced traditional light organic solvent formulations; sole plates are commonly treated with copper-containing preservatives, which fix in the wood and resist leaching, because of their exposed position and the difficulty of remedial work. Specification should follow BS 8417:2011 Preservation of wood, Code of practice.

## Rules for modelling

1. Frame external walls with studs 38 mm x 140 mm at 600 mm centres (400 mm or closer if the design needs it), butt jointed and nailed to rails of the same section.
2. If the model needs an insulated service zone (post-2010 thermal standard), add 50 mm to the inner face of the external wall, taking nominal depth from 140 mm to 190 mm.
3. Size sheathing panels at 1200 mm x 2400 mm; stud spacing of 600 mm is chosen to use these boards economically.
4. Build an externally sheathed wall, outside to inside, as: cladding (brick, block, or board on battens over a drained cavity) / ventilated cavity with open perpends at max 1.5 m centres / breather membrane / sheathing / studs with insulation between them / vapour control layer / internal lining (plasterboard).
5. At openings, frame with cripple studs carrying a lintel (timber or steel with clips where required); tuck the breather membrane under any head cavity tray by a minimum of 100 mm.
6. Keep the finished ground level at least 150 mm below the lowest external timber.
7. For vertical board-on-board timber cladding, space horizontal battens at max 600 mm centres and cut the top edge of each batten to at least 15 degrees to shed water away from the sheathing.
8. For a rendered finish, build up as battens on studs, backing board, fibreglass scrim, render, with a drained and vented cavity behind.
9. Space floor joists at 400 mm or 600 mm centres and keep joist depth constant across a storey so every wall panel on that platform is the same height; support joists on the wall's top rail, or on a head binder where they fall between studs.
10. Run a header joist around the building perimeter to support the upper wall panels and, at party walls, to preserve fire and acoustic separation.
11. Model party floors as double joists (solid or proprietary) with mineral wool between, a resilient bar or resilient battens breaking the sound path, a structural deck plus resilient layer, and gypsum or plasterboard linings; add a resilient flanking strip and load transfer blocking at the perimeter.
12. Model party walls as two independent stud frames with a cavity between, insulation inside each frame, and two staggered layers of plasterboard on each room face.
13. At a masonry-clad strip foundation, hold a 50 mm cavity between brickwork and the sheathing face, space open perpends at max 1.5 m centres, and take foundation depth to at least 900 mm where the ground is shrinkable clay (not stated for other ground conditions); leave foundation width wide enough for bricklayers to work, no fixed figure given.
14. For an erection sequence: platform (foundations plus sole plate) first, then crane and nail wall panels onto it, then install the floor deck to form the next platform, repeat per storey, then fix and brace the roof (normally trussed rafters) last, then dry-line on site once services, insulation and vapour control layer are in.
15. Treat with preservative: sole plates, bottom wall frame members on the dpc, external wall framing, cavity barriers, cold flat roof timber, non-durable cladding timber, cladding battens, tiling battens. Sole plates typically get a copper-containing preservative for leach resistance.
16. Where this sheet gives no number (nailing patterns, span tables, U-values, exact foundation width), do not invent one; open the named TRADA publication or referenced Wood Information Sheet instead, or write "not stated in the source".
