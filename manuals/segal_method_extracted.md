# The Segal method: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/THE SEGAL METHOD - color.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://jonbroome.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/AJ-Segal-Special-Issue-The-Segal-Method-05-Nov-1986-reduced.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** The Architects' Journal, volume 183, number 45, "Special issue: the Segal method", 5 November 1986, printed pages 31 to 68. Written by Jon Broome, self-builder and joint architect with Walter Segal on the Lewisham self-build housing schemes. No ISBN, this is a magazine special issue. The PDF scan runs to 17 pages (front cover, 14 content spreads, one closing spread, and 2 blank pages at the back).

**Scope of source:** A magazine feature describing the principles and essential features of Walter Segal's self build timber frame method, following the self build sequence from planning through to finishes: modular tartan grid planning, the post and beam structural frame, foundations, roof, floors, external walls, windows, partitions, ceilings, stairs, and services, plus the documentation set an architect prepares (layout drawings, calculations, framing drawings, schedule of materials, catalogue of elements, building instructions). It is a case study and manifesto piece aimed at architects, not a construction manual or design code. It does not contain span tables, nailing schedules, a structural calculation method, timber grading rules, or a complete set of working drawings. Numbers given are typical or example values drawn mostly from the Lewisham self build schemes and the author's own practice, not standardised specifications.

**Most useful for:** establishing the geometry, sequence and modular logic of a Segal method timber frame house, tartan grid planning, a post and beam frame with a floor level tie beam, a loose laid flat roof build up, and clamped infill wall and partition panels, for a CraftBot experiment modelling this kind of self build structure.

## Walter Segal's approach (pp. 3 to 4)

In 1962 Walter Segal built a temporary house in his Highgate garden for his family while the main house was rebuilt. It was completed in two weeks for a materials cost of 800 pounds, about 6500 pounds at 1986 prices, and it still stood in 1986. Private clients then commissioned about two dozen private houses in England and Ireland over the next 15 years. A students' residence was designed and built by students in Stuttgart in 1983. A house was built by this method in Western Australia in 1980, arranged after two long telephone calls. Other architects, notably David Lea (vicarage at Melling near Liverpool, housing at Churt, Surrey) applied Segal's ideas.

The method uses widely available building materials put together in an open ended way, giving the speed and economy of open system building without the monotony and inflexibility of a closed system. Segal rethought common technical problems from first principles, such as flat roof failures and condensation, avoiding the defects that afflict much system built housing.

The houses are not monumental or massive. They show concern for comfort and utility, respond to their occupants, and can be easily altered, an architecture of liberation. Not to be confused with prefab: quality is harmony of form, fitness and purpose (after Christopher Alexander), together with being ordinary and unremarkable. The method is essentially an updated version of medieval timber framing using basic industrial products and commonly understood skills.

Simplification lets non experts build a house and non architects control the design. In 1971 a school teacher near Woodbridge, Suffolk, built the first self build Segal house after watching the carpenter and deciding he could do it himself. In 1978, 14 families from Lewisham council's housing waiting and transfer lists built their own houses in south east London. A second Lewisham scheme was nearing completion in 1986. No one was excluded by circumstances, houses were built by people in their 60s and by a single mother. Each family builds its own house individually from start to finish, setting its own programme, which avoids the pressure of communal rota schemes and lets parents and children take part. Self builders do nearly all operations themselves, with no subcontractors, saving money and avoiding coordination problems. Houses need not be identical, and the design can be amended during construction, for example moving a window to catch a view. Houses can be extended: one family added a bedroom over three weekends for 1200 pounds. Progressive development can cut initial cost and extend useful life beyond the 60 year design life.

Essential simplicity does not mean the highest conventional performance, for example on sound insulation, but the houses are detached so there is no neighbour noise anyway, typical of Segal's lateral thinking. Thermal insulation standards improved over the method's development. Segal experimented with new materials and techniques only when based on knowledge or experience, never using untried materials of unproven performance. The method developed from temporary to permanent houses, and from one off contractor built designs to self built houses with a high degree of user control.

## The essential method (p. 5)

An attitude of mind rather than a system of construction, this is rigorous simplification of the whole building process from design to completion.

- The process is rethought from first principles to handle fundamentals such as tolerances and thermal and moisture movement.
- Elements are reduced to their essentials for maximum economy of material and effort.
- The method uses readily available materials and techniques rather than special manufacture, so it benefits from market competition on price and availability, and can absorb improvements in techniques and materials.
- Standard materials are assembled on site in their market sizes, minimising on site production and waste.
- Building boards, plasterboard and woodwool slabs, form the enclosure within a framework of standard timber sections. The approach could also apply to steel for frame and infill.
- Materials are chosen for performance and cost, workable with simple hand power tools, and of an easily handled size and weight.
- The building is organised on a modular grid determined by the sizes of standard materials.
- Components are assembled with dry joints only, bolts and screws, so wet trades such as bricklaying and plastering are eliminated.
- Foundations and groundworks are reduced to a minimum.
- The whole process is simplified so it can be carried out by one person with basic carpentry skills, with the possible exception of services and roofing.
- Construction is quick and economical.
- Documentation is simple: small freehand A4 diagrams of layout and structure, standard junction details, a schedule of materials, and a set of instructions.
- The schedule of materials combines the functions of a bill of quantities, a specification and an order list, giving accurate ordering and cost control from the outset.
- The architect's role includes those of quantity surveyor and, ideally, engineer.
- The simplicity of construction and documentation makes the process transparent, understandable and controllable by someone without experience.
- The buildings are very adaptable and easy to extend.
- The method can produce one and two storey buildings with flat or pitched roofs, courtyards, split levels and double height spaces.

## The planning process

### 1 General arrangement (p. 6)

Fig 18, p. 6: a cutaway isometric of a two storey Segal building locating every numbered element in place: foundations (10) as discrete pads below posts, structural frame (11) of posts and beams, roof (12), floors (13), external walls (14), windows (15), partitions (16), ceilings (17), stairs (18), services (19), the modular grid (2), and structural layout (4). The order these are described in the article, and here, is generally the sequence in which they are carried out on site.

### 2 Modular grid (p. 6)

Planning starts with the basic layout. Both the structural frame and the infill walls and partitions are planned on a modular tartan grid. Its dimensions come from the width of the building panels, commonly 600 mm or 1200 mm, and from the structural thickness, commonly 50 mm. A tartan grid lets corners be formed and columns, generally 50 mm thick, be placed at any grid position without cutting panels. The trade off is that a 50 mm gap is left at every joint position along a straight run of wall, needing more jointing material, but this is accepted because grid planning with uncut standard panels outweighs the disadvantage (fig 19).

As columns are deeper than 50 mm, they stand partly outside the grid. These projections are accommodated by placing columns outside the building where possible, or projecting from partitions, never set longways within the grid since that would mean cutting panels.

A client can plan the layout on squared paper together with the architect, giving a high degree of design control. Fig 20, p. 6: a self builder's first floor plan drawn on graph paper, showing bedrooms, bathroom, stair and bracing position. Walls carry no load, so they can be placed in any position on either floor, and can be changed at any stage of construction (fig 21). Vertical dimensions are controlled the same way, by infill panel height and the structural depth of the frame. Fig 22, p. 6: section showing a ceiling panel fitting between joists without cutting, at about 200 mm structural depth with the 50/600/50 tartan spacing.

### 3 Layout drawings (p. 7)

Once the layout is decided, two or three simple A4 freehand diagrams are prepared, showing the position of walls and openings in plan and elevation, with superfluous information left out (figs 23 to 24, a Lewisham self build example). Layout drawings are used together with the calculations, framing drawings, schedule of materials, catalogue of elements and building instructions. In practice, layout drawings are referred to only occasionally once the frame is up and the modular arrangement is legible in the building itself.

### 4 Structural layout (p. 7)

The structure is a post and beam frame (fig 25a), not stud wall panel construction (fig 25b). A frame is less efficient in its use of timber than a panel structure, but has other advantages: the extent and cost of foundations is considerably less, and a frame gives great planning freedom and adaptability.

The design order (figs 26a to c) is plan first: the building's partition layout is decided, then the frame is designed to suit the plan, and column layout follows partition layout, with columns sitting partially outside the tartan grid where needed. Columns can be spaced up to six modules apart in either direction, which gives an optimum span before beam deflection becomes critical. This gives a structural bay of up to 3.85 m in either direction, which accommodates most domestic size rooms. Composite beams or trusses can be used for larger spans.

### 5 Calculations (p. 7)

Once the structural layout is fixed, the structure is calculated to check it is adequate and to find the most economical form, consistent with the plan and the constructional discipline. Particular attention is given to the design of the joints, often the most critical part of a structure of this kind. A set of calculations for Building Regulations approval typically includes tabulation of dead and imposed loads, calculation of wind loads, bending and deflection of critical beams and joists at floor and roof level, calculation of critical columns for axial and bending loads, calculation of joints, wind overturning effects, and soil bearing pressures. Such a set of calculations might run to about a dozen pages, for example the floor joist bending calculation shown in fig 27.

### 6 Framing drawings (p. 7)

Once the structure has been checked by calculation and amended as necessary, the frame drawings are prepared. These are again A4 freehand diagrams, such as the typical ground floor framing plan and sections shown in figs 28 and 29. Framing drawings show the dimensional information needed to construct the frame. A typical set would include a foundation layout, plans at roof levels, sections, and details of joints, about eight sheets altogether.

### 7 Schedule of materials (p. 8)

Once the drawings are complete, the schedule of materials is prepared: a detailed list of the materials needed for a particular building. Each component is described with its size, length and finish, together with its location in the building, with diagrammatic annotations where necessary (fig 30). The schedule combines a traditional bill of quantities and specification, prepared by the architect, describing each piece of material accurately. It is also used as a list for ordering material and for checking deliveries, and it gives a very accurate cost at the outset that will not vary significantly on completion. Typically the schedule for a house runs to about 10 pages.

### 8 Catalogue of elements (p. 8)

This is a standard document used for each building: a series of simple A4 diagrams showing standard details of each element of the construction and how they go together to form the basic shell of the building. Fig 31, p. 8, shows how typical wall junctions are formed. Using the typical junction arrangements given, buildings of many shapes and sizes can be formed.

### 9 Building instructions (p. 8)

These are the final elements of the documentation: a simple step by step description of the building process, annotated with diagrams as necessary. Fig 32, p. 8, describes construction of the main cross frames, headed "framing, be methodical, be careful," and lays out four steps.

1. Mark out all posts and beams. Use running dimensions to improve accuracy: start at the top of the post (for example at roof level), add each dimension from the drawing cumulatively down the post rather than measuring each length separately, and check the actual depth of the planed beam as delivered against the drawing. The example marks a typical two storey post with a ground floor level, a first floor level about 2325 mm above it, and a roof level a further run above that, with the exact running totals varying by project.
2. Mark out all joints, if necessary. Dimensions on the drawing are to the centreline of the joint. Add 25 mm to the length of each beam to form the joint.
3. Mark out notches at the top of posts for the end frames. The notch is 50 mm from the face of the post regardless of the actual thickness of the beam, which will be about 45 mm.
4. Cut beams and posts to length, cut joints and notches, treat all cut ends with preservative, and stack the posts and beams for each frame together.

Self builders without building experience attend a short course of about 12 evening sessions, explaining the method and its documentation with slides, models and drawings, plus workshop sessions building confidence with hand and power tools, making typical joints, and learning basic plumbing and wiring. Once self builders are on site they learn readily by example, devise their own ways of doing things and pass on information to one another. At Lewisham, regular meetings monitor progress. The architect is available by phone in the evening and visits the site regularly at weekends. Training and supervision matter, but it is vital that people approach the job in the right frame of mind: careful, methodical, thinking operations through and planning ahead.

## The building process

### 10 Foundations (pp. 8 to 9)

At last, after planning, finding land, obtaining consents, arranging finance, preparing documents and obtaining materials, the moment to break ground arrives. The essential feature of the foundation arrangement is that the building stands above the ground on posts, rather than being built in the ground in the conventional way.

Foundations generally consist of separate in situ concrete bases below each post position. A hole is dug, and once the building inspector approves the bottom, it is filled with concrete and capped with a concrete paving slab set at a slight fall. The posts stand on the slabs on a piece of lead, forced into the end grain of the timber by the weight of the building, sealing the post against moisture. Fig 33, p. 8, gives a pad about 600 mm square, or to suit the bearing pressure of the soil, and 900 mm deep, or to suit local ground conditions.

The topsoil below the building is removed and replaced with gravel, contained within a border of paving slabs. A concrete oversite slab is not required. Concrete quantities are small, so digging is by hand and placing is by wheelbarrow (fig 34), avoiding the cost of ready mixed concrete and machine excavation, and making the groundworks a manageable self build operation. Foundations are built at whatever ground level exists, without levelling the site, so the method works on sloping sites without a significant cost increase, and nearby trees are not damaged.

A typical three bedroom bungalow might have about 18 such concrete bases, a two storey house about 12. Isolated pads of this kind are less extensive and therefore less expensive than the continuous foundation needed under a loadbearing wall.

Levels are taken and each post is cut to length so that when the frames are erected the floors come out precisely level. An undercroft below the building makes a useful storage cellar (fig 35). The posts stand on the foundations but are not anchored to them, the weight of the building keeps them in place. This means that once the frames are erected, post positions can be adjusted for distance and square independently of the foundations (fig 36: check modular dimensions between frames, check diagonals for square, with lead between posts and foundations). Accurate setting out of the foundations is therefore unnecessary, removing an operation that inexperienced builders find difficult. Short bored piles are an economical alternative, since ground beams are not needed, and are quick and suit poor soils.

### 11 Structural frame (pp. 9 to 10)

Like medieval timber framing, the structure is a number of cross frames with rigid joints, assembled flat on the ground, raised into position and braced together (fig 37, a frame assembled flat on the oversite deck).

Erection sequence, figs 38 to 41, p. 9: frames are assembled flat and stacked in order of erection. The first frame is erected and securely braced in position with struts into the ground. The remaining frames are raised one at a time and temporarily braced to the first with timbers that will be used later on in construction, until the permanent bracing is fixed in position. The temporary braces can then be removed one at a time to adjust the frames for square and position.

Framing uses slender sections of stress graded, imported softwood, pressure treated against rot. Rigid joints are formed with galvanised steel bolts, in place of the medieval mortice and tenon joints held together with wooden pegs. The form and proportion of the frame have more in common with steel than with traditional timber framing, and the slender sections let the frames be handled by hand.

Frames are a form of portal frame with rigid joints that resist horizontal wind forces in the plane of the frame (fig 44, p. 10). Unlike a conventional portal, there is a tie beam at floor level which eliminates the horizontal reactions at the base of the column. This is what allows the building to stand above the ground without being anchored to it, with the attendant advantages of ease of construction and elimination of critical tolerances in the foundations. Frames are rigid in one direction, but have to be braced together in the other to resist wind. The floor is fixed so it forms a rigid horizontal plate that transfers horizontal forces to the position of the bracing (fig 45). The method of construction could provide for the roof to act as an effective plate too. Bracing is generally a cross brace at each floor level, extending for one bay at a convenient position near the centre of the building (figs 41, 42, 45). The lower wind forces in single storey houses allow the use of knee braces below floor level (fig 43), avoiding the planning limitation that a bay of fixed bracing above floor level imposes. The size of the bolts at the joints is given by the load on the joint and the type of timber, and bolt sizes in turn dictate the minimum spacing between bolts and between the bolts and the edge of the timber (fig 46).

Frames in the centre of the building are constructed with double beams, whereas single beams are used at the edge of the building, so all the beams carry a similar load (fig 49). Once the frames are erected and braced, joists are fixed between them at roof and floor levels, their spacing and thickness conforming to the modular grid (fig 47). Equally important, the beams and joists have the same depth, so that when the joists are fixed using a bearer on the side of the beams, the undersides of both beams and joists are at the same level (figs 50 to 51). As there are no beams projecting below ceiling level, all the wall panels are the same height. Having beams and joists of the same depth is possible because the timber used for the heavily loaded beams is much stronger than that used for the joists.

Timbers used later in the construction are fixed temporarily to the posts to provide a safe working platform for fixing the joists at the upper levels, so scaffolding is not necessary (fig 48). The frame can be designed with projecting cantilevers (fig 52), in which case deflection becomes the critical factor.

### 12 Roof (p. 11)

Once the frame is completed, constructing the roof is the next operation, which allows all subsequent operations to be carried out under cover (fig 54). Many forms of roof are possible, but a flat roof has particular advantages: it is quick and easy to construct, economical, and fits over any plan shape without difficulty. A flat roofed building can be extended in any direction without problems.

Segal rethought flat roof construction from first principles to avoid the problems that commonly lead to failure. The waterproof membrane is not fixed down but is laid loose on top of the roof deck, draped over the building in the manner of a tablecloth (fig 55). This means structural movement of the deck or timber frame is not transferred to the membrane, avoiding cracks and leaks, and the membrane is free to expand and contract without wrinkles or edge tears. Many large flat roofs are now designed on this loose lay principle.

The roof has a generous overhang all round, which affords protection and creates almost a microclimate of shelter around the building (fig 53). The roof outlet is the only penetration and is positioned in the overhang so the joint is fail safe. The roof deck comprises woodwool slabs laid loose on the joists, dimensioned to avoid cutting the slabs, and laid level over the joists without any falls. With a perimeter upstand the roof is wet at all times, which keeps the membrane at a relatively even temperature.

The membrane is commonly a conventional three layer build up of bituminous felt, for economy. Layers are bonded one to another with hot bitumen, but not to the roof deck. This is generally a job for a professional roofer, although cold bitumen or a single layer butyl rubber membrane could make it a fully self build operation, eliminating all subcontractors. The membrane is weighted down with a 40 mm layer of 20 mm diameter shingle, which also keeps direct sunlight off the felt (fig 56). The felt is carried up and over an upstand at the edge of the roof and turned down over the top of the fascia. If the roof outlet should get blocked, water simply flows over the upstand without harm, indicating the blockage. The edge of the membrane is wedged in position with a timber capping section fixed through spacer blocks below the edge of the felt, so no fixing goes through the felt itself, allowing it to expand and contract freely (fig 57).

The roof is commonly insulated at ceiling level, and the void above the ceiling is ventilated by virtue of the loose construction, avoiding a build up of interstitial condensation. A roof of this kind is also very easy to insulate on the outside, as an upside down roof.

### 13 Floors (p. 11)

Once the roof is complete, floors are fixed. These are generally conventional tongued and grooved softwood boarding on joists (fig 58). Since walls are not yet in place at this stage, chipboard flooring would be vulnerable to weather. The ground floor is raised above the ground and therefore has to be insulated against extra heat losses. The raised floor gives an undercroft for storage the whole extent of the house, and it is also easy to run services within this space. Insulation is laid between joists on panels which can be removed for access to services (fig 59).

### 14 External walls (p. 12)

Once floors are fixed they provide a working platform to build the walls from. External walls are non structural infill clamped into the structural frame, the clamping done with bolts, the bolt heads visible outside (fig 60).

Walls are formed by combining layers of readily available building boards. The filling is an insulating and structural core, with a weatherproof external finish and a decorative internal finish. Segal likened the walls to clothes, a comfortable vest on the inside, a jumper for warmth, and a raincoat to keep dry on the outside (fig 61). Just as clothes fit loosely so that sweat does not build up, this wall construction relies on a loose fit between the layers to let air circulate and prevent condensation building up. The approach is safe because it does not rely on a vapour barrier, whose integrity cannot be guaranteed, though it does lead to higher ventilation heat losses.

The materials are selected to give the performance required for a particular situation. Commonly the materials are fully compressed fibre cement panels with an enamel finish on the outside, painted plasterboard on the inside, with a core of 50 mm woodwool slabs between. Extra layers can be added, for example a layer of urethane foam for thermal performance, or a layer of plastic laminate on the inside for a washable splashback around a bath.

Panels are clamped together with timber battens, bolted together with three spun galvanised steel bolts. The battens engage on a sole plate at the bottom and the structural joist at the top (fig 62). Blocks fixed top and bottom establish the position of the tartan grid (fig 63). The joint is not sealed (fig 65): a weep hole is formed at the bottom of the joint to drain it and to allow ventilation of the wall. Because the wall is formed of elements simply lapping over one another, conventional tolerances are not relevant, and the degree of tolerance provided is much greater, which makes a wall of this kind easy to construct, as the plan view of a junction illustrates (fig 64: structural column 50, weatherproof external finish, insulation and structural core, decorative internal finish, corners formed with battens screwed into blocks, infill panels clamped into the frame with battens bolted together, on the 50/600/50 tartan dimensions).

Components that make up the infill wall, partitions and ceilings are finished as you go along, plasterboards painted and external battens coated with preservative wood stain. Decorating is one of the early operations in the process instead of one of the last, as is usual.

### 15 Windows (pp. 12 to 13)

Once the external wall panels are in position the building can be made weathertight by fixing the windows (fig 66). Segal's approach was to assemble easily available materials on site to make a window, rather than incorporating a factory made component. This avoids problems of tolerance and modular coordination, dependence on a single source of supply, and delivery delays, and it also reduces costs by eliminating transport and factory overheads.

Windows are, in effect, sheets of glass with polished edges that slide horizontally in a track made of two aluminium angles, held in position with timber beads, all within a timber window lining (fig 69). One angle is fixed through a spacer washer, a tap washer, to provide a drainage slot to the track. The angles provide both a sliding surface for the glass and a spacer between the fixed and sliding sheets of glass, giving the clearance needed for the panes to slide past one another. The glass is lubricated by running a wax candle along the edge of the pane before fixing. Rainwater falling on the sliding pane runs behind the angle and out through the drainage slot, and dry glazing tape seats the fixed pane.

Such a window is adaptable and can be made in a great variety of fixed and opening panes up to about one metre square, for example storey height glazing (fig 67), horizontal strip glazing (fig 70), and varied shapes at the vicarage at Melling by David Lea (fig 68). Double windows can be formed by constructing a second set inside the first. The chief disadvantage of a window of this kind is that it is not airtight. A clear plastic channel pushed onto the edge of one pane closes the 1 mm gap between panes, and a foam strip seals the part of the drainage slot not needed below the fixed pane. Conventional windows can also be incorporated where preferred.

### 16 Partitions (p. 13)

Once the building is weathertight the internal subdivisions can be constructed (fig 71). Partitions are made by clamping panels into the frame with timber battens, in a similar way to the external walls. In this case there is a structural core with an internal finish on either side, commonly a 50 mm thick woodwool slab with painted plasterboard on each side. Extra layers can be added as before, for example to provide a pinboard (fig 72).

Partition panels engage on a sole plate at the bottom and the structural joist at the top, as before, with blocks fixed top and bottom establishing the tartan grid. Where the partition runs perpendicular to the joists, a filler piece is needed at its top. Corners and junctions are formed using timber battens as for the external wall. Because the wall is made of overlapping elements clamped together, the boards forming the finish can be offset off the modular grid at the corners as necessary to accommodate their thickness, avoiding cut panels (fig 73). Door linings are made on site and incorporated into the wall. Fixings for shelving and fittings are available at every batten position.

### 17 Ceilings (p. 14)

Once the internal partitions are in place, ceilings can be fixed (fig 74). They are commonly painted plasterboard laid between the joists on battens. Generally the boards are 600 mm wide and simply need trimming in length to fit. Some boards only come 1200 mm wide, in which case they are cut in half on site. Rooms six or more modules wide need two boards end to end with a cover strip to mask the joint. Other materials and finishes are possible. The ceiling below the roof can include extra insulation, and the boards can be lifted out to get access to the services.

The ceiling below a first floor is designed to achieve a modified half hour fire resistance, using sacrificial timber linings fixed to the joists (fig 75). These linings burn in the event of a fire, leaving the structural joists unaffected. This technique, which satisfies the Building Regulations, relies on the fact that timber burns at a predictable rate, so that a particular thickness of timber will take a particular length of time to burn through.

### 18 Stairs and other features (p. 14)

Stairs and steps are designed to avoid complicated setting out, angle cutting and jointing. Treads are supported on hangers (fig 76), posts (fig 77), or cantilever beams (fig 78), with timber members lapping over one another at right angles. Externally, hardwood, generally keruing, is used for treads and walkways because it resists decay. The basic structure can be adapted to form many different features, a pergola, porch, seat (fig 80), bay window (fig 79), trellis, veranda (fig 81), walkway or balustrade, giving great variety of form, colour and detail within the modular discipline.

### 19 Services (p. 14)

The construction incorporates voids in roofs, floors and walls to accommodate services, all accessible by unscrewing a batten or lifting out a panel (fig 83). Lighting wiring is run in the ceiling or roof void, with wiring to light switches dropping down in the spaces between wall panels at joints. Ring main wiring to power outlets is run in the floor void and rises to socket outlets at batten positions in the wall. Light switches and socket outlets can be positioned on any batten by cutting a cabling hole in it (fig 82). Pipework for heating, gas, and hot and cold water is run in the floor void. Waste pipes from sinks and other fittings can be suspended in the space below the ground floor.

## The future (p. 15)

The Segal method has a number of potentially far reaching implications. The role of tradesmen and professionals in the building industry would be altered by this rigorous simplification of the whole building process. Attitudes towards buildings would change if they were genuinely adaptable and flexible, no longer fixed edifices designed for a particular purpose but rather tools to be put to different uses. Attitudes towards maintenance would alter, given the ease of changing worn out parts. Methods of financing building would change if people could build a small, basic initial house capable of easy upgrading and extension. Residents of both public and private developments could enjoy a far greater degree of choice about their accommodation if houses were built using this kit of parts approach. Far more people would be able to undertake the construction of their own house using these techniques, with a high degree of control, variety and satisfaction.

While building a house is probably something only a minority will want or be able to do, there is nevertheless a hidden demand that would be satisfied if the opportunities existed and people thought it a real possibility. Stockholm City Council, for example, has run departments carrying out self build developments in and around the city since the 1920s, with a waiting list of 10,000 families in 1986.

An important idea is that of choice. The Segal method offers an alternative to subsidised mass housing on the one hand and anonymous mass produced private housing on the other. Making opportunities of this kind available to unemployed people would let them create something useful for themselves and their community, gaining self confidence that could help them find employment. More generally, one could look forward to land and finance going into small scale, self regulating communities of self built houses on vacant sites in major towns across the country.

Walter Segal became, as far as the author knew, the only living architect to get his name into the London A to Z road atlas, with Segal Close, SE23 (figs 84 to 85). Walter's Way was due to be listed the following year.

## Rules for modelling

This is a design guide, not a step by step build script, but the sequence it describes (planning process, then building process) works well as a procedural build order.

1. Plan on the tartan grid: 600 mm or 1200 mm panel modules with 50 mm structural bands. Decide the partition layout first, then place columns to suit, generally at up to 6 modules apart in either direction, giving a max structural bay of 3.85 m. Keep columns outside the building envelope or projecting from partitions rather than longways within the grid.
2. Foundations: one discrete concrete pad per post, about 600 mm square and 900 mm deep or to suit ground conditions, capped with a paving slab at a slight fall. No site levelling, no continuous footings, no oversite slab beyond a gravel bed within a paving slab border.
3. Cut each post to the length needed for that pad's level, so floors come out level once erected. Posts stand on the pads, unanchored.
4. Assemble each cross frame flat (posts, beams, and a floor level tie beam, joined with bolted rigid joints), then raise it, plumb it and brace it to the previous frame. Fix joists between frames at the same depth as the beams, using a bearer on the side of the beam, so beam and joist undersides align. Give centre frames double beams and edge frames single beams. Put one braced bay per floor level near the centre of the plan (or knee braces below floor level for a single storey building).
5. Build the roof immediately after the frame: woodwool deck laid loose without falls, a three layer felt membrane loose laid over it (not fixed to the deck or at the edges), a perimeter upstand with a two piece timber capping, and a 40 mm layer of shingle ballast. One roof outlet, in the overhang.
6. Fix floors: tongued and grooved softwood boarding on joists, with liftable insulation panels between joists.
7. Fix external walls once floors are down: non structural sandwich panels (weatherproof outer layer, 50 mm woodwool core, plasterboard inner layer) clamped by bolted battens to a sole plate below and the structural joist above, positioned by blocks at the tartan grid. Leave joints unsealed with a weep hole at the bottom of each.
8. Fix windows once walls are up: site made horizontal sliding glass in aluminium angle tracks within a timber lining, panes up to about 1 m² with a 1 mm clearance between fixed and sliding panes.
9. Build partitions once the building is weathertight: the same clamped panel construction as external walls, but with a structural core finished on both sides (commonly woodwool and plasterboard both sides), placeable at any grid position.
10. Fix ceilings once partitions are in: 600 mm wide plasterboard between joists on battens, with sacrificial timber fire linings below a first floor for fire resistance.
11. Add stairs and other features last among the shell elements: timber members lapping over one another at right angles, on hangers, posts or cantilever beams, with keruing hardwood outside.
12. Run services after the shell is complete: lighting in the roof void, switch drops in the wall joint voids, ring main and pipework in the floor void, waste pipes suspended below the ground floor, all accessible by unscrewing a batten or lifting a panel.
