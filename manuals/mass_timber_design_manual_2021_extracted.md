# Mass Timber Design Manual 2021: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Mass Timber Design Manual 2021.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://info.thinkwood.com/download/mass-timber-design-manual and save it under that name.

**Source:** *Mass Timber Design Manual*, U.S. Edition, WoodWorks (Wood Products Council) and Think Wood, funded by the Softwood Lumber Board, 2021, no ISBN listed, 98 pages as paginated in the PDF.

**Scope of source:** A marketing and awareness compilation, not a structural design handbook. It introduces the five common mass timber products (CLT, DLT, NLT, glulam, and MPP mentioned only once in passing), summarizes 2021 IBC changes for tall mass timber construction, and presents about 20 built case studies with brief project data (location, team, size, construction type, occasionally a structural grid or member size). Most technical chapters are one or two paragraphs that summarize a separate WoodWorks or Think Wood technical paper and end with a "Learn More" link to that paper, which is not included in this PDF. The manual does not contain span tables, connection design values, nailing schedules, or construction sequence drawings of its own. It does contain a few real numeric tables: acoustic ratings of tested panel assemblies, mass timber connection classes, and IBC fire resistance ratings by construction type. Case study write ups occasionally give a structural grid dimension or panel thickness, which is the main source of geometric data in this document.

**Most useful for:** orienting a modelling agent to the five mass timber product categories and their rough proportions, to typical structural grid dimensions used in built mass timber office and residential buildings (25x25 ft to 30x30 ft), and to the IBC Type IV-A/B/C construction types that govern how much mass timber can be left exposed. Not useful for deriving member sizes from loads, connection details, or fabrication drawings.

## Chapter notes

### Foreword (p.5 to 7)

Two personal essays from WoodWorks board members on the case for mass timber (climate, schedule, occupant wellbeing, fire safety). No technical content.

### Introduction (p.8 to 9)

Defines mass timber as a category of engineered wood products made of multiple solid wood laminations fastened or glued together, positioned as a strong, low carbon alternative to concrete and steel, well suited to prefabrication and tight sites. States over 1000 multifamily, commercial, or institutional mass timber or heavy timber projects had been built or were in design in the US since 2013 (per WoodWorks). "How to use this manual" section explains the manual bundles WoodWorks technical papers, Think Wood continuing education articles, case studies, expert Q+As and technical guides, each accessible via "Learn More" links to a master resource folder, and states the book is updated annually. "Looking for support" section points to help@woodworks.org and the WoodWorks Innovation Network (WIN) for project support.

### Mass Timber Products (p.10 to 29)

Products overview (p.13): mass timber panels are large solid wood panels usable for load bearing walls, floors and roofs; can be curved, cantilevered, and combined with concrete toppings to form timber concrete composite (TCC) panels that reduce cross sections, increase spans, and reduce noise and vibration transfer; can be combined with light frame wood or hybrid structures.

Cross laminated timber, CLT (p.16, isometric diagram of a 3 layer stacked panel): layered lumber boards, usually 3, 5, or 7 layers, stacked and glued crosswise at 90 degrees for bidirectional rigidity. Board thickness 5/8 in to 2 in, board width most commonly 2.5 to 5.5 in. Panels can be manufactured to custom dimensions but transportation restrictions limit overall size. Applications: floors, walls, roofing, cantilevered floors and balconies, load bearing elevator shafts, stairs. Exposed CLT is allowed up to 8 stories under the 2021 IBC.

Dowel laminated timber, DLT (p.17, isometric diagram showing boards on end with visible dowel pins): softwood lumber boards (2x4, 2x6, 2x8, etc) stacked on end and friction fit together with dowels, typically hardwood. All timber design with no metal connectors allows CNC processing. Can be topped with concrete to form TCC panels. Used for walls, floors, roofs, stairs, elevator shafts, and curved bent structures.

Nail laminated timber, NLT (p.18, isometric diagram showing edge stacked boards with visible fasteners): dimensional lumber stacked on edge and fastened with nails or screws. Board thickness nominal 2x, 3x, 4x; width typically 4 to 12 in. Adding plywood or OSB sheathing to one face gives load bearing / shear wall / diaphragm capacity. A century old technology, revived because it needs no dedicated manufacturing facility, only readily available dimensional lumber.

Glued laminated timber, glulam (p.19, isometric diagram of laminated straight member with fanned laminations): individual wood laminations bonded with durable, moisture resistant adhesive, grain running parallel to member length. Can be straight, curved, arched, tapered. Used as columns, beams (straight or curved), affixed side by side to form panels, and, in deep flatwise sections, as floor or roof decking similar to NLT.

Insurance for Mass Timber Construction (p.21): summary of a paper covering builder's risk and fixed property insurance for mass timber, aimed at developers, project teams, and insurance underwriters; discusses fire performance, post fire remediation, and moisture impacts as concerns insurers weigh.

Construction Types for Mass Timber and 2021 Codes (p.22 to 23): traces code recognition of CLT from the 2015 IBC (Type IV heavy timber) through the 2018 IBC (added detail for Types III, IV, V) to the 2021 IBC's three new construction types:
1. Type IV-A, maximum 18 stories, noncombustible protection required on all mass timber elements.
2. Type IV-B, maximum 12 stories, limited area of exposed mass timber walls and ceilings allowed.
3. Type IV-C, maximum 9 stories, all mass timber may be exposed (with exceptions such as shafts), designed for 2 hour fire resistance.

These three types are based on the existing Heavy Timber type, renamed Type IV-HT.

Case study, 77 Wade, Toronto (p.24 to 25): a hybrid concrete filled steel beam, glulam column, and prefabricated wood decking office tower, described as a modern take on century old NLT warehouse construction. Approx. 150,000 sq ft (14,000 sq m), 20,000 sq ft per floor, about 175 workstations per floor, 12 ft ceilings, nearly 80 percent of mass timber left exposed. Completed circa 2021.

Case study, Catalyst, Spokane WA (p.26 to 27): first CLT office building in Washington state, built from panels made at Katerra's CLT plant, targeting Passive House and zero carbon/zero energy (ILFI) certification. Five stories, approx 1000 students of classroom/lab space. All wood structural system: glulam columns and beams, CLT shear wall panels, and glulam/CLT composite floor and roof ribbed panels achieving long span wood to wood composite action (normally achieved with concrete). Structural grid was coordinated with the CLT plant's capabilities to a 30x30 ft spacing. 350 panels were erected by four crews (shear walls, columns and beams, floor panels, hardware) of 4 to 5 people each; the entire structure was erected in 11 weeks.

Case study, Model-C, Boston MA (p.28 to 29): a mid rise, multifamily, Passive House certified demonstration project by Generate and Placetailor, five stories, 19,000 sq ft, fourteen residential units plus ground floor co-working space. Kit of parts assembly from prefabricated CLT panels ("Model-C" building system), paired with high performance insulation and air sealing. Intended to adapt to diverse site parameters and taller applications.

### Timber Design Applications (p.30 to 55)

Section framing (p.30): span capability, construction type, fire resistance rating, and connections drive a mass timber building's optimal layout and structural grid. Notes overbuilds (adding stories atop an existing building) as a mass timber use case, and prefabrication as a schedule and labor cost lever, particularly for repeatable typologies (hotels, multifamily, dorms).

Case study, Candlewood Suites at Redstone Arsenal, AL (p.32 to 33): first hotel built in the US using CLT, part of the Privatization of Army Lodging program. Four story, 62,688 sq ft, rectangular slab on grade hotel using CLT for all exterior walls, parapet walls, interior walls, elevated floor slabs, and roof deck, plus glulam columns and beams. Thicker CLT can span up to 25 ft without beams or columns (a stated capability, not used here); the actual 3-1/8 in thick roof panels spanned 16-1/2 ft. Floors can bear directly on columns without intermediate beams at panel edges due to CLT's bidirectional capacity. CLT panels were manufactured to a tolerance of less than 1/16 in. Full height, four story concrete brick veneer used an engineered self-supporting brick product (self-supporting up to 85 ft) to avoid floor-by-floor support. CLT floor assembly achieved FIIC 74 versus a code minimum IIC of 50.

Mass Timber Office Design intro (p.34): frames the following four case studies around layout flexibility, code opportunities for fire/life safety, structural layout, acoustics, vibration, and cost, across solid wood, glulam, CLT, and NLT.

Case study, Clay Creative, Portland OR (p.36): mixed use, five stories of Type IIIA post and beam construction over a one story Type IA deck, 90,000 sq ft, about $300/sq ft. Dimension wood floor framing with plywood sheathing over vertical NLT decking, decking spans between glulam beams and girders on glulam columns. Exterior steel moment frame keeps the core open. Typical structural grid 25 x 30 ft, with mid bay beams in the long spans and girders in the short spans. Concealed steel bearing and knife plate connections attach to exposed glulam beams with countersunk bolts for fire protection.

Case study, Hudson Office Building, Vancouver WA (p.37): 45,000 sq ft, three story Type VB, structural brick exterior, exposed wood columns, beams, ceilings. Columns and beams on a 25 x 25 ft grid. NLT decking for floor and roof; exposed wood ceiling texture cited for reducing noise reflection (acoustics benefit for Class A office space).

Case study, Diamond Foods Innovation Center, Salem OR (p.38): two story, Type VA, dimension lumber walls, I-joists and glulam floors, light frame truss and glulam roof. Solar shading analysis used to size vertical fins and a moderate roof cantilever. Completed 2015, about $2.1 million including site work.

Case study, Karuna at One North, Portland OR (p.39): 85,540 sq ft, a five story Type IIIB building and a separate four story Type VA building, both heavy timber post and beam over a concrete podium, offices above ground floor retail. Fire retardant treated wood (FRTW) shear walls for lateral resistance; exterior sprinklers added to exceed the 40 ft combustible exterior finish limit. Estimated $175 to $225 per sq ft.

Mass Timber Cost and Design Optimization Checklist (p.41): summary of a WoodWorks checklist covering pre-design through design development cost items, meant to guide coordination between designers and builders (contractors, estimators, fabricators, installers). Full checklist is external (Learn More link).

Efficient Structural Grid Designs for Mass Timber Buildings (p.42): summarizes an external paper's argument that forcing a mass timber solution onto a grid laid out for steel or concrete produces member size inefficiencies; recommends laying out the grid around mass timber's material properties and the manufacturer's capabilities (a "system vs. product" approach), covering design parameters, connections, grid spacings and lessons from built projects. No grid numbers are given in this summary itself; see the case study grids above for concrete examples (25x25 to 30x30 ft).

Mass Timber Fire Design (p.44 to 45): mass timber chars at a predictable rate, giving inherent fire resistance while exposed. Summarizes construction type allowances: Type III permits timber in floors, roofs, interior walls (FRTW required at rated exterior walls); Type V permits timber throughout including exterior walls; Type IV (Heavy Timber) has been code recognized for over a century. Explains fire resistance rating (FRR) as the code metric (IBC section 703) and notes a 1/2 hour FRR requirement between dwelling units in sprinklered Type II-B/III-B/V-B construction, 1 hour otherwise (IBC 420, 708, 711). Points to a companion "Inventory of Fire Resistance Tested Mass Timber Assemblies and Penetrations" (p.45) covering floor, roof, and CLT wall assemblies, penetrations and fire stops, and connection tests.

Acoustics and Mass Timber (p.46 to 47): code baseline is STC 50 and IIC 50 for assemblies separating dwelling units (2018 IBC section 1206). Table of tested panels, transcribed under Quick reference above. Bare mass timber floor/ceiling assemblies are described as seldom used due to inadequate acoustic performance (example: bare 5-ply CLT floor at 6.875 in gives STC 41, IIC 25); a concrete or gypsum topping of 1 to 3 in is the common fix. Decouplers (resilient channels, air spaces, or underlayments/mats under a topping) break direct sound paths between finishes. Points to a companion "Acoustically Tested Mass Timber Assemblies" inventory (p.47) that also covers MPP and tongue and groove (T&G) decking assemblies, the only mention of MPP in the whole document.

Mass Timber Floor Vibration Design (p.48): summarizes an external design guide covering CLT (solid sawn or SCL laminations), NLT, and DLT floor panels on timber beam framing, aimed at giving vibration performance targets comparable to steel and concrete floor vibration guides. Notes vibration, not just strength, can govern panel grade, thickness, and support spacing on long span lightweight floors. No numeric targets given in this manual, the paper is external.

Mass Timber Connections (p.50 to 51): WoodWorks groups structural connections into three "Connection Classes" by cost, constructability, and fire rating.
- Class 1: mass timber elements and structural fasteners only. Example: beam bears directly on girder. May be inherently fire resistant per NDS calculations.
- Class 2: custom steel fabricated elements (plates, angles) plus structural fasteners. Examples: beam bears on a steel bearing seat with a knife plate, or beam connects to girder with steel angles. Needs additional fire protection.
- Class 3: prefabricated proprietary connectors (Simpson Strong-Tie, Rothoblaas, MiTek, etc), often backed by tested strength and fire ratings. Examples: beam connects to girder with a proprietary concealed knife plate connector (tested fire resistance rating as specified by manufacturer), or with a proprietary hanger (needs additional fire protection).

Fig., p.50: table "Connection Classes" with a simple line drawing for each class (beam-on-girder bearing notch for Class 1, steel angle bracket for Class 2, concealed fastener pattern for Class 3).
Fig., p.51: table "Connection Classes in Relation to Fire Rating" repeating the three classes plus a fourth column for a proprietary hanger variant, each with its own connection line drawing.
Cites governing references: IBC, ASCE/SEI 7, AWC National Design Specification for Wood Construction, APA T300 Glulam Connection Details Construction Guide, AISC Manual of Steel Construction.

Mass Timber Construction Management (p.52): summarizes a WoodWorks best practices overview of project management and installation training, and previews an upcoming (at time of writing) WoodWorks Mass Timber Construction Manual.

Building Enclosure Design Best Practices (p.53): summarizes an RDH Building Science guide; key point is that mass timber enclosures should stay dry and near room temperature, generally meaning most or all thermal insulation should sit on the exterior of the mass timber element.

Durability and Moisture Management (p.55): companion RDH Building Science guide on keeping mass timber warm and dry through construction and occupancy, and giving wetted elements a chance to dry.

### Solutions for Building Taller (p.56 to 73)

Section framing (p.56): 2021 IBC includes 14 code changes enabling mass timber structures up to 18 stories. Oregon, Washington, and California adopted taller wood construction ahead of the 2021 code change.

Case study, Origine, Quebec City (p.58 to 59): 13 story, 92 unit condominium, at the time the tallest timber building in eastern North America, completed October 2017, 9,600 sq ft footprint. Built under an "equivalent measures" performance path since the applicable code required noncombustible material above four stories at project start (2014); took two years of research to demonstrate equivalent safety. Wood used floor 1 through 13: load bearing walls, shear walls, floors and roof in CLT, glulam posts and beams. CLT encapsulated for fire protection in most areas, left exposed in some common areas. Erected in four months, including through winter months (December to April).

Case study, 2150 Keith Drive, Vancouver BC (p.62 to 63): 10 story commercial office, 162,491 sq ft (15,096 sq m), anticipated completion 2022. Perimeter braced structural system eliminates conventional cast in place concrete cores; above level 2, timber shear walls (interior) and diagonal glulam buckling restrained braces (exterior) resist wind and seismic load, creating a cellular exterior expression. Balconies hang from a diagonal strut aligned with the braced frames rather than cantilevering from the primary structure. Uses CLT and glulam as a prefabricated kit of parts on a concrete podium.

Tall Wood Buildings in 2021 IBC (p.64 to 65): explains the three new Type IV-A/B/C construction types (see Quick reference for the two tables reproduced here: "Required Noncombustible Protection on Mass Timber Elements by Construction Type" and "Required Fire-Resistance Ratings by Construction Type", IBC Table 601).
Fig., p.64: full table, columns IV-A / IV-B / IV-C / IV-HT, rows for interior surface of building elements, exterior side of exterior walls, top of floor above mass timber, and shafts, each giving minutes of required noncombustible protection.
Fig., p.65: full table, columns I-A / I-B / IV-A / IV-B / IV-C / IV-HT, rows primary structural frame, exterior bearing walls, interior bearing walls, floor construction, roof construction, each giving hours of required FRR.

Shaft Wall Requirements in Tall Timber Buildings (p.66): a shaft (2021 IBC section 202) is an enclosed space through one or more stories connecting vertical openings (stairs, elevators, MEP chases). Mass timber may be used for shaft walls in Types IV-A, IV-B, IV-C, except Type IV-A buildings exceeding 12 stories or 180 ft, where the shaft wall must be noncombustible. Shaft enclosures need FRR of at least 2 hours when connecting 4 or more stories, 1 hour otherwise, and never less than the FRR of the floor assembly they penetrate.
Fig., p.66: table "FRR Requirements for Tall Mass Timber Structures (Hours)", columns IV-A / IV-B / IV-C, rows primary frame, exterior bearing walls, interior bearing walls, roof construction, primary frame at roof, floor construction.

Demonstrating Fire Resistance Ratings for Mass Timber Elements in Tall Wood (p.68 to 69): FRRs for the new types mirror those for Type I steel/concrete construction. All mass timber in Types IV-A/B/C must also meet minimum member size criteria in IBC section 2304.11. Exposed timber allowances: Type IV-A none; Type IV-B ceilings (including integral beams) up to 20 percent of floor area per dwelling unit/fire area, or walls (including integral columns) up to 40 percent, or a combined ratio not exceeding 1.0; Type IV-C all exposed timber permitted (except shaft walls, concealed spaces, exterior wall exterior face).

Concealed Spaces in Mass Timber and Heavy Timber Structures (p.70): clarifies that exposed structural mass timber (CLT, glulam, NLT, SCL, T&G decking) is not limited to Type IV construction. Type III allows mass timber in floors, roofs, interior walls (exterior walls must be noncombustible or FRTW). Type V allows mass timber throughout, including exterior walls. Types I and II allow mass timber in select circumstances: roof construction (including primary frame under the 2021 IBC) in Types I-B, II-A, II-B; exterior columns and arches with 20 ft or more horizontal separation; balconies, canopies, similar projections.

Mass Timber Buildings and the IBC Code Guide (p.71): describes a joint ICC/AWC handbook (external, not this manual) covering 2015/2018/2021 IBC and 2021 IFC mass timber provisions, with over 100 photos/illustrations/tables, results from five full scale fire tests simulating Types IV-A/B/C, and 50 ICC certification practice questions.

Tall With Timber: A Mass Timber Tower Case Study (p.72 to 73): a 100 page external report comparing a mass timber design case against a cast in place post tension concrete baseline for a hypothetical 12 story mixed use tower in Seattle, 214 ft tall, roof of the highest occupied floor at 180 ft, street retail plus five floors of offices plus a hotel. Covers system design, structural cost, constructability, fire performance, environmental footprint, health/wellness, procurement/labor, and risk/insurance, with an appendix of modeling and scheduling detail.

### Mass Timber and Sustainability (p.74 to 95)

Section framing (p.74): positions wood as the lowest embodied energy major structural material and frames forest certification as the basis of mass timber's sustainability claim.

Case study, Platte Fifteen, Denver CO (p.76 to 77): five story, 150,418 sq ft, Type III-B office building. Glulam beams and columns, CLT floor and roof panels. Reduced construction time 20 percent versus a comparable steel structure. 30 x 30 ft structural grid. Lateral system is a concrete core plus a glulam frame with steel rod bracing (no wood shear walls). Final design: 3-ply CLT panels (floor and roof) with glulam beams and exposed columns. Roof designed for Colorado snow loads and a terrace with large tree planters. CLT floor panels topped with 3 in of concrete, but the team used the CLT itself, not the concrete topping, as the diaphragm.

Case study, John W. Olver Design Building, UMass Amherst (p.78 to 79): four story, 87,500 sq ft, Type IV, $52 million total / $36 million construction cost, completed January 2017. Glulam column and beam frame, glulam brace frame, CLT shear walls, timber concrete composite floor system, unconventional cantilevered forms. Predicted energy use intensity 43 kBTU/sq ft/yr versus a 62 kBTU/sq ft/yr code baseline (about 50 percent better). LCA showed savings of over 2,500 metric tons of carbon versus a steel and concrete baseline.

Case study, Bullitt Center, Seattle WA (p.80 to 81): six story, 52,000 sq ft office, built to Living Building Challenge standards (on site photovoltaics, water recycling, over 80 percent less energy than an average office). Podium structure of four wood floors over two stories of reinforced concrete, Type IV heavy timber frame in Douglas fir glulam beams and columns finished to an industrial appearance grade. Floors and roof are a solid dimension lumber deck, Douglas fir members set on edge and nailed together (an NLT-type floor, though the text calls it a "wood deck"), with CDX plywood diaphragms at roof, floor, and some walls. All wood FSC 100 percent certified, sourced within about 600 miles of the site. Designed for a 250 year service life. Total potential carbon benefit stated as 1,703 metric tons CO2.

Q+A with Edie Sonne Hall on forestry and carbon (p.83 to 85): forest product demand does not, per the interview, drive deforestation in the US and Canada, and can fund incentives to keep land forested. About 96 million acres of US forest are third party certified (SFI, FSC, ATFS), about 19 percent of US timberland versus an 11 percent global average. Best Management Practice compliance for water quality is over 90 percent across states cited. Cites the FAO's six strategies for forests and wood products in climate policy (afforestation, higher forest carbon stocks, more wood product carbon storage, reduced deforestation, biomass energy, wood construction materials displacing fossil-fuel-intensive materials).

How to Calculate the Wood Carbon Footprint of a Building (p.86 to 87): explains embodied carbon and life cycle assessment (LCA) concepts, the substitution benefit of using a lower-embodied-carbon material, EPDs (environmental product declarations), and notes wood is roughly 50 percent carbon by dry weight. Cites the Athena Impact Estimator for Buildings as a whole-building LCA tool. States a 2013 estimate of about 19,671 Gt CO2e of carbon stored globally in wood products in use, growing about 315.3 Gt CO2e/yr (units as printed in the source; likely intended as Mt, not Gt, but transcribed as stated).

The Impact of Wood Use on North American Forests (p.89): forest sustainability in the US/Canada is assessed against criteria covering biodiversity, ecosystem condition, soil and water, and social/economic value; wood demand is framed as an incentive to keep land forested.

The Role of Wood Products in Green Building (p.90): wood construction can earn 8 to 10 percent of available credits under LEED and Green Globes.

Designing Beneficial Spaces for Living, Working, and Well-being (p.92): summarizes a paper on biophilic design benefits of exposed wood (stress reduction, healing, learning, productivity, retail experience) and its overlap with wellness building standards, written in the context of Americans spending about 90 percent of their time indoors and post-COVID design shifts (touchless entries, ventilation, spacing).

Timber City (p.94): a Yale University research initiative (Gray Organschi Architecture, Hines Research Fund) studying mass timber's role in turning cities from carbon sources to carbon sinks, including regional mass timber supply and the practical challenges of building tall timber in dense urban centers.

## Rules for modelling

This source gives context and a handful of numeric anchors rather than a modelling procedure. Use it as follows.

1. Pick a product first: CLT (crosswise-layered solid panel, 3/5/7 ply, 5/8 to 2 in board thickness) for walls, floors, roofs and shear panels; NLT (edge-stacked, nailed, nominal 2x to 4x boards, 4 to 12 in wide) for decking-type floors and roofs, especially where a rib or plank aesthetic is wanted; glulam (parallel-laminated, straight or curved) for beams, columns, and long-span or curved members; DLT (on-end boards, dowel-fastened) as a metal-free NLT alternative when an all-timber joint is wanted. MPP is not detailed in this source; treat it as dimensionally similar to CLT/NLT plank products until a dedicated MPP source is available.
2. Default a structural grid to the 25x25 ft to 30x30 ft range seen across the case studies (Hudson 25x25, Clay Creative 25x30, Catalyst and Platte Fifteen 30x30) unless the experiment brief specifies otherwise; expect mid-bay beams on the long span and girders on the short span in a rectangular bay (as at Clay Creative).
3. For an exposed CLT or NLT floor or roof needing an acoustic or fire topping, add a 1 to 3 in concrete or gypsum topping layer (matches Platte Fifteen's 3 in topping and the acoustics chapter's general guidance); treat the mass timber panel, not the topping, as the diaphragm if the model needs to distinguish structural vs. added mass.
4. Pick a connection class to match the level of connection detail the experiment needs: Class 1 (timber-on-timber bearing, no visible steel) for a simple model, Class 2 (steel angle or knife plate bracket, partly concealed) for a mid-detail model, Class 3 (fully concealed proprietary connector) when the brief calls for a clean exposed-wood aesthetic with no visible hardware.
5. If the experiment involves a code-driven construction type choice, default to Type IV-C (all mass timber exposed, up to 9 stories, 2 hour fire resistance) for a low-rise fully-exposed design, Type IV-B (up to 12 stories, exposed ceilings capped near 20 percent of floor area or walls near 40 percent) for a mid-rise partly-exposed design, or Type IV-A (up to 18 stories, no exposed mass timber) for a tall, fully-protected design.
6. Do not invent span or member-size values beyond what is listed in the Quick reference table above. Where the brief needs an actual sized beam, column, or panel for a span the source does not cover, treat this document as insufficient and consult a design-oriented mass timber source instead (this manual is a case study and code overview, not a span table).
