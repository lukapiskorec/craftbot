# The CLT Handbook, CLT structures, facts and planning: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/The CLT Handbook 2022 - facts and planning.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://www.swedishwood.com/siteassets/5-publikationer/pdfer/clt-handbook-2019-eng-m-svensk-standard-2019.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** *The CLT Handbook: CLT structures, facts and planning*. Lead author Anders Gustafsson (RISE Research Institutes of Sweden). Co-authors Roberto Crocetti (Faculty of Engineering, Lund University), Alar Just, Pierre Landel, Jorgen Olsson and Anna Pousette (RISE), Magnus Silfverhielm (AIX Arkitekter), Birgit Ostman (Linnaeus University). Editors Eric Borgstrom and Johan Frobel, Swedish Wood. Published by Svenskt Tra (Swedish Wood, a unit of the Swedish Forest Industries Federation, Foreningen Sveriges Skogsindustrier), Stockholm, first edition, May 2019. ISBN 978-91-983214-4-3. 188 pages. English translation with Swedish standard references.

Despite the filename in this repository, the PDF's own imprint dates it 2019, not 2022. A separate, more heavily revised UK edition exists, *The CLT Handbook: CLT structures, design and detailing*, UK Edition 1:2022, adapted for UK codes by Arup and published by the Confederation of Timber Industries with Swedish Wood (file `manuals/The CLT Handbook 2022 - design and detailing.pdf`, covered in a separate extracted summary). The two books share most chapter topics and much source material but are separate editions for separate markets, not a facts volume plus a details volume.

**Scope of source:** A structural engineer's and architect's handbook for cross laminated timber, covering the material itself (manufacture, standard sizes, strength classes), CLT building systems and typologies, Eurocode 5 based structural design of CLT as beam and plate, joints and connections, floor and wall structural design, fire, acoustics, heat and moisture, and purchasing, handling and site assembly. Refers throughout to Eurocode 5 and the Swedish national annex (EKS), so some numeric design values (partial factors, some fire and acoustic classes) are Sweden specific; the underlying geometry, panel sizes, cross-section tables, span and load charts, joint layouts and transport and handling rules are general and usable anywhere. Does not cover connection design worked all the way to nailing schedules for a specific manufacturer's product line (refer to the manufacturer's ETA), does not cover non-CLT timber systems (glulam design beyond its use alongside CLT, timber frame), and does not include architectural drawings or a full building case study beyond the short worked examples in each design chapter.

**Most useful for:** sizing CLT floor and wall panels for early design (thickness versus span and load charts, panel and board dimension limits, transport limits), laying out joints and connections between CLT walls, floors, foundations and roofs, and building up wall and floor sections (build-up layers, thicknesses, fire and acoustic ratings) for a CLT structure model.

## Chapter 1: CLT as a construction material

### 1.1 to 1.3, introduction and CLT as a material
CLT (also called crosslam, X-Lam, BSP, mass timber, multiply) is glued boards layered at right angles, at least 3 layers, made to standard SS-EN 16351 with properties declared in a European Technical Approval (ETA). Introduced in Austria and Germany in the late 1990s (Kreuzlagerholz, KLH); first major Swedish CLT project was Inre Hamnen, Sundsvall, completed 2006. Figure 1.1 (p.11) charts European CLT production volume rising from near zero in 1990 to a forecast 0.6 to 1 million m3/year by 2018.

### 1.4, CLT in the eco-cycle
Table 1.1 (p.13) sets out the EN 15978 life-cycle module structure (A1 to A5 production and construction, B1 to B7 operation, C1 to C4 end of life, D other) used to compare a CLT frame against concrete-frame and other timber-frame alternatives. Figures 1.3 and 1.4 (p.14 to 15) chart production-phase and full life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions for six four-storey building designs (timber frame, concrete frame with timber curtain walls, box units, CLT surface units, each in standard and passive-house variants); the concrete-frame alternative runs roughly 100 kg CO2eq/m2 higher in the production phase than the wood options, about 10 tonnes CO2 for a 100 m2 apartment.

### 1.5, CLT manufacture
Manufacture (figure 1.6, p.17, a schematic loop diagram): finger-joint boards to length, plane the flat faces, glue under pressure (vacuum, low and even pressure even on non-level surfaces, or hydraulic, cold or hot pressed), then CNC finish (sawing, milling channels for installations, drilling, joint prep), visual check, label, package. Table 1.2 (p.16) gives raw board dimensions: thickness 20 to 45 mm commonplace (20 to 60 mm available), width 80 to 200 mm commonplace (40 to 300 mm available), strength class C14 to C30, width to thickness ratio 4 to 1. Table 1.3 (p.16) gives finished CLT panel dimensions: thickness 80 to 300 mm commonplace (60 to 500 mm available), width 1.2 to 3.0 m commonplace (up to 4.8 m available), length 16 m commonplace (up to 30 m available), 3, 5, 7 or 9 layers commonplace (up to 25 available). The architect's narrative on p.9 separately states panels up to 4.80 m tall and 30 m long with thicknesses of 60 to 500 mm. Treat 16 m x 3 m as the practical master panel envelope for early layout and the wider figures as manufacturer-dependent upper limits, always confirm with the specific supplier. Boards are glued at 8 to 15 percent moisture content, adjacent boards within 5 percent of each other.

### 1.6, properties
Strength (1.6.1): CLT is orthotropic, strength falls as moisture content and load duration rise; characteristic design values come from the 5 percent fractile of laboratory tests, stiffness values from the mean. Thermal (1.6.2): practical thermal conductivity 0.12 to 0.13 W/(m*K), specific heat capacity about 1600 J/(kg*K). Moisture movement (1.6.3, figure 1.7, p.20): a CLT panel drying from 20 to 10 percent moisture content moves roughly 0.1 mm per 100 mm across its width and length but about 1.4 mm per 100 mm through its thickness, since cross-lamination suppresses in-plane movement much more than through-thickness movement; target moisture content 12 percent, max 16 percent on delivery. Fire (1.6.4): charring penetrates at roughly 0.6 to 1.1 mm/minute (refined to 0.65 to 0.8 mm/min in chapter 7). Appearance classes (1.6.5, table 1.4, p.21): exposed surface, industrial surface, not exposed surface, each defined by permitted knot size, pitch pockets, splits and visible glue lines; use this when deciding which face of a CLT wall or floor can be left exposed in a model.

### 1.7, where CLT is used
Walls, floor and roof slabs, balconies and access balconies, mezzanine floors, lift shafts, stairwells (including solid CLT stair flights cut from offcuts), car park decks (a 17 m wide two-lane parking deck can be formed from solid or glulam-reinforced CLT slabs), riding arena linings, screens and balustrades, ventilation towers.

## Chapter 2: Design systems for CLT

Short chapter on how CLT panels are used structurally: as two-way or one-way spanning plates on two or more supports (figure 2.1 self-supporting roof panel, figure 2.2 trussed roof panel), as beams (integral lintels over openings form automatically when a door or window is cut from a panel, figure showing a CLT lintel over a door at Hyttkammaren, Falun), and as shell structures (figure 2.5, four examples of hyperbolic paraboloid and intersecting shells, since HP shells can be built from straight boards).

Composite floor structure principle (figure 2.3): a concrete topping takes compression, the CLT slab takes tension, requiring a shear connection between the two.

Section 2.4, Initial design, gives four preliminary sizing charts calculated to Eurocode 5 plus the Swedish national annex, explicitly for early-stage sizing only, not a substitute for final design:
- Figure 2.6 (p.27): floor panel thickness t (50 to 350 mm) against achievable span L (0 to 9 m) for service class 1, domestic load 2.0 kN/m2, deflection limit L/300 with max 20 mm and fundamental frequency over 8 Hz, for three flooring self-weights (25, 50, 100 kg/m2). Reading the chart: span rises from about 2.3 m at 60 mm thickness to about 8.0 to 8.4 m at 300 mm thickness (heavier flooring self-weight reduces the achievable span at a given thickness by roughly 0.5 to 1 m at the top end).
- Figures 2.7 to 2.9 (p.28 to 29): permitted centric vertical wall load Nd (kN/m) against wall clear height le (2.2 to 3.8 m) for panel thicknesses of 80, 100 and 120 mm, at two transverse load levels (qd approximately 0 to 1.5 kN/m and 3.0 kN/m). ## Chapter 3: Design of CLT structures

The Eurocode 5 structural design chapter. Compressed here to the geometric and dimensional content a modelling agent needs; the partial-factor arithmetic, beam and plate formulas and worked derivations are not transcribed.

Basis for design (3.1): safety classes 1 to 3 (partial factor 0.83, 0.91, 1.0), load duration classes (permanent, long, medium, short, instantaneous term) each with a kmod modification factor, and service classes 1 to 3 defined by relative humidity exposure (service class 1 under 65 percent RH most of the year, typical of heated buildings; service class 2 under 85 percent, typical of unheated or crawlspace conditions; service class 3 wetter than that, CE-marked CLT is not intended for service class 3). Material partial factor gamma_M recommended at 1.25 for CLT in Sweden (varies 1.15 to 1.3 by country, table 3.2).

Material properties (3.2): CLT is normally 3 to 7 layers, occasionally 9 or more, boards typically softwood, strength graded to SS-EN 338 in classes C14 to C30 (table 3.5, p.37 gives the standard board strength and stiffness values per class). ## Chapter 4: Joints and connections

The most useful chapter for connection geometry in a model. Figure 4.1 (p.72) shows five joint locations in a simple CLT frame: (1) wall panel to foundation, (2) post to floor slab, (3) wall panel to floor slab, (4) wall panel to roof, (5) ridge beam to roof.

Fastener types (4.3, figure 4.4, p.74): anchor nail and anchor screw (used with metal plates), wood construction screw (self-drilling, no pre-drilling), universal screw (upper and lower threads, joins two timber pieces), self-drilling dowel (for inset steel plates). Self-drilling wood screws range 4 to 13 mm diameter, up to 1000 mm long. ## Chapter 5: Floor structures

Three CLT floor types (figures 5.1 to 5.4, p.91 to 92): flat slab floor (the CLT panel alone carries and distributes the load), cassette/hollow floor (glulam web joists bonded above or below the slab, or two slabs sandwiching spaced web joists to form a hollow unit, joist spacing on the order of 500 mm per figure 5.2), and composite floor (CLT slab plus cast concrete topping joined by a shear connector).

Composite CLT-concrete floors (5.1.3, p.93 to 94): suited to spans of 6 to 12 m. Preliminary rule of thumb, total depth htot approximately span/25; concrete slab thickness hb approximately 0.4 x htot; CLT slab thickness hCLT approximately 0.6 x htot. Shear connectors: perforated steel plate glued to the CLT top face (HBV, Holz-Beton-Verbund, figure 5.6, about 85 percent composite interaction assumed for preliminary design) or a notch cut into the CLT top face with screws (figure 5.7, about 70 to 75 percent interaction).

Deformation and vibration (5.2, 5.3): serviceability usually governs CLT floor design (utilisation typically under 50 percent for load-bearing capacity). Deflection limit recommendation L/300 to L/600 (net final, table 5.2), max 20 mm. Vibration check per Eurocode 5's simplified method: fundamental frequency must exceed 8 Hz; deflection under a simulated 1 kN footstep point load must not exceed a national limit (Swedish EKS value a = 1.5 mm/kN); impulse velocity response checked against a national b factor (100 m/(Ns2) for Sweden). Table 5.3 gives relative damping guide values from 1.0 percent (plain wood floor) up to 3.5 to 4.0 percent (CLT with cast layer, four-sided support, especially on stud walls).

Fire (5.4) and acoustic (5.5) performance are covered in a short paragraph each here, referring forward to chapters 7 and 8; table 5.4 (p.102 to 103) is worth keeping as a build-up reference, it lists 11 named floor structure types with full layer stacks, total heights (250 to 493 mm) and CLT slab thickness (110 to 260 mm) alongside their measured or estimated impact and airborne sound performance.

Details (5.6, figures 5.11 to 5.15, p.104 to 105): five vertical sections for joining a floor slab to a bearing inner wall, an apartment-separating wall and an exterior wall, at increasing acoustic performance levels, each showing where the flanking transmission strip, sylomer isolation pad and suspended ceiling sit relative to the CLT slab.

Example calculation (5.7, p.106 to 109): a 160 mm 5-layer panel (40/20/40/20/40, all C24), 4.5 m simple span, self-weight 1.1 kN/m2, imposed load 2.0 kN/m2, walked through moment, shear, deflection and vibration checks; useful as a template for sanity-checking a modelled span.

## Chapter 6: Walls

Panel size and weight (p.110 to 111): wall panels are built 60 to 300 mm thick. Transport limits: panel height normally under 3.6 m (exceptionally up to 4 m), length up to 12 m without special transport. Storey-height wall units weigh 25 to 130 kg/m2. Table 6.1 (p.110) gives common panel dimensions with approximate line-load capacity for a 3 m wall (safety class 2, medium-term load): 80 mm/3-layer, 40 kg/m2, qRd 100 kN/m; 100 mm/5-layer, 50 kg/m2, qRd 175 kN/m; 120 mm/5-layer, 60 kg/m2, qRd 320 kN/m.

External wall example build-up (figure 6.1, p.111, outside to inside): 25 mm horizontal glulam cladding, 34x70 mm vertical battens, wind protection sheeting, vertical studs and insulation, 80 mm CLT panel, vapour retarder, horizontal studs and insulation, internal cladding. Partition wall example (figure 6.2): 13 to 15 mm plasterboard, 100 mm CLT, 70 mm insulation, 10 mm air gap, 70 mm insulation, 100 mm CLT, 13 to 15 mm plasterboard.

Structure stability (6.3): CLT wall panels stabilise buildings via diaphragm action from the floor slabs to the walls (figures 6.8 to 6.10). Overturning check treats the whole building as a unit; a simple safety check is that the vertical ground reaction stays within the building's "core limit", approximately width/6 from the centreline (figure 6.7). Compression perpendicular to the grain at wall-to-wall or wall-to-floor nodes should not use more than 1/3 of the total wall length as the effective bearing area unless a load-distributing layer is used. Progressive collapse is normally addressed at the whole-frame level (redundant stabilising walls), not component by component.

Fire (6.4): table 6.5, EI60 achieved with an 80 mm 3-layer wall, EI90 with a 120 mm 5-layer wall (fixed to an adjacent structure).

Acoustic (6.5): a single 65 mm CLT wall gives about 30 dB (weighted sound reduction + adaptation term); a double wall of two 65 mm CLT leaves with an insulated gap can reach much higher values, table 6.6 gives 31 dB (single 65 mm), 34 dB (single 105 mm), 55 dB (double 2x65 mm, 10 mm gap, 2x70 mm insulation), 58 dB (same but 2x95 mm insulation).

Wall cross-sections (6.6, tables 6.7 and 6.8, p.123): four external wall build-ups with U-values 0.10 to 0.25 W/m2K and total thickness 291 to 567 mm (CLT layer 80 or 100 mm plus insulation layers of 45 to 195 mm plus cladding), and two partition wall build-ups: single 80 mm CLT plus 30 mm insulation plus a second 80 mm CLT plus plasterboard (216 mm total, REI60, Dw 56 dB); or two 100 mm CLT sheets with 30 mm insulation between plus a separate stud/insulation layer (327 mm total, REI90, Dw 62 dB).

Detailed connections (6.7, p.124 to 127, figures 6.16 to 6.20): wall to foundation via a sole plate on the concrete slab, screwed from inside, with a waterproof strip; wall to floor either supported (floor rests on the wall, centric load, simpler) or suspended (floor hung between walls on hangers, allows lighter flanking strips but harder on tall buildings); wall to roof joists via angle brackets, truss hangers or skew screws, sometimes with cover plates to spread bearing pressure on thin roof ties; window openings mounted directly in the CLT or via an applied frame/bracket; external corners with supplementary studs and insulation added on site or prefabricated.

Example calculations (6.8, p.127 to 131): a ground-floor external wall (90 mm 3-layer panel, height 2.95 m, effective width between two windows 2.40 m) checked for buckling under 30 kN/m vertical load and 2.4 kN/m2 wind, 46 percent utilisation; and a wall panel over two spans (4.5 m and 6.5 m, 130 mm 5-layer panel) acting as a beam over openings supported on posts, 41 percent utilisation, including a 30 degree load-spreading rule from the support up to a height of h/4.

## Chapter 7: CLT and fire

Charring physics (7.1, figure 7.1): a char layer forms at roughly 300 degrees C, insulating the wood behind it; temperatures over 100 degrees C only reach about 10 mm beneath the char front, but strength and stiffness there are already reduced. Building classes Br0 to Br3 (Swedish BBR, table 7.1) set fire protection requirements by building size and evacuation risk; fire resistance classes combine load-bearing (R), integrity (E) and insulation (I) with a time in minutes (15 to 360).

Charring rates (7.2.1): one-dimensional charring rate beta0 = 0.65 mm/min for edge-glued or tight-jointed CLT (board gaps under 2 mm); notional design rate betan = 0.8 mm/min for CLT under Eurocode 5's rectangular-section rule (0.7 mm/min for glulam/LVL). Two adhesive-dependent charring scenarios (figure 7.5): melamine-urea-formaldehyde (MUF) glue lines char at the base rate with no delamination; some polyurethane (PUR) glue lines delaminate, doubling the charring rate for the first 25 mm of each board.

Effective cross-section method (7.2.2): the residual load-bearing section hef is the original thickness minus the char depth dchar minus a further non-load-bearing zone d0; tables 7.3 and 7.4 give d0 for 3-layer and 5-layer panels at fire durations 0 to 120 minutes, for floor slabs and wall panels, unprotected or protected. Section 7.4 works four numeric examples (unprotected/protected floor slab, unprotected/protected wall panel) applying this method; table 7.6 tabulates characteristic load-bearing capacity of CLT wall panels exposed to fire on one side.

## Chapter 9: CLT, heat and moisture

Thermal mass (9.1): CLT's high specific heat capacity (about 1600 J/(kg*K), table 9.1) versus its low density gives it good thermal storage relative to weight, evening out daily indoor temperature swings; periodic penetration depth for a 24-hour cycle is about 70 mm for wood versus 140 mm concrete, 210 mm granite (table 9.2), and phase shift through a mass timber wall can reach about 12 hours, roughly twice as fast as an equivalent stone wall would need to be to match it. Moisture movement (9.2): CLT's in-plane swelling/shrinkage is about 0.016 to 0.023 percent per percentage change in moisture content, similar to plywood and much less than solid wood across the grain, because of the alternating layer directions. Thermal insulation (9.3): CLT's thermal conductivity in practice is taken as 0.13 to 0.14 W/(m*K); a full worked U-value example (13 mm plasterboard, 100 mm CLT, vapour retarder, 170 mm insulation with 12 percent timber studs, wind barrier, 34 mm air gap, cladding) is carried through both the SS-EN 6946 U-value method and lambda-value method, arriving at U approximately 0.22 W/(m2*K).

## Chapter 10: Purchasing and assembly

Highly relevant to erection sequencing and transport limits in a model. An enquiry to a CLT manufacturer should specify assembly order and part numbering, unit counts, dimensions and tolerances, appearance grade if exposed, wood species if non-standard, and packaging/craning preferences (table 10.1).

Delivery, storage, transport (10.2.1, p.165): in Sweden, loads up to 24 m long, 2.6 m wide and 4.5 m high travel without special transport permits. Delivery moisture content is at most 16 percent. On-site storage: keep components clear of soil, avoid sun exposure (causes splitting/condensation risk), rest panels on clean supports at least 300 mm off the ground for ventilation and to prevent bending, with enough supports along the length; keep the substrate dry and level.

Lifting (10.2.2 to 10.2.4, p.166 to 169, figures 10.1 to 10.3): floor and wall units are lifted via statically determinate rigging (figure 10.1) using webbing slings, wire rope or chain, through cast-in bolts with load-distributing washers, spherical head lifting anchors fixed with wood screws (figure 10.2), or slings threaded through drilled holes. Wall units commonly have 2 lifting points (two-leg chain sling) or 4 (four safety hooks and wire ropes) depending on weight and shape; walls over 8 to 10 m long are considered unstable in wind during lifting. Crane dynamic supplement on self-weight: 10 to 20 percent. Table 10.2 gives guide self-weights: CLT material 500 kg/m3, a 70 mm CLT panel about 35 kg/m2, a 180 mm panel about 90 kg/m2, a complete external wall (with insulation and cladding) 110 to 150 kg/m2.

Frame stabilisation during construction (10.2.5): wall units need immediate temporary bracing and anchoring after lifting; anchors are fitted storey by storey as the frame rises and re-checked for tension once the frame is complete, since vertical shortening under load can loosen them.

Weather protection during construction (10.3, p.170 to 171): four systems in use, temporary tarpaulins on an exposed frame, weather sheeting on facade scaffolding, fixed or wheeled temporary roofing (aluminium lattice beams with PVC sheeting, can run on rails), and climbing weather protection (mast structures that follow the building up storey by storey, sometimes with an internal crane). Moisture inspection (10.3.2, 10.4): electrical resistance moisture meters with insulated hammer electrodes are the standard field method (dry weight method is the reference method); measurement error in the field is about +/- 2.6 percent moisture content; local moisture content should stay under 18 percent to prevent rot risk, and mould risk starts around 75 percent relative humidity in warm conditions. Figure 10.5 shows correct electrode placement for measuring average moisture (0.3x the panel width from the edge, 300 mm from the end) versus surface moisture (electrodes flat against the surface parallel to the end).

## Rules for modelling

1. Pick a panel envelope. Default a CLT master panel to 16 m long by up to 3 m wide by up to 300 mm thick for early layout (table 1.3); treat up to 4.8 m wide, up to 30 m long, up to 500 mm thick as manufacturer-dependent maximums, not defaults.
2. Choose a wall thickness from table 6.1: 80 mm (3-layer, about 40 kg/m2), 100 mm (5-layer, about 50 kg/m2) or 120 mm (5-layer, about 60 kg/m2) for typical multi-storey loads, or read off figures 2.7 to 2.9 for a specific wall height and load. Keep wall panel thickness at 60 to 300 mm; treat under 70 mm as fire-load limited.
3. Keep wall units under 3.6 m tall and 12 m long for standard transport (up to 4 m tall only in exceptional cases); treat any panel over 8 to 10 m long as unstable in wind until braced.
4. Pick a floor slab thickness and layer count from table 5.1 by target span: 100 mm/3-layer up to about 3.7 to 3.9 m, 140 mm/5-layer up to about 4.2 to 4.5 m, 160 mm/5-layer up to about 5.2 to 5.3 m (simply supported figures are slightly larger than continuous-span figures, which are sagging-governed). For CLT-concrete composite floors spanning 6 to 12 m, size the total depth at span/25, split roughly 40 percent concrete and 60 percent CLT by depth.
5. Build cross-sections from the real layups in table 3.11 (3-layer, 60 to 120 mm) and table 3.12 (5-layer, 100 to 200 mm, 27 combinations) rather than inventing arbitrary layer splits; all follow an odd, symmetrical layer count with the outer layers running the panel's main structural direction.
6. Place joints at the five locations in figure 4.1 (foundation, post/floor, wall/floor, wall/roof, ridge/roof) and choose a joint type per section 4.4: loose tongue or cover plate for CLT-to-CLT edge joints, self-drilling screws or angle brackets for wall-to-wall and wall-to-floor, a sole plate and angle bracket or nail plate for wall-to-foundation.
7. Space fasteners in multiples of the screw diameter d: in the CLT plane use a1 = 4d (parallel/perpendicular), a2 = 2.5d (end/edge), a3/a4 = 6d (loaded end/edge); at a panel edge use a1 = 10d, a2 = 3d, a3,t = 12d, a3,c = 7d.
8. Add a concrete podium or ground floor under a CLT superstructure where the ground-floor program differs from the floors above; this is standard practice even though it is not covered as a numbered rule in this source (see chapter 2 introduction and the parking deck example in chapter 1).
9. Size openings and lintels so piers between windows are wide enough to be checked as columns (buckling length equal to storey height, chapter 2.1); CLT panels give an automatic integral lintel when an opening is cut, so a separate lintel member is only needed for wide spans.
10. When laying out fire-rated walls, use 80 mm 3-layer for EI60 and 120 mm 5-layer for EI90 as starting points (table 6.5), then add board thickness for the design charring rate, 0.65 to 0.8 mm/min, plus a non-load-bearing buffer layer (chapter 7.2).
11. When generating a build-up (not just the structural CLT layer), use the layer stacks in tables 6.7/6.8 (walls) and table 5.4 (floors) for realistic total thicknesses: exterior walls run 291 to 567 mm total, partition walls 216 to 327 mm, floors 250 to 493 mm.
12. For transport and erection sequencing, respect the 24 m x 2.6 m x 4.5 m permit-free envelope (Sweden), lift with 2 or 4 point rigging depending on unit weight and shape, and brace/anchor each wall unit immediately after placement, storey by storey.
