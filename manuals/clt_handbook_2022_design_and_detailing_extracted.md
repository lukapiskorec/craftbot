# The CLT Handbook, CLT structures, design and detailing: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/The CLT Handbook 2022 - design and detailing.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://www.swedishwood.com/siteassets/5-publikationer/pdfer/clt-ukedition-2022.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** The CLT Handbook, CLT structures, design and detailing, UK edition 1:2022. Lead author Anders Gustafsson (RISE Research Institutes of Sweden). UK edition authors Andrew Lawrence and Ishan Abeysekera (Arup), with Philip Wright and Sam Peckham (Arup, sound chapter) and Jake Hacker (Arup, thermal chapter). Co-authors of the Swedish original: Roberto Crocetti, Alar Just, Pierre Landel, Jorgen Olsson, Anna Pousette, Magnus Silfverhielm, Birgit Ostman. Published by Swedish Forest Industries Federation (Swedish Wood), Stockholm, in collaboration with Arup and the Confederation of Timber Industries, 2022. ISBN 978-91-985212-2-1. 156 pages.

A companion volume, "The CLT Handbook, CLT structures, facts and planning" (also UK edition 1:2022), covers the more general planning-stage material and is extracted separately. This file covers only the design and detailing volume.

**Scope of source:** covers CLT as a construction material and how it is manufactured, the Eurocode 5 partial-factor basis of design, out-of-plane and in-plane structural behaviour with section-property design tables for 3-layer and 5-layer panels, connection types with worked design equations and minimum spacing and edge-distance tables, floor design (deflection, vibration, fire, acoustics, example build-ups), wall design (shear walls, stability, build-ups, connection details), fire resistance and charring design, acoustic performance and junction detailing, thermal comfort and condensation, and procurement, moisture protection, delivery, lifting and temporary stability on site. It does not include manufacturer-specific span tables or ETA values (the reader is repeatedly pointed to the relevant manufacturer's European Technical Assessment), no seismic design guidance, no costing information, and no dedicated roof chapter. Roof geometry only appears where it intersects with walls (wall-to-roof connections in chapters 4 and 6) and briefly in a fire and thermal-mass sense; there are no rafter, truss or roof-covering details of the kind found in framed-timber roofing manuals. History and general market context are covered only briefly in chapter 1.

**Most useful for:** sizing and detailing CLT wall, floor and roof panel build-ups and thicknesses, laying out the connections between CLT panels (wall-to-wall, wall-to-floor, wall-to-foundation, wall-to-roof) with correct screw, nail, dowel and bracket spacing, and getting the sound and fire junction details, moisture-content limits and erection sequence right for a CLT building model.

## Chapter notes

### Chapter 1, CLT as a construction material (p8)

CLT (cross-laminated timber, also called crosslam or X-Lam) is boards or planks glued in layers at right angles, at least 3 layers, usually up to 7, sometimes 9 or more. Spruce and pine are the commonest species. Manufacture (fig 1.6, p17): boards are finger-jointed into long lengths, planed, assembled into sheets and glued under vacuum or hydraulic (cold or hot) pressure, then CNC-finished (sawing edges, milling channels, drilling, sanding). Certification is via BS-EN 16351 and an ETA (European Technical Assessment); products carry a CE mark or the UK's own UKCA mark.

Table 1.2 (p16) gives board dimensions (see Quick reference). Table 1.3 (p16) gives panel dimensions. A CLT panel's cross-section is described by a length l, width b and thickness t per figure 1.5 (p16), with layer thicknesses often, but not always, equal.

Appearance classes (Table 1.4, p21): Exposed surface (few small pitch pockets, small knots, no enclosed bark or open scars), Industrial surface (larger permitted knots and pitch pockets), Not exposed surface (most defects except firm/soft rot permitted).

Moisture-related movement (1.6.3, p20): CLT expands/contracts far less in-plane than solid wood because of the alternating grain, but an uninsulated panel exposed to a moisture gradient (warm dry inside, cold damp outside) can bow. Fire (1.6.4, p20): charring rate about 0.6 to 1.1 mm/min for panels with heat-resistant glue.

CLT is used mainly for walls and floors, low to medium rise blocks of flats, schools, nurseries, houses, offices and sports centres; it is also used for mezzanine floors, balustrades, stairs and non-structural screens.

The eco-cycle/LCA material in 1.4 (p12 to 15) is background only; no geometry.

### Chapter 2, Basis of design of CLT structures (p24)

Design follows the Eurocode partial factor method: Ed less than or equal Rd. Loads are combined per BS-EN 1990 with combination (psi0), frequent (psi1) and quasi-permanent (psi2) factors (2.1.1, p25).

Service classes 1, 2, 3 are defined by ambient relative humidity and determine kmod and kdef (2.1.2, p26; tables 2.2 and 2.3, p29). CLT is not used in service class 3 (2.1.5, p29). Moisture-related dimensional change is given in table 2.1 (p27): negligible parallel to grain, 0.19 percent per 1 percent moisture change radially, 0.36 percent tangentially, 0.24 percent averaged across the grain; a CLT panel's in-plane change is close to the "parallel to grain" figure because of cross-lamination (about 0.016 to 0.023 percent per 1 percent moisture change).

Table 2.4 (p31) gives characteristic strength and mean/5-percentile stiffness values for boards of strength classes C14, C16, C24, C30 (see Quick reference); C24 with C14/C16 inner layers is the commonest CLT make-up. The system strength factor ksys (2.1.6, p30, fig 2.2) increases CLT's effective bending/tensile strength above the constituent board value because several boards share the load; it ranges roughly 0.9 to 1.2 depending on effective width.

### Chapter 3, Design of CLT structures (p34)

Out-of-plane behaviour (3.1, p34): bending and shear are calculated with beam theory using effective properties [EI]eff,out, accounting for the fact that transverse layers do not contribute directly to bending stiffness but do transfer shear between the longitudinal layers (fig 3.2, p35, EIeff,out calculation; fig 3.3, shear stress distribution; fig 3.4, first moment of area).

In-plane behaviour (3.2, p38): CLT panels used as diaphragms or shear walls see compression, tension and shear parallel to the panel plane, plus torsion and complementary shear flows at the crossing points of the boards (figs 3.10 to 3.15, p41 to 44), which is the CLT-specific failure mode that has no solid-timber analogue.

Design tables (3.5, p50): Table 3.4 gives cross-sectional area, Iy,out/Wy,out (strong axis) and Ix,out/Wx,out (weak axis) per linear metre for 3-layer panels from 60 to 120 mm total thickness. Table 3.5 gives the same for 5-layer panels from 100 to 200 mm total thickness, with layer thicknesses from 20/20/20/20/20 up to 40/40/40/40/40 mm. These tables are the fastest way to size a panel's out-of-plane bending capacity by hand; a modelling agent building CLT floor/roof panels can read a thickness build-up straight off them (see Figure index below, p50 to 51).

### Chapter 4, Connections (p52)

The most detailed and geometry-heavy chapter for jointed CLT construction. Figure 4.1 (p52) shows the five generic joint locations in a simple CLT building: wall-to-foundation, post-to-floor, wall-to-floor, wall-to-roof, ridge-beam-to-roof.

Joint types (4.2, p54 to 55, fig 4.4): anchor nail, anchor screw (used with metal plates), wood construction screw (self-drilling, no pre-drilling), universal screw (different thread pitches each end, joins two timber pieces), self-drilling dowel (assembles inset steel plates). Standard metal plates and brackets: light duty angle brackets (moderate loads, 2 to 4 mm steel, 5 mm holes) and heavy duty angle brackets (2 to 3 mm steel, larger holes for expansion anchors into concrete), plus punched nail plates.

Joints in the CLT plane (4.3.1, p55 to 56): joint with a loose tongue (plywood/LVL spline, rarely used in the UK); joint with single or double cover plate (fig 4.8, 4.9); half-lap joint (fig 4.10, popular for its simplicity); joint with single cover plate plus diagonal skew screws for out-of-plane shear/tension (fig 4.11); joint with connecting steel sleeves and fully threaded screws (fig 4.12, uncommon in UK); joint with a specialist fixing on skewed screws (fig 4.13); weak moment connections perpendicular to and along the span direction using cover plates (figs 4.14, 4.15).

Connections to beams (4.3.3, p56 to 57, fig 4.16): steel I-beam let into the CLT depth (a to c), glulam beam below the CLT (d), or glulam beam as continuous internal support (e).

Wall-to-wall connections (4.3.4, p57 to 58): screws through the face at an angle (fig 4.17) or angle brackets (fig 4.18) at a corner.

Wall-to-floor connections (4.3.5, p58, figs 4.19 to 4.23): long wood screws through the floor into the wall below and skew-screwed wall above; angle brackets; fully threaded rods with threaded sleeves; concealed inset fixings with dowels; longitudinal angle bracket.

Wall-to-foundation and wall-to-roof (4.3.6, p58 to 59): nail plate or angle bracket cast or anchored into concrete (fig 4.24); for roofs, self-drilling screws directly through the wall panel into roof members is simplest (fig 4.25, three erection stages a to c, wall panel then glulam ridge beam then roof panel).

Design of connections (4.4, p60 to 68) gives the calculation method (not needed for geometry alone) but also the tables a modelling agent needs directly:
- Table 4.1/4.2 (p61): embedment strength fh,k for screws perpendicular to the panel face and into the panel edge, by screw diameter 6 to 12 mm.
- Table 4.3/4.4 (p63): characteristic withdrawal capacity Fax,Rk in kN by screw diameter (6 to 12 mm) and anchorage length (50 to 250 mm), for face and edge fixing respectively.
- Skew screwing (fig 4.29, p63): when screwing perpendicular to grain into an edge, angle screws at least 30 degrees to the grain and place them in pairs.
- Tables 4.5 to 4.8 and figures 4.32/4.33 (p67 to 68): minimum spacing and edge/end distance for screws, nails and dowels in both the face and the edge of a CLT panel, expressed as multiples of fastener diameter d (see Quick reference for the values).

### Chapter 5, Floors (p69)

Three floor types (5.1, p69 to 71): CLT slab (fig 5.1), ribbed slab with glued glulam web joists top or bottom of the CLT slab, voids often filled with mineral wool and a separate suspended ceiling for acoustic isolation (fig 5.2, about 500 mm overall depth in the example), and CLT concrete composite floor (fig 5.3), joined either by an HBV (Holz-Beton-Verbund) perforated steel shear connector glued into the CLT top surface (fig 5.5) or by a notch cut into the CLT with a screw to take tension (fig 5.6).

Deformation and dynamics (5.2, 5.3, p72 to 81) are calculation-heavy; the key rule of thumb is that composite floor depth h_tot approx L/25 for spans 6 to 12 m, split roughly 40 percent concrete / 60 percent CLT by depth. Dynamic design uses a response factor method (Willford et al); floors are checked for both resonant response (up to 4x walking frequency) and transient response.

Details (5.6, p82 to 83, figs 5.11 to 5.15) show full wall and floor build-ups for a single-family house and party-wall acoustic separation, transcribed under Figure index below; these are drawn cross-sections a modelling agent can follow directly for layer order.

### Chapter 6, Walls (p86)

A CLT wall panel ranges 60 to 300 mm thick, up to about 3.6 x 12 m for ordinary transport (exceptionally 4 m x 16 to 20 m with specialist transport), weighing 25 to 130 kg/m2. Load-bearing external walls carry floor loads and resist out-of-plane wind and (if part of the stability system) in-plane lateral loads; visually exposed CLT is rarely thinner than 70 mm even in a small house because of fire charring allowance.

Wall build-ups (fig 6.1, p87, external wall of single family house, outside to inside): 25 mm horizontal glulam cladding, 34x70 mm vertical battens, breather membrane, vertical studs and insulation, 80 mm CLT panel, vapour barrier, horizontal studs and insulation, internal cladding. Fig 6.2 (p87, partition wall between apartments, twin-wall acoustic separation): plasterboard, 100 mm CLT, 70 mm insulation, 10 mm air gap, 70 mm insulation, 100 mm CLT, plasterboard.

Shear wall deformation (6.3, p88 to 92) has four modes: bending, shear, sliding (at anchor connections) and rocking (fig 6.3 to 6.6). Overturning is checked against the middle-third rule (fig 6.7, p91); the load path for wind is facade to floor diaphragm to shear wall to foundation (fig 6.8). The handbook recommends keeping the lateral stability system statically determinate, for example just two shear walls in one direction and one in the other, because indeterminate timber diaphragm/wall stiffness is hard to predict reliably.

Connection details (6.7, p93 to 96): wall-to-foundation typically a sole plate screwed up from inside plus a waterproof strip and (recommended, not always shown) an upstand to keep the wall off standing water (fig 6.10). Wall-to-floor is either "platform frame" with the floor directly supported on the wall below (fig 6.11, described as the usual UK method) or a suspended floor between walls on hangers or a support beam. Wall-to-roof (fig 6.12, p95): angle brackets, truss hangers, or skew-screwing directly; roof trusses may have glued/nailed cover plates to spread bearing. Window details (fig 6.13, p95): window mounted directly in the CLT plane, or on an external frame/angle bracket. External corners (fig 6.15, p96): additional studs and insulation added on site around the load-bearing CLT corner panel.

Example calculations (6.8, p96 to 97) size a post between two window openings in a 90 mm 3-layer wall panel for combined axial load and out-of-plane wind bending, including buckling check; this is the pattern to follow for any wall-with-openings sizing.

### Chapter 7, CLT and fire safety (p98)

Fire resistance of CLT is calculated by an effective (reduced) cross-section method: an effective charring depth d_char (one-dimensional, beta0 = 0.65 mm/min, doubling to 2 x beta0 for the first 25 mm of each lamella if the glue is not heat/fire resistant, fig 7.4, p103) plus a non-load-bearing "zero-strength" layer d0 whose thickness is read from tables 7.1 to 7.3 (p104) depending on the number of layers (3, 5 or 7), whether the panel side is in tension or compression, and whether it is protected (for example by plasterboard) or unprotected. The residual effective cross-section h_ef = h_CLT minus d_char minus d0 (fig 7.5, p105) is then checked at 20 percent fractile strength (k_fi = 1.15 for CLT, converting 5 percent to 20 percent fractile, eq 7.11, p106).

Design examples (7.4, p108 to 111) work through: an unprotected 7-layer (133 mm, 19 mm boards) floor slab after 60 minutes fire exposure, residual cross-section 49 to 69 mm depending on whether char ablation is assumed; the same slab protected by 12.5 mm gypsum plasterboard, residual 80 mm; an unprotected 5-layer (95 mm, 19 mm boards) wall panel after 30 minutes, residual 58 mm (2 of 3 layers remain load-bearing); the same wall protected by 15 mm plasterboard on the fire side, after 60 minutes, residual 49 mm. Table 7.4 (p111) tabulates load-bearing capacity (kN/m) at 30, 60 and 90 minutes for several protection combinations on a 95 mm wall, from 277 kN/m cold-state down to as little as 0.6 kN/m for a thin, poorly protected build-up at 90 minutes.

Fire-stopping at joints (fig 7.8, p107) identifies four routes fire can bypass fire-resisting elements: through service penetrations, at panel-to-panel joints, through the connection area before the protection fails, and through interior wall panel/element joints; all must be sealed.

### Chapter 8, CLT and sound (p112)

Bare CLT panels are lightweight and stiff, and transmit sound efficiently, so flanking transmission (via connected floors and walls, not just the direct path through the separating element) dominates acoustic design in CLT buildings; controlling flanking by discontinuities/resilient junctions is generally more effective than adding mass to linings.

Table 8.2 (p120) and Table 8.3 (p121) give example flat, cassette and CLT-concrete floor build-ups with indicative Rw, Rw+Ctr and Ln,w (impact) values across a range of depths, from a bare 135 to 175 mm slab (Rw 36 to 40) up to a 300 to 395 mm build-up with suspended ceiling and resilient hangers (Rw up to 65 to 70). Table 8.4 (p123) and Table 8.5 (p124) do the same for single-panel and double-panel walls, from bare CLT (Rw 30 to 33) to a lined double-panel wall (Rw up to 65).

Junction details (8.5, p125 to 130, figs 8.4 to 8.13) are drawn vertical and plan sections showing angle brackets, resilient bearings, sylomer strips, impact insulation layers and suspended ceilings at floor-to-floor and wall-to-floor junctions, at three performance levels (low/medium/high sound insulation); see Figure index.

### Chapter 9, CLT and thermal comfort (p131)

CLT's thermal mass (density 500 kg/m3, specific heat capacity 1,380 J/kgK, Table 9.1, p132) is moderate, well below masonry or concrete but useful for evening out daily temperature swings. Vapour control (9.2, p132): a CLT panel of 5 or more layers and over 70 mm thick can often act as its own vapour barrier if joints are sufficiently airtight. Thermal insulation (9.3, p132 to 135) works through a full U-value calculation example for an external wall (13 mm plasterboard, 100 mm CLT, vapour barrier, 170 mm insulation with 12 percent timber studs by area, breather membrane, 34 mm air gap, glulam cladding), giving U approx 0.22 W/m2K.

### Chapter 10, Procurement and site works (p136)

Tender information should specify strength class, appearance grade, glue/charring rate, dimensions and tolerance, factory-cut openings, programme and target moisture content (10.1, p136); it is recommended that one engineer sizes both the CLT panels and their connections, since panel thickness is often governed by the connections.

Moisture protection (10.2, p136 to 139, fig 10.1): protect from precipitation, keep the ground drained, ventilate and protect outdoor storage, protect from dirt and sun, check packaging integrity and quality, stack on stickers if damp, keep CLT stored indoors where possible. Moisture content must stay below 18 percent at all times to prevent decay or delamination; CLT arrives at no more than 16 percent. Panels should rest on clean, dry supports at least 300 mm off the ground. A moisture content control plan should record target and critical moisture contents, drying and protection methods, and how and when moisture will be measured. Measurement method (fig 10.2, p139): electrical resistance meter with insulated hammer electrodes, measuring about 300 mm from the panel end and 0.3 times the panel's width from the edge, to a depth of 0.3 times the board thickness (drilling deeper to check the second and third lamellas if needed).

Delivery, lifting and temporary stability (10.3, p139 to 141): panels are loaded in reverse assembly order so they unload in the order they are erected, and each is labelled per the assembly plan. Lifting uses webbing slings, wire rope or chain, through pre-formed eyes or spherical head lifting anchors screwed in place (fig 10.4); the lifting system must be statically determinate (fig 10.3) so load is shared evenly across all lifting points. Wall units with two lifting points use a two-leg chain sling; four-point units use four safety hooks and wire ropes. Wall panels longer than 8 to 10 m are considered thin and unstable in wind during lifting. Temporary bracing is needed until the next floor locks the frame; permanent tie-down anchors are usually pre-stressed slightly to allow for long-term settlement, and must be re-checked for tension once the building is complete.

## Rules for modelling

1. Build a CLT panel as an odd number of layers (3, 5, 7, 9, or more), each layer's grain rotated 90 degrees from the one below; total thickness from Table 3.4 (3-layer, 60 to 120 mm) or Table 3.5 (5-layer, 100 to 200 mm), or any manufacturer thickness in the 60 to 500 mm range (p16).
2. Default panel plan size to within 1.20 to 3.60 m width and up to 20 m length for routine transport; flag anything larger than 3.6 x 12 m as needing specialist transport (p86).
3. Size a wall panel's thickness first for fire (rarely under 70 mm if visually exposed, more if a longer fire-resistance period is required), then check it satisfies the connection and buckling requirements of chapter 4 and 6.8.
4. At every wall-to-wall corner, model either screws driven parallel to the grain at an angle (skew screwing) or an angle bracket per figures 4.17/4.18; do not place a joint on end-grain screwing alone.
5. At every wall-to-floor junction, decide platform frame (floor sits on top of the wall below, the default UK method, fig 6.11) versus suspended floor (floor hung between walls); this decision changes the vertical stacking order of panels.
6. At the wall-to-foundation junction, model a sole plate or angle bracket fixed to the slab, plus an upstand under the wall if water pooling is a risk (fig 6.10, p94; p93).
7. At the wall-to-roof junction, model self-drilling screws, angle brackets or truss hangers directly connecting the top of the wall panel to the roof member (fig 4.25, fig 6.12).
8. When placing fasteners in a CLT face or edge, respect the minimum spacing and edge-distance multiples of fastener diameter d given in tables 4.5 to 4.8 (p67); never place a fastener load in tension parallel to the grain in the outer edge without angling it at least 30 degrees to the grain, in pairs (fig 4.29).
9. For a composite CLT-concrete floor, split the total depth roughly 60 percent CLT, 40 percent concrete, with total depth about span/25 for spans of 6 to 12 m (p72).
10. For acoustic separation between dwellings, do not rely on a single CLT panel; use either a lined single panel with a cavity (Table 8.4) or a double CLT panel arrangement with an air gap containing mineral wool (Table 8.5, fig 6.2), and keep floor/ceiling continuity broken at the separating line wherever the acoustic table calls for a discontinuous floor.
11. For fire design, model the loss of an effective charred layer (d_char, doubling near the surface if using non-fire-resistant glue) plus a non-load-bearing zero-strength layer (d0) at every exposed CLT face, per tables 7.1 to 7.3, rather than assuming full thickness is structural during a fire check.
12. Keep the CLT moisture content assumption during erection below 18 percent, storage at least 300 mm off the ground, and treat any panel longer than 8 to 10 m as unstable to handle in wind during lifting (p137, p141).
13. Sequence erection so that walls are craned and immediately braced before the next floor is placed on top; do not model a completed floor diaphragm before the walls below it are laterally braced (p141).
