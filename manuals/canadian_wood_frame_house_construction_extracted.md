# Canadian Wood-Frame House Construction: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Canadian Wood-Frame House Construction.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.700100/publication.html and save it under that name.

**Source:** *Canadian Wood-Frame House Construction*, Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC), text updated by John Burrows, JF Burrows Consulting Inc. Third combined imperial and metric edition, revised 2013 (first published 1967). ISBN 0-660-19535-6, catalogue no. NH17-3/2005. 335 pages as paginated in the PDF (the printed folio starts at "1" on PDF page 20, so PDF page number equals printed page number plus 19 throughout the body and appendices).

**Scope of source:** A full-book, chapter-by-chapter guide to building a typical platform-framed, wood-frame house in Canada, aligned with the 2010 National Building Code of Canada (NBC) and its 2012 energy efficiency changes. Covers planning and permits, concrete, lumber and panel products, the building envelope (water, air, vapour, heat), site work, footings and foundations, floor, wall, ceiling and roof framing, roof and wall sheathing and coverings, flashing, windows and doors, exterior trim, stairs, chimneys and fireplaces, plumbing and electrical framing accommodations, space conditioning, interior finishes, floor coverings, interior doors and millwork, coatings, eavestroughs, decks, garages, site drainage and maintenance. Appendix A carries 44 reference and span tables; Appendix B is a fully labelled cutaway drawing of a whole house. Does not cover structural design procedures beyond prescriptive span-table lookups (points to NBC Part 4 or the Canadian Wood Council's Engineering Guide for Wood-Frame Construction 2009 for anything outside the tables), mass timber or CLT construction, factory modular or panelized systems beyond roof trusses, seismic or wind engineering calculations, detailed cost estimating, or a glossary (refers out to CMHC's separate Glossary of Housing Terms).

**Most useful for:** sizing and spacing every platform-frame wood member from the appendix span tables (floor joists and beams, wall studs, lintels, ridge beams, roof rafters and roof joists, ceiling joists, deck beams and joists); footing, foundation wall and slab dimensions; stair and guard geometry; roof and wall sheathing thickness and nailing; and the overall construction sequence and member layout for a typical one- to three-storey house.

## Chapter 1, Important general information

Advantages of wood-frame construction (fast, durable, renewable, insulator, adaptable to Canadian wind/snow/seismic ranges). Fire safety is mostly a code and occupant-behaviour matter; secondary suites need extra sound and smoke separation (12.7 mm drywall both sides, interconnected alarms). Room height, radon mitigation (roughed-in capped pipe under the slab, air barrier at the slab-wall joint), energy efficiency direction from the NBC, material compatibility (galvanic corrosion, sealant selection) and construction safety are covered in short subsections. "Protection against moisture and termites" gives the ground-clearance and termite-zone numbers captured above; this is a load-bearing detail for any modelled foundation-to-framing transition.

## Chapter 2, Planning, design and construction

Mostly process (permits, inspections, site drainage planning) with one useful part for modelling: "Stages of construction" (Fig. 4, p. 20) gives the canonical build order for a typical house, about 16 weeks for an average house: building layout, excavation and footings, foundations/drainage/backfill, framing (including roofing and flashing, about 2 weeks), doors and windows, plumbing/heating/electrical rough-in, insulation/air barrier/vapour barrier, exterior finishes, interior finishes, paint/cabinets/fixtures, landscaping. Each subsection under "Planning and Design" (framing, floor framing, wall framing, roof framing, exterior finishes, and so on) is a one-paragraph pointer to the detailed chapter and is not itself dimensional.

## Chapter 3, Concrete

Minimum strengths and air entrainment are in the quick reference. Table 2 (p. 264) gives mix proportions by volume for the three water-cement ratios (0.70 walls/footings, 0.65 floors, 0.45 garage floors/exterior steps). Placement: lifts not over 1.2 m, free-fall not over 2.4 m, chute slope 1:2 to 1:3, temperature kept 10 to 25 C while placing. Curing: forms stay on at least 3 days, further curing 1 to 3 more days depending on temperature; slabs cured at least 5 days at 21 C or 7 days at 10-21 C.

## Chapter 4, Lumber and other wood products

Nominal versus actual dimension explained (2x4 nominal is 38 x 89 mm actual). Grade stamps identify agency, species group, grade, moisture content (S-GRN over 19 per cent, S-DRY or KD at or under 19 per cent) and mill. Select Structural, No. 1 and No. 2 grades explained; No. 2-and-better is the typical structural category (Table 7, p. 267; species combinations Table 10, p. 271; grade-mark facsimiles Table 9, p. 269). Engineered wood products: wood I-joists (LVL or lumber flanges, OSB web), glulam, LVL, PSL, LSL and OSL, used as beams, headers, columns, joists. Panel products: plywood (6.0-18.5 mm common), OSB (structurally equivalent to plywood, made with exterior glue), waferboard (largely obsolete), fibreboard, particleboard, MDF, hardboard.

## Chapter 5, Functions of the building envelope: water, air, vapour and heat control

The longest non-framing chapter (27 pages); it sets the assembly build-up rules that every wall/roof/floor section in later chapters assumes.

- Water penetration control: every wall needs a first plane of protection (cladding) and a second plane (sheathing membrane or insulating sheathing); a rainscreen air space (capillary break) is required in high-moisture-index regions, formed by a 10 mm (7/16 in.) furred gap, a mesh at least 10 mm thick and 80 per cent open, or specific siding profiles; furring must not cover more than 20 per cent of the wall area.
- Basement floor waterproofing needs two 75 mm slabs sandwiching a membrane. Roofs 1:6 slope or steeper get sheathing paper; under 1:6 need a fully waterproof membrane.
- Air leakage control: the air barrier system must be continuous from the basement floor through walls to the roof, commonly 0.15 mm (6 mil) polyethylene on the warm side of the insulation, taped/sealed at joints, penetrations and floor junctions. Key details illustrated: wrapping the rim joist with vapour-permeable, air-impermeable spun-bonded polyolefin (Fig. 7-8), polyethylene tabs at partition top plates (Fig. 12-13), sealed window/door perimeters (Fig. 9-11).
- Vapour diffusion control: vapour barriers (commonly the same polyethylene) sit on the warm side of insulation, must be continuous but need not be sealed (unlike the air barrier). Exception: up to one-third of a wall's total RSI-value may sit inside the vapour barrier (double-wall construction).
- Heat flow control: insulation amounts follow NBC Part 9.36 by heating-degree-day climate zone (Tables 11-13, pp. 272-274); insulation must overlap by 4 times the offset where planes do not align.
- Typical insulated wall is 38 x 140 mm (2x6) studs with wood sheathing and batts (Fig. 20). Fig. 18-22 show batt-in-stud-space, double-wall, and exterior-rigid-insulation approaches, including a masonry veneer wall (Fig. 21, brick tie at every other stud, 25 mm air space) and an insulated-sheathing wall over 2x4 studs (Fig. 22, bracing note at corners).
- Basement wall exterior insulation must reach the footing top if the footing is under 0.6 m (2 ft.) deep (Fig. 15); interior insulation over concrete is commonly 25 mm expanded polystyrene plus batts (Fig. 16). ICF walls (Fig. 17) are "flat" (uniform concrete thickness) with exposed polystyrene needing a fire-rated cover.
- Raised-heel roof trusses need to be at least as deep as the wall insulation requires (about 250 mm/10 in. modern, versus about 150 mm/6 in. pre-1970s); loose-fill baffles (Fig. 24) protect eave vents.
- Cathedral/joist-type roofs need a vent space above the insulation, minimum 89 mm (3 1/2 in.) recommended for lumber joists (Fig. 25) or 63 mm (2 1/2 in.) minimum for I-joists with web holes (Fig. 26); low-slope roofs can carry insulation above the deck instead (Fig. 27, protected by roofing membrane and ballast).

## Chapter 6, Location and excavation

Setback and utility checks before staking. Corner stakes plus offset markings support batter boards (Fig. 28) after excavation; squaring is checked by equal diagonals or by 300/400/500 mm multiples (3-4-5 triangle in metric). Excavation width, back-slope, headroom-driven depth and finish-grade slope numbers are in the quick reference (Fig. 29-30).

## Chapter 7, Footings, foundations and slabs

The core dimensional chapter for substructure geometry.

- Footings: below frost line or otherwise frost-protected; rest on undisturbed soil, rock or compacted granular fill; project at least 100 mm (4 in.) beyond the wall each side; thickness not less than the projection and never under 100 mm (Fig. 31, Table 3 depths p. 264, Table 4 sizes p. 265). A polyethylene capillary break sits under the wall on top of the footing. Wood (preserved) footings are an alternative to concrete, sized per the Canadian Wood Council's Permanent Wood Foundations guide.
- Column footings (Fig. 32-33): 0.4 m2 for one storey, 0.75 m2 for two storeys on average soil, doubled if the water table is within one footing-width of the bearing surface.
- Stepped footings (Fig. 34-35) for sloped sites: vertical step under 600 mm, horizontal run at least 600 mm (400 mm max step in sand/gravel); the vertical riser between steps is poured concrete at least 150 mm thick, same width as the footing.
- Foundations: four common types, cast-in-place concrete, concrete block, ICF, preserved wood. Formwork (Fig. 37) uses form ties and wood spacer blocks or lumber forms; basement window/door rough openings and beam pockets (Fig. 38-39) are built into the formwork; wood beams framed in need 12 mm (1/2 in.) air clearance if untreated and at or below grade.
- Anchor bolts (Fig. 40): 12.7 mm minimum, 2.4 m o.c. standard, tighter spacing (12.7 mm at 1.7 m, or 15.9 mm at 2.4 m) for high-exposure multi-storey buildings, first bolt within 0.5 m of each end, minimum two per braced wall panel.
- Floor-wall isolation joints (Fig. 41) and vertical control joints (Fig. 42, bevelled strips totalling about one-fifth the wall thickness) control shrinkage cracking; control joints required over 25 m of wall, at intervals not exceeding 15 m.
- ICF walls (Fig. 43): typical finished concrete thickness 140-240 mm (5 1/2-9 1/2 in.) for up to two floors and 3 m storey height, one 10M bar within 300 mm of the top plus horizontal bars at 600 mm max, vertical bars per engineering, 30 mm min cover.
- Concrete block walls: modular block sizes and mortar-joint thickness in the quick reference; sash/pier/universal blocks (Fig. 44) frame openings; walls parged at least 6 mm (1/4 in.) and dampproofed with bituminous coating (Fig. 45); pilasters capped with 200 mm solid masonry.
- Preserved wood foundations (Fig. 46-47) use pressure-treated studs, plates and plywood over a granular drainage layer, with a polyethylene ground cover and dampproof strip under the bottom plate; floor options are slab-on-grade, sleepers on a drainage layer, or a suspended wood floor.
- Slabs: basement floor slabs at least 75 mm thick over 100 mm of crushed rock and 0.15 mm polyethylene (dampproofing/radon barrier), sloped to a drain; control joints per Fig. 48 at 4.5-6.0 m spacing, one-quarter slab depth; radon mitigation via a gas-permeable layer plus vent pipe/outlet, or a 100 mm granular layer plus a capped 100 mm pipe.
- Slabs-on-ground (Fig. 49) need finished floor at least 150 mm above finished grade, perimeter rigid insulation protected by parging or board, 9.5 mm (10M) rebar at 600 mm o.c. both ways or 152 mm welded wire mesh.
- Dampproofing versus waterproofing: dampproofing (bituminous coating) is standard; waterproofing (two layers of bitumen-saturated felt, or a sandwiched membrane between two 75 mm slab layers) is required only where hydrostatic pressure is likely.
- Foundation drainage: perimeter drain pipe (Fig. 50) at or below the basement floor level, sloped to outlet, covered with at least 150 mm of clean gravel wrapped in filter fabric; wall drainage layer (mineral fibre at least 19 mm/57 kg/m3, or free-draining backfill).
- Backfilling only after first-floor framing is anchored and subfloor installed (lateral support); backfill within 600 mm of the wall must be free-draining granular material, placed and compacted in lifts.
- Crawl spaces: footings sized as for basement walls; ground cover required (concrete, asphalt, or 0.15 mm polyethylene/Type S roll roofing with 100 mm min laps, 300 mm laps if heated and covered by 50 mm concrete).
- Deck/step/garage foundations: frost-depth rule applies over 600 mm (24 in.) above ground or more than three risers; garage foundation walls at least 150 mm thick, 150 mm above grade, sill anchors at 2.4 m o.c. with two bolts minimum per sill piece.

## Chapter 8, Framing the house

Platform framing (floor built first, walls raised per storey, most common) versus balloon framing (studs continuous past floor lines, needs added fire blocking, less vertical shrinkage, sometimes used for one intermediate wall in an otherwise platform house to route ducts). Advanced framing techniques (optimum value engineering) align roof/floor members over studs to allow single top plates and skip cripples, cutting framing lumber 15-20 per cent. Lateral load resistance has three NBC risk categories: low-to-moderate (671/630 of 680 locations for wind/seismic, standard sheathing/cladding suffices), high (19 wind, 33 seismic locations, needs "braced wall panels" per NBC 9.23), extreme (1 wind, 3 seismic locations, requires full engineering).

## Chapter 9, Floor framing

- Sill plates bolted to the foundation (levelled on a gasket, foam, or mortar bed); rim joists and floor joists toe-nailed to the sill.
- Columns and beams: wood columns at least 140 x 140 mm, spaced 2.4-3.0 m; built-up wood beams (Fig. 52) nailed with 89 mm nails at 450 mm max spacing per row, joints within 150 mm of the quarter points or over a post, not in end spans, not more than half the plies at one location; steel W-beams and glulam/LVL/PSL are alternatives (Tables 15-19, pp. 276-283).
- Beam-joist installation: joists on top of the beam lap up to 300 mm (Fig. 53); joists flush with the beam use hangers or ledger strips, 38 x 64 mm nailed with two 82 mm nails per joist (Fig. 54), or a 38 x 38 mm ledger bolted to a steel beam web with splices leaving 12 mm clearance for shrinkage (Fig. 55).
- Foundation wall-floor connection: sill-plate method (Fig. 56-58, standard, sill at least 150 mm above grade or separated by a moisture barrier) versus the rarely used joist-embedded method (Fig. 59-60, floor framing set into wet concrete, at least two-thirds of joist depth embedded).
- Floor joists: dimension lumber usually 38 mm thick, 140/184/235/286 mm deep (Tables 20-21, pp. 285-286); spacing 400 mm common, 300 mm for heavy loads or shallow depth, up to 600 mm for deep joists.
- Wood I-joists (Fig. 61-62) need rim framing, blocking and squash blocks to route point and wall loads around their thin webs; manufacturer details govern hole sizing.
- Load-bearing partitions parallel to joists need a beam or wall below; perpendicular load-bearing partitions must be within 900 mm of a joist support (unsupported by a floor) or 600 mm (supporting a floor). Non-load-bearing partitions bear on 38 x 89 mm blocking at 1.2 m o.c.
- Floor openings (Fig. 64) use doubled headers/trimmers per the size triggers in the quick reference. Bridging/blocking/strapping controls joist twist and vibration; intermediate restraint at intervals not exceeding 2.1 m.
- Subfloor: plywood/OSB/T&G lumber, minimum thicknesses Table 22 (p. 287); nailed 150 mm o.c. edges, 300 mm intermediate (Table 23, p. 287); lumber subfloor at right angles or 45 degrees diagonal to joists, boards up to 184 mm wide.
- Floor projections (bay windows, Fig. 65): cantilever limited to 400 mm for 2x8 joists or 600 mm for larger joists unless engineered for additional load; insulate and air/vapour-barrier the cantilever soffit and header.
- Worked examples (Fig. 66-67) walk through Table 15 (beams) and Table 20 (joists) selection for a stated span, spacing and grade.

## Chapter 10, Wall framing

- Studs (38x89 or 38x140 mm, 400 mm o.c. common, range 300-600 mm per Table 25 p. 290) sit on a bottom plate and carry a doubled top plate (single plate allowed only if trusses/rafters land within 50 mm of the studs); top plate laps at corners and intersections, or ties with a 0.91 mm galvanized plate.
- Lintels (Fig. 68) are at least two plies of 38 mm lumber with a rigid-insulation spacer to match wall thickness, sized by opening width and load (Tables 26-27, pp. 291-293); openings under 3 m need one jack stud per side, 3 m or more need doubled jack studs.
- Platform framing: walls built flat on the deck, sheathed before raising, temporarily braced, then tied at corners; polyethylene air barrier strips are placed under interior partition top plates before erection.
- Multiple-stud posts (Fig. 69-70, at least three studs or equivalent) at exterior corners and partition intersections.
- Braced wall panels sit inside 1.2 m wide "braced wall bands" continuous floor to floor (Fig. 73), offset no more than 1.2 m, spaced at most 15 m apart or forming a perimeter band; basements/crawl spaces can rely on the concrete perimeter instead.
- Balloon framing (Fig. 74): studs and first-floor joists both land on the sill; upper floors bear on a ribbon board (at least 19x89 mm) let into the studs; fire blocking is mandatory at every floor and ceiling line since there is no plate to break the cavity.
- Highly insulated walls: double-wall framing (Fig. 19) or exterior rigid insulation add depth without thermal bridging.
- Structural insulated panels (SIPs, Fig. 75) sandwich a polystyrene core between OSB/plywood skins, tongue-and-groove sealed joints, negligible thermal bridging.
- Worked example (Fig. 76) walks Table 25 for a stated wall load and height.

## Chapter 11, Ceiling and roof framing

Covers site-built and prefabricated pitched roofs (gable, hip, valley, dormer, gable-end projections), low-slope roofs and roof space ventilation, with worked sizing examples. Roof slope is always expressed rise first: the imperial convention fixes run at 12 (x/12); the ratio convention puts 1 as the smaller side of the pair (1:5 for slopes under 45 degrees, 5:1 above). Pitched roofs range 1:6 to 1:1 or steeper.

Prefabricated trusses (most common): raised-heel trusses give a deeper insulation zone at the wall; standard shapes are king-post, Howe, Fink/W, mono-pitch, parallel chord, scissor and mansard (Fig. 79). Erected at 600 mm o.c., gable end first, temporarily braced (Fig. 80) then permanently braced with lateral top-chord bracing, web bracing and diagonal bracing repeated about every 6 m (Fig. 81); trusses under 6 m span can be lifted by hand.

Site-framed roofs (Fig. 82-88): ceiling joists span the short direction at 400/600 mm o.c., installed before rafters, lapped over a centre bearing wall; where a hip rafter crowds the end wall, a doubled joist plus stub/tail joists keep the spacing consistent (Fig. 85). Common rafters run from plate to ridge board in opposing pairs (or offset by at most their own thickness); a bird's-mouth seats the heel over the wall plate. Ridge support: slopes under 1:3 need a vertical-support ridge beam (38x140 mm on 38x89 mm struts at 1.2 m o.c., or a bearing wall); slopes 1:3 and over can use a ridge board with collar ties (38x89 mm, braced if over 2.4 m) or roof struts at 45 degrees or steeper bearing on an interior wall. Hip and valley rafters run about 50 mm deeper than common/jack rafters so the jack-rafter seat cuts land flush. Gable-end overhangs under 300 mm use a rake rafter and nailing strip; over 300 mm use lookout rafters at 600 mm o.c., length at least twice the overhang, with a doubled rafter where lookouts project more than 1.5 rafter spaces (Fig. 89-90). Low-slope "roof joists" double as ceiling joists, sized for combined roof and ceiling load (Tables 29-30), minimum drainage slope 1:50.

Roof ventilation: baffles keep at least 25 mm (with baffle) or 63 mm (without) clear above the insulation at the eave; vent area at least 1/300 of ceiling area for slope 1:6 or steeper (1/150 below that), at least 25 per cent of the vent area at the eaves and 25 per cent high; attic hatch at least 0.32 m2, no dimension under 500 mm.

## Chapter 12, Roof sheathing and coverings

Panel roof sheathing (plywood or OSB): face grain across framing, joints staggered, 2-3 mm gap, edge nails 150 mm o.c./field 300 mm o.c., thickness per Table 35 (p. 305) depending on support spacing and edge support (H-clips or 38x38 mm blocking). Lumber board sheathing: 19 mm nominal (17 mm if supports 400 mm or closer), boards up to 286 mm wide, laid closed (edge to edge) or, for wood-shingle roofs, spaced at the shingle exposure. A 50 mm clearance from interior masonry chimneys (12 mm for exterior masonry) is required, and sheathing must fit tight at hips and valleys. Eave protection membrane runs at least 900 mm up-slope to at least 300 mm past the interior wall face, unless the roof is 1:1.5 or steeper, over an unheated space, or the climate is under 3,500 heating-degree-days. Roof coverings: asphalt shingles (min 1:6 low-slope application or 1:3 normal, 130/145 mm exposure, 4 nails/strip), wood shingles (min 1:4, double first course, 6 mm gap, joints offset 40 mm, two nails at exposure+40mm), shakes (min 1:3, 190/250 mm exposure by length, felt interlayment), built-up roofing (3+ felt plies plus gravel/cap sheet, cant strip and counter-flashing at walls), sheet metal (min 1:4, 762-914 mm wide, 0.33-0.48 mm thick by material), and heavier concrete/clay tile needing engineered structure. Slope limits per covering are tabulated in Table 36 (p. 306).

## Chapter 13, Wall sheathing and exterior finishes

- Sheathing types and thicknesses (plywood, OSB, gypsum, fibreboard, insulating boards, lumber) are tabulated in Table 28 (p. 294); minimum thickness depends on stud spacing (400 vs 600 mm).
- Panels applied vertically before wall erection, 2-3 mm gaps, nails at 150/300 mm; longer 2.74 m panels or an added filler piece let sheathing cover the header/sill area to tie framing to the sill plate.
- Sheathing membrane types: building paper, spun-bonded polyolefin (housewrap, 2.74 m wide rolls), self-adhesive membranes at sills/heads, liquid-applied membranes for EIFS.
- Cladding: metal/vinyl siding factory-interlocking, laps staggered over 600 mm; hardboard and lumber siding (bevel, tongue-and-groove, drop, board-and-batten, board-on-board patterns) with specific thickness, lap and nailing rules per pattern (see the source for exact figures per siding type); plywood/hardboard panels 6-11 mm depending on spacing and grain orientation; fibre cement board siding.
- Wood shingles/shakes (three grades, 400/450/600 mm lengths); stucco (three coats over mesh, Table 39 mix p. 307, rainscreen air space at least 10 mm in high-exposure areas); masonry veneer (air space, ties, weep holes, flashing per the quick reference); EIFS (insulation board, reinforced base coat, finish coat, needs a compatible substrate barrier).
- Rainscreen furring strips are required in wet/coastal climates behind most claddings, with a 10 mm drainage gap.

## Chapter 14, Flashing

Explains the physical forces flashings must defeat (gravity, surface tension in gaps under 9 mm, capillary action in openings under 5 mm, wind-driven kinetic energy, pressure differentials) and the standard flashing types by location: base flashing (roof-wall or roof-penetration turn-ups), counter flashing (over base flashing, sheds water and protects it from UV), through-wall flashing (directs cavity water out, with weep holes), valley flashing (open valley needs 600 mm wide metal or double roll-roofing layers, closed valley uses one metal layer under continuous shingle courses), stepped/shingled base flashing at chimneys and walls (100 mm minimum shingle overlap onto flashing, cricket/saddle required where a chimney exceeds 750 mm wide), cap flashing at parapets, dampproof flashing under copings, and drip flashing at material transitions and under windows/doors (required where the head-to-overhang vertical distance exceeds one-quarter of the overhang's horizontal projection). Performance requirements (water barrier, movement capability, durability, compatibility, buildability, maintainability) are discussed conceptually rather than dimensionally.

## Chapter 15, Windows, exterior doors and skylights

Governed by the Harmonized NAFS standard (airtightness, watertightness, wind load, forced-entry ratings). Egress window and main door sizes are in the quick reference. Window types: fixed, single/double-hung, horizontal/vertical slider, casement, awning, tilt-and-turn, each with different open-area fractions and airtightness. Performance improvements: multiple glazing layers, low-E coatings, argon/gas fills, low-conductivity edge spacers, thermally broken or low-conductivity frames (aluminum needs a thermal break; vinyl, wood and fibreglass are inherently low-conductivity). Installation sequence (Fig. 130) builds up sub-sill slope, water-impermeable sill membrane, corner reinforcement, then the window with shims, head flashing, and lapped sheathing membrane above and below; rough opening should be 25 mm larger than the window frame, 12.5 mm clearance per side (Fig. 132). Doors: pre-hung manufactured units are standard; garage-to-house doors need a gas-tight, self-closing, weatherstripped seal. Skylights need the same air-barrier continuity as roof penetrations, with stepped base flashing (Fig. 135) matched to the roof slope.

## Chapter 16, Exterior trim and millwork

Eave projection styles: narrow (rake rafter, nailing strip), wide with horizontal soffit, wide with sloped soffit (Fig. 136). Plywood soffit (where used) is 6 mm sanded plywood, nailed 150 mm o.c. edges/300 mm intermediate, with a 19x19 mm strip along the wall supporting blocking. Gable/eave intersection trim has three standard treatments (Fig. 137). Window and door trim is covered by cross-reference to Chapter 15's air-sealing and flashing requirements (Fig. 138).

## Chapter 17, Stairs

The core numbers (rise/run, tread depth, width, headroom, flight height, stringer sizing, third-stringer trigger, handrail and guard heights and spacing) are captured in the quick reference; this is one of the most geometrically load-bearing chapters in the book for any procedural stair generator. Additional detail: winders form either 30 degrees (three treads per 90-degree turn) or 45 degrees (two treads), only one winder set permitted between floor levels; a comfortable riser height for the initial rise/run calculation is 184 mm (7 1/4 in.), used to compute the whole-number riser count by dividing total rise by this value and rounding up. Stringers may be cut/open (Fig. 142-143, mitred riser edges, tread nosing returned along the stringer face) or ploughed/housed (wedged and glued treads and risers into a routed profile). Basement stairs commonly use open risers; exterior concrete stairs need 32 MPa air-entrained concrete and follow the same riser/tread numbers as interior stairs. Ramps (for barrier-free access) need a clear width of at least 870 mm, slope not steeper than 1:12, level areas top and bottom and every 9 m of run or direction change.

## Chapter 18, Chimneys, flues and fireplaces

Chimney/flue height and clearance numbers are in the quick reference. Masonry chimneys (Fig. 145): outer wall at least 75 mm thick solid masonry; flue liner starts at least 200 mm below the lowest connection and extends 50-100 mm above the cap; multiple flues separated by at least 75 mm solid masonry (90 mm if fire brick); flue area rule of thumb is at least one-tenth the fireplace opening area, never less than 200x200 mm. Factory-built flues need no foundation, use insulated stainless-steel liners with lateral clip support. Masonry fireplace proportions (Fig. 146): fire-chamber depth at least 300 mm from face to back, firebrick lining at least 50 mm thick, back/sides at least 190 mm thick with a firebrick liner (or 90 mm solid / 190 mm hollow masonry with a steel liner and air-circulation chamber); front hearth at least 100 mm thick reinforced concrete, extending 400 mm in front and 200 mm each side of the opening; smoke-chamber sides slope at most 45 degrees from vertical. Factory-built and natural gas fireplaces (Fig. 147) are lighter alternatives with sealed combustion options.

## Chapter 19, Plumbing, electrical and appliances

The notching and drilling limits (joists, studs, top plates, roof trusses cannot be notched or drilled at all) are captured in the quick reference and matter directly to any modelling agent checking whether a routed service conflicts with structure (Fig. 148-150, 159). Plumbing roughing-in happens after framing is complete and before wall finish; typical fixture layouts for kitchen/bathroom stacks, washbasins, toilets and vent stacks are shown in Fig. 151-156 (soil stack commonly framed in a 140 mm/6 in. stud wall where it continues to upper floors; headers frame around horizontal stack runs through joists, Fig. 156). Wiring: roughing-in (boxes, cable runs) precedes insulation and finish; typical service entrance equipment (100-200 amp) and drilling limits for wiring are shown in Fig. 157-159 (protection plates required where holes are drilled closer than 30 mm/1 1/4 in. to a stud edge). Switch height about 1.4 m (4 ft. 6 in.) above floor, or 1.2 m (4 ft.) for wheelchair accessibility. Smoke alarms required near every bedroom on every storey, hard-wired with no disconnect switch; interconnection is mandatory only where a secondary suite is present.

## Chapter 20, Space conditioning systems

Mostly mechanical-system description (forced-air, electric baseboard, hydronic/radiant, air conditioning, natural and mechanical ventilation, HRV/ERV heat exchangers) rather than framing geometry, but a few framing-relevant numbers: supply duct diameters typically 100/125/150 mm; return-air stud/joist cavities lined with sheet metal within 600 mm of the furnace; combustion air intake kept at least 900 mm from other vents; lintels required over cut studs for large return grilles, following the same lintel framing as a door opening. Ductwork, plenums and combustion venting all need planned framing openings to preserve air/vapour barrier continuity, ideally roughed in during framing rather than cut in afterward.

## Chapter 21, Interior wall and ceiling finishes

Gypsum board is the dominant interior finish; thickness, nailing/screw spacing and joint-taping sequence (three compound coats, widening bands from 125 to 300+ mm) are in the quick reference and this chapter's body. Where gypsum board provides braced-wall-panel bracing in high-wind/seismic areas, nail/screw spacing tightens to 200/300 mm. Wall tile over moisture-resistant gypsum board in wet areas; other finishes (plywood, MDF wainscoting, insulating fibreboard ceiling tiles) get brief mentions.

## Chapter 22, Floor coverings

Sub-floor and underlay rules by finish type: wood strip flooring at least 19 mm thick can skip underlay if perpendicular to joists at up to 400 mm spacing (otherwise underlay required); laminate, engineered, parquet, resilient flooring and tile generally need a panel underlay over unsupported lumber subfloor edges. Nailing/underlay attachment specifics are in Tables 41-42 (pp. 308-309). Ceramic/porcelain/granite/marble tile is set in a mortar bed (about 30 mm) or adhesive over a deflection-limited base; shower bases need a waterproof liner connected to the drain.

## Chapter 23, Interior doors, frames and trim

Interior door sizes and hinge/hardware spacing are in the quick reference (Fig. 168-171 show frame/jamb/doorstop assembly and hardware placement). Trim: casing set 5-6 mm from the jamb edge, base moulding one-piece or two-piece (baseboard plus shoe mould), mitred exterior corners and coped interior corners. Millwork: kitchen cabinet dimensions are in the quick reference (Fig. 173); minimum 750 mm clearance above cooktop burners (reducible to 600 mm with a protective hood). Closets use rod-and-shelf or manufactured storage units; exterior-wall closets are discouraged (condensation risk).

## Chapter 24, Coating finishes

A materials-chemistry chapter (solvent, pigment, resin composition of paint, varnish, stain, lacquer; alkyd versus latex properties) with no framing geometry. Worth knowing it exists for finish-schedule notes and the general rule that coatings need 10 C or higher application temperature and a dry, clean, dust-free substrate.

## Chapter 25, Eavestroughs and downspouts

Not required by the NBC but recommended; metal or plastic troughs mounted on the fascia with a slight slope, spikes at about 750 mm o.c.; downspouts fastened with at least two straps per 3 m length, discharged through a splash block or storm connection.

## Chapter 26, Decks, porches and balconies

Load basis and member sizing tables are in the quick reference and Tables 43-44 (pp. 310-312). Fig. 175 details a complete deck section: post on a concrete pier (below frost line if the deck is over 600 mm above grade or has more than three risers), post-to-beam and beam-to-joist connections, guard/handrail/baluster assembly, and the 100 mm max baluster gap. ## Chapter 27, Garages and carports

Attached garages need a continuous air barrier, vapour barrier and insulation in the shared wall/ceiling assembly (Fig. 177), plus a weatherstripped, self-closing door and CO alarms in bedrooms sharing a wall or abutting attic with the garage (within 5 m along hallways). Garage clear dimensions and carport pier sizes are in the quick reference; carport is defined as more than 60 per cent open perimeter.

## Chapter 28, Surface drainage, driveways and walkways

Site grading directs water away from the foundation via swales where slopes reverse; driveway and walkway width, slope and thickness numbers are in the quick reference. Concrete driveway control joints at 3-3.5 m intervals with 6-12 mm premoulded filler; walkway control joints at about 1.5 times the walkway width.

## Chapter 29, Maintenance

One page. General guidance: foundation-area settling should be corrected with fill to restore drainage slope; provide new owners a maintenance manual, especially for mechanical systems. No new dimensional content.

## Rules for modelling

The book is a reference and design guide organized by construction stage, not a single procedural build script, so these are lookup rules keyed to the chapter and table that governs each decision, in the order a platform-framed house goes up (per the Chapter 2 stages-of-construction sequence).

1. Site and excavation: set back from the property line per local zoning (2 m/6 ft. 7 in. triggers extra restrictions on openings, Ch. 1); excavate 600-700 mm wider than the footprint; back-slope past 1.2 m depth (Ch. 6).
2. Footings: size from Table 3 (depth) and Table 4 (wall footings) or the column-footing rule of thumb (0.4/0.75 m2 for one/two storeys); step per the 600 mm vertical/horizontal rule on slopes (Ch. 7).
3. Foundation walls: pick a system (cast-in-place, block, ICF, preserved wood) and thickness from Table 5; place anchor bolts at 2.4 m o.c. (tighter in high wind/seismic exposure, Ch. 7-8); add control joints beyond 25 m of wall length.
4. Slab or crawl space: 75 mm minimum basement slab over 100 mm gravel and a polyethylene barrier, sloped to a drain; or a ground-covered, ventilated/insulated crawl space (Ch. 7).
5. Floor platform: sill plate on the foundation, rim joist and floor joists per Table 20/21 by species, grade, spacing and load case; built-up beams per Tables 15-19 on columns spaced 2.4-3.0 m; subfloor thickness per Table 22, nailed 150/300 mm (Ch. 9).
6. Exterior and load-bearing walls: studs per Table 25 by supported-load case; doubled top plate; lintels over openings per Tables 26-27, jack-stud doubling past 3 m spans; braced wall panels within 1.2 m bands if in a high-exposure zone (Ch. 10).
7. Ceiling and roof framing: choose trussed or site-built; size ceiling joists (Table 33-34), roof rafters (Tables 31-32) or roof joists (Tables 29-30) by species, spacing and snow load; ridge support method by slope (beam under 1:3, ties/struts at 1:3 or over); hip/valley rafters 50 mm deeper than common rafters; gable overhang framing by the under/over 300 mm rule (Ch. 11).
8. Roof sheathing and covering: panel or board sheathing per Table 35, nailed per the standard edge/field pattern; eave protection membrane per the 900/300 mm rule; covering per Table 36 slope limits (Ch. 12).
9. Wall sheathing and cladding: sheathing thickness per Table 28; membrane and, in wet climates, a furred rainscreen air space; cladding type per Chapter 13's per-material rules; flashing at every discontinuity per Chapter 14's location list.
10. Openings: windows/doors sized and flashed per Chapter 15's sequence; egress and forced-entry minimums from the quick reference; trim per Chapter 16.
11. Interior systems that constrain framing: keep notches/holes within the Chapter 19 limits when routing plumbing and wiring; leave duct chases per Chapter 20; place stairs per Chapter 17's rise/run/headroom/guard numbers.
12. Finishes (gypsum board, flooring, doors/trim, coatings, eavestroughs) follow in the Chapter 2 sequence and rarely affect the structural geometry already fixed by steps 1-9; use Chapters 21-25 only for surface build-up thickness when it matters (for example gypsum board thickness against a stud space).
13. Site-adjacent structures (decks, garages, driveways) each have their own sizing tables (Table 43-44 for decks; Chapter 27 clearances for garages; Chapter 28 slopes for paving) and can be modelled as attached sub-assemblies once the house shell is fixed.
14. When a table does not cover the species, grade, spacing or load case needed, the source itself says to fall back to NBC Part 4 or the Canadian Wood Council's Engineering Guide for Wood-Frame Construction 2009; do not extrapolate a table.
