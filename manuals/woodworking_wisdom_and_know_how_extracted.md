**Source:** Woodworking Wisdom and Know-How: Everything You Need to Know to Design, Build, and Create, from the editors of Fine Woodworking magazine, compiled by Josh Leventhal. Black Dog and Leventhal Publishers, New York, 2014. Distributed by Workman Publishing Company. Originally published and copyrighted by The Taunton Press (publisher of Fine Woodworking) and used by permission. ISBN-13 978-1-57912-981-1, eISBN 978-1-60376-412-4. 1891 pages as paginated by pdftotext and the PDF reader (this count includes the cover, contents, and back-of-book index).

**Scope of source:** A single-volume anthology of roughly 150 short magazine articles from Fine Woodworking, reorganized into six parts: wood identification and lumber buying, the workshop and its tools, shaping and joining wood, finishing, furniture projects, and period-style projects. It covers hand and power tool technique, joinery methods, wood movement and moisture, finishing recipes, and about 45 furniture, box, and cabinet projects with cutting diagrams. It is a furniture-making book: there is no timber framing, no structural engineering, no span tables, no fastener schedules, and nothing about house or building construction. Species data is limited to what a cabinetmaker needs (color, grain, working properties, shrinkage), not a full wood-technology reference. Project dimensions appear in the prose and in figures the text extraction cannot fully capture; where a fraction or a drawing dimension did not survive extraction it is marked "not stated in the source" here rather than guessed, and the page is given so the modelling agent can open the original figure.

**Most useful for:** wood movement and moisture-driven clearances (breadboard ends, frame-and-panel construction, door and drawer gaps); joinery geometry and comparative strength (mortise and tenon, dovetails, half-lap, bridle, biscuit, dowel, miter); lumber sizing conventions (rough-sawn quarter system, board footage, hardwood grading); and furniture proportions and member sizes for boxes, tables, chairs, beds, cabinets, and period-style (18th century, Shaker, Arts and Crafts) pieces.

## The Wood (part 1, pp. 13 to 124)

### Types and species of wood (p.13)

A run of single-species identification articles (Jon Arno and others), each covering a wood's color, figure, working properties, seasoning behavior, and typical furniture uses. No structural or span data anywhere in the chapter; this is species selection and hand-tool working-property guidance for cabinetmaking, not a wood-technology handbook.

- Cherry (p.13). Native black cherry, Prunus serotina, a "nurse tree" that colonizes cleared land and grows under constantly shifting light, producing wood with variable color and gum pockets from board to board even within one shipment; its extractives are photosensitive and darken with light exposure over years rather than fading, which is why it was favored by 18th-century cabinetmakers and by the Shakers.
- Maple (p.18). Hard (sugar) and soft maple grades and figure types, including birds-eye, curly, and spalted maple.
- Walnut (p.21). Black walnut, specific gravity about 0.51, and its close botanical relative butternut; walnut's extractives include juglone, part of the tree's own natural chemical defense.
- Red, white, and live oak (p.25).
- Pine (p.34).
- Mahogany, covering five commercial varieties (p.38); a later sidebar in the grain chapter describes mahogany's plum-pudding figure and its ribbon-stripe pattern from interlocked grain (p.97).
- Ebony (p.41).
- High-quality, low-cost woods (p.44); overlooked West Coast species (p.48); species suited to outdoor furniture (p.53).
- Plywood for Woodworkers (p.58, grades, core types, veneer core versus MDF core) and Handling Plywood (p.61, moving and cutting large sheets without tearout or breakage).
- Fine Furniture from Reclaimed Wood (p.63, sourcing and using reclaimed lumber).

### Shopping for lumber (p.74)

Lumberyard Basics (p.74) explains rough-sawn thickness measured in quarters (4/4, 5/4, 8/4, and so on, each quarter equal to 1/4 in), the difference between rough and surfaced stock, the board-foot unit of volume, and the National Hardwood Lumber Association grading system (FAS, F1F, Selects, No. 1 Common, No. 2A Common), with the minimum board size and percentage of clear face required for each grade (see quick reference). It also names common types of warp (bow, crook, cup, twist) and shows that cutting a warped board into shorter or narrower pieces reduces how much material must be planed away to flatten it. Smart Shopping for Lumber (p.81) is a process article: draw the piece before buying, make a parts list (not just a cutlist) noting finished thickness, width, and length plus the rough thickness needed, and shop by picking boards for grain match, favoring riftsawn stock for legs so the grain runs straight on all four faces.

### Grain, figure, moisture, and movement (p.86)

A Guide to Wood Grain (p.86) and Understanding and Using Figured Wood (p.90) describe flatsawn versus quartersawn grain orientation and named figure types: ray fleck, spalting, stump or butt figure, bird's eye, blister and quilt figures, and ribbon stripe from interlocked grain (stripes tighter than 1/4 in apart are called pencil stripe, p.97).

Water and Wood, by R. Bruce Hoadley (p.97), is the source's central wood-science article.

- Defines free water (in the cell cavity), bound water (in the cell wall), and the fiber saturation point, about 30% moisture content, the threshold below which moisture loss actually shrinks the wood.
- Gives a table of average green moisture content by species and by heartwood versus sapwood (p.98): examples include white ash 46% heartwood, 44% sapwood; American beech 55%/72%; black walnut 90%/73%; Douglas fir 37%/115% (sapwood exceeding 100%, meaning it is more than half water by weight).
- Plots an equilibrium-moisture-content curve against relative humidity, 0 to 100% (p.100), and a seasonal moisture-content swing for unfinished thin veneer, finished kiln-dried furniture, and finished air-dried furniture (p.101).
- Gives a shrinkage table (tangential, radial, and their ratio) for 14 hardwoods and 8 softwoods, green to oven-dry (p.101 to 103, reproduced in the quick reference above), and the shrinkage-calculation formula: change in dimension equals original dimension times the species' shrinkage percentage times (change in moisture content divided by the fiber saturation point, about 30%) (p.104).
- Describes the oven-dry method for measuring moisture content directly: weigh a sample, dry it at 212 to 221 F to constant weight, moisture content equals lost weight divided by oven-dry weight; and a worked example where a cellar workshop's spruce test wafers came back at 21% moisture content, prompting a dehumidifier that brought the shop's equilibrium moisture content down to about 9% within a few weeks.
- Ranks finishes for moisture-exchange control: linseed oil is among the least effective seals; lacquer is better; urea-alkyd or urethane varnishes give the best protection against moisture uptake; aluminum paint, paraffin, or a commercial end-sealing compound are recommended for sealing the end grain of drying lumber.

Drying Wood (p.105) covers air-drying and kiln-drying practice, sealing end grain, and the risk of drying too fast. Understanding Wood Movement, by Christian Becksvoort (p.110), is the most dimensionally useful article in the book: it works through actual clearances and tolerances that a modelling agent can apply directly.

- Grain orientation predicts movement: flatsawn boards (growth rings roughly parallel to the face) move the most and are prone to cupping; quartersawn boards (rings roughly perpendicular to the face) move far less (p.111).
- Slab construction (a chest with grain running in a band around the whole box) lets the whole piece change height seasonally while width and depth stay fixed; frame-and-panel construction isolates movement to a floating panel inside a stable frame (p.111 to 112).
- Breadboard ends: the tongue is cut out to within 1/4 to 1/2 in of the shoulder for strength; a slight concave bow (about 1/16 in) is planed into the breadboard before fitting; a 3/8-in-dia hole is drilled at center and pinned, with outer holes elongated using a 3/8-in rat-tail file, away from center for dry wood (6% MC or less) and toward center for wet wood (12% MC or more) (p.111).
- Cross-grain glued mortise and tenon joints (as in an apron-to-leg or rail-to-post joint) are safe up to about 5 in of tenon width in cherry if only the top half of the joint is glued, letting the bottom half slip slightly as the rail moves (p.111).
- A slab headboard 12 to 14 in wide can move up to 1/4 in seasonally; a pinned and glued mortise needs 1/8 in of space top and bottom to absorb that; a loose pencil-post headboard instead sits in an oversized mortise held by the bed rail bolts, needing no glue at all (p.113 to 114).
- Tabletops are attached with shopmade wood buttons riding in a 1/4-in-wide groove located 1/2 in below the inside top of the rail; for rails thicker than 3/4 in, countersunk 1/2-in-dia holes and 1/4-in through-holes are drilled from below and elongated away from center with a rat-tail file (p.115, 118).
- A frame-and-panel case back keeps its outer frame members (glued into rabbets) quartersawn and no wider than 1 3/4 in in cherry, so the frame has enough give to absorb movement without breaking out of the rabbet (p.116).
- Frame-and-panel door and drawer gaps in quartersawn cherry: hinge-side gap stays constant year-round; lock-side gap runs between 3/64 in (a dime's thickness) and 5/64 in (a nickel's thickness); the top gap is set to a dime fit and the bottom to a nickel fit; drawer side clearance runs 1/64 in minimum to 1/16 in maximum total, with the drawer front planed about 1/32 in narrower top-to-bottom than the sides (p.117).
- Dovetailed keys let applied side molding move with the case: the key sits 0.003 to 0.005 in thinner than the slot depth, in five or six segments fixed with countersunk screws and a single brad each, chiseled free at each pencil mark once installed (p.117).
- Web frames (drawer runners in a slab-sided case) are dovetailed into the case sides at front and back, with the runner length set to the case width minus the depth of the two dovetail sockets, minus 1/16 in for dry wood or 3/8 in for damp wood (p.118).

## The Workshop (part 2, pp. 125 to 490)

### Hand tools (p.125)

Buying-guide and technique articles on handsaws, handplanes, card scrapers, chisels, hammers and mallets, and sharpening. Essential Handsaws (p.139) names four saws worth owning and their recommended tooth count: a dovetail saw at about 19 tpi sharpened for ripping, a crosscut backsaw at 12 to 14 tpi for parts up to about 1 in thick and 3 to 4 in wide, a dozuki at about 20 tpi crosscut for flush-cutting pegs, a coping saw with a fine-cut blade for sawing dovetail waste, and, as an extra, a panel saw at 8 to 12 tpi for breaking down long boards. Essential Tools for Furniture Makers (p.138) recommends a No. 4 bench plane as the size to start with if machines do the rough milling. Essential Chisels covers bench chisels sharpened at 25 degrees versus the 30-degree bevel used on dedicated mortise chisels (also detailed in the Joinery chapter, p.645). Sharpening Planes and Chisels and Keeping Tools Sharp cover primary and secondary bevel honing on progressively finer stones. No general structural dimensions; this is tool selection and sharpening technique.

### Power tools (p.210)

Technique articles on the tablesaw (ripping, crosscutting, cutting joinery), bandsaw, miter saw, three router types, and drill press fundamentals. Ten Essential Router Bits (p.258) gives a basic 10-bit starter kit (straight bits in two widths, a spiral-fluted straight bit, a four-bearing rabbeting bit, a dovetail bit, a roundover bit, a cove bit, a 45-degree chamfer bit, a three-wing slot cutter, and a flush-trimming bit), mostly on 1/4-in shanks, plus shopmade jigs for straight edge-jointing and right-angle dado cuts with a handheld router. Useful mainly for understanding which machine can cut the joint geometry described later in the Joinery chapter.

### Other tools and accessories (p.277)

## Working with and Shaping Wood (part 3, pp. 494 to 947)

### Joinery (p.613)

The chapter a modelling agent should read closely.

- Joinery Strength: A Comparison (p.613). A controlled test: 18 joint types cut from 3/4-in by 2 1/2-in by 8-in cherry stock, glued with Titebond III, loaded to failure in a diagonal-compression rig simulating racking force. Results and typical failure modes are in the quick reference table above.
  - The half-lap and bridle joints (both with large long-grain glue surfaces, clamped across their faces) outperformed the classic mortise and tenon; both failed only by the wood shearing across its fibers, not at the glueline.
  - Thicker tenons were consistently stronger than thinner ones: the 3/8-in tenon was almost twice the strength of the 1/4-in tenon; adding pins or wedges slightly reduced peak strength but is retained for insurance against long-term glueline fatigue.
  - The splined miter (No. 3 of 18) and even the plain miter (No. 6 of 18) outperformed expectations, but an unreinforced miter still relies entirely on its glueline and will eventually open at the outside corner as the wood moves.
  - Store-bought systems (pocket screw, Domino, biscuit, Beadlock, Dowelmax) all placed in the middle of the ranking; the weakest joints were cope-and-stick and stub tenon, both still adequate for production cabinet doors when the panel is glued in.
- Basic Joinery with Dadoes and Rabbets (p.627). States the dado-depth rule of thumb, about one-third of the stock's thickness, inside a small shop-storage project; also covers stopped dadoes cut with a router and a rub-collar template.
- Master the Miter Joint (p.635). Miter geometry and reinforcement options (splines, keys).
- Quick Mortises with a Drill and Chisels (p.644). A drill-and-chisel mortising method: drill most of the waste on a drill press or with a doweling jig, then square the ends with a rectangular mortising chisel (parallel sides, not tapered) in 1/4, 3/8, or 1/2-in widths, sharpened to 30 degrees versus a bench chisel's 25, because the steeper bevel resists fracturing when levered.
- Fitting Tenons with Handplanes (p.653). Sets a 0.002-in blade-projection tolerance (about the thickness of a sheet of paper) for shoulder and rabbet-block planes trimming tenon cheeks and shoulders to a piston fit; recommends buying a large shoulder plane first, a rabbet block plane second.
- Through Mortise-and-Tenon Joinery (p.657). Drilling and chiseling through-mortises from the show face inward (an "outside-in" sequence that keeps tearout on the hidden face), cutting matching tenons with about 1/32 in of extra length for trimming flush after assembly, and wedging the through-tenon with the tablesaw blade tilted about 3 degrees to cut the wedges, wedges always bearing against the mortise's end grain so the workpiece cannot split.
- Mortise-and-Tenon Joints for Curved Work (p.663). Three techniques for joining a curved member: leaving or routing a flat landing spot on the curve (used for chair posts and curved table legs, flat extended about 1/16 in beyond the rail on each side to allow for movement); leaving a raised pad where the curve flows through the joint, refined only after glue-up (as on a Sam Maloof rocker's leg-to-rocker joint); and scribing the tenon shoulder to match a concave curve, with the tenon trimmed to about 1/32 in less than the mortise depth once shoulders are pared. All three recommend quartersawn stock for the rail to minimize cross-grain movement at the curved joint.
- Pegging Mortise-and-Tenon Joints (p.673). Peg sizing 3/16 to 3/8 in diameter typical (smaller for delicate boxes, larger for a trestle base); peg placement kept clear of the tenon's edges to avoid splitting; peg holes drilled about 1/32 in undersized in softer stock so the primary wood grips; drawboring offsets the tenon hole 1/32 to 1/16 in toward the shoulder (harder wood gets less offset) so driving the peg pulls the joint tight without clamps.
- Laying Out Dovetails (p.684). The classic slope ratio, 1:6 for softwoods and 1:8 for hardwoods (softwood fibers compress more easily and need the steeper angle to draw tight); half-pins at the outer edges with interior tails sized larger than the pins between them, both for mechanical strength and for appearance; baseline scribed to the exact thickness of the mating board so no pins stand proud after glue-up. A worked example lays out four tails with 3/16-in pins, tail spacing found by dividing the half-pin-to-half-pin span by the tail count and walking a pair of dividers across the board end.
- Later articles cover cutting dovetails on the tablesaw, mitered (blind) dovetails, biscuit joinery (biscuit sizes No. 0, 10, and 20; slot position 3/16 in to 1 in from the reference face, never closer or the biscuit's swelling bulges the surface; a 4-in-dia blade cuts the slot, p.710 to 717), dowel joinery (dowel diameter about half the workpiece thickness for a structural joint, 3/8-in dia by 2-in long a common size; dowels for panel glue-up alignment only need be 1/4-in dia, p.718 to 726), the rule joint for drop-leaf tables (p.727), gluing and clamping technique, and a troubleshooting piece on joinery mistakes.

### Bending (p.760)

- The Basics of Steam-Bending, by Lon Schleining (p.760). Working rule: steam the blank about 1 hour per inch of thickness, in a steambox held at 200 to 212 F with a pressure-release vent so the box cannot overshoot 212 F; if the box will not reach 200 F within about 20 minutes, the boiler is undersized for it. Long-grained woods (hickory, ash, oak) bend easiest; dense woods (cherry, hard maple, most exotics) are harder; straight grain matters more than green versus kiln-dried stock, and the author bends kiln-dried stock successfully. Bending forms must be cut to a tighter radius than the finished part to allow for springback, which depends on radius, thickness, degree of bend, bending speed, steaming time, and species.
- Bend and Twist with Free-Form Steam-Bending (p.767). Bending blanks are cut oversized, roughly an extra 1/4 in in thickness and width and about 4 in of extra length. After steaming (1 hour per inch of thickness), the blank is bent around a tight traditional form, straightened, flipped face for face, and bent again, to loosen lignin bonds throughout the section before it is bent free-form without a compression strap; the whole sequence must happen within 5 to 10 minutes of leaving the steambox or the blank needs 5 to 10 more minutes of reheating.
- Curved Panels Made Easy (p.772). Vacuum-bag laminated curved panels built from a core, edge banding, and show veneer, using the bending forms to laminate and square the core.
- Tapered Laminations Made Easy (p.786). The key structural-lamination rule: a 1-in-thick laminated part bent to a 12-in radius should use about 10 plies, tapered individually before gluing rather than sawn after lamination (sawing through gluelines shows as ugly lines and risks "cold creep," where too few gluelines let the part slowly straighten over time). Minimum ply thickness at the tapered end is 3/16 in; plies are cut about 1/4 in wider than the finished part and about 1 in longer at each end for cleanup.

### Turning wood and making curves (p.795)

Spindle-turning and faceplate-turning technique for furniture parts (legs, finials, pulls), smoothing curved parts by hand, and cutting curves on the bandsaw. No structural sizing data beyond individual project dimensions covered in the Chairs and Beds and Boxes chapters (turned pens, candlesticks).

### Carving (p.847)

### Veneer, marquetry, and inlay (p.875)

Through-mortises are routed into the posts before the posts are shaped, using a shopmade template and guide bushing, in shallow passes from both faces for thick stock. The stretcher's through-tenon is cut on the bandsaw and fitted by hand until it slides through with no gap on the show face; the tenon's wedge mortise is drilled and chopped at an 8-degree angle, its inner wall buried in the post so it need not be squared, and the wedge mortise is started about a set distance inside the post face so the wedge cannot bottom out. ## Projects for Traditional Styles (part 6, pp. 1727 to 1851)

### 18th century designs (p.1727)

Three period-reproduction projects, each with commentary on the proportions and joinery characteristic of the style. Full cutting dimensions are mostly in the accompanying drawings rather than in extractable running text, but the Classic Corner Chair gives one clear stock-sizing example.

- Classic Corner Chair, by W. Mickey Calahan (p.1727). A roundabout chair built with simple mortise-and-tenon joinery and cabriole legs, no compound angles. Leg patterns are transferred onto 16/4 stock machined to 3 in square, left long enough for two knee blocks per leg; the front leg is oriented for a bull's-eye grain pattern on the exposed knee, the other legs for straight, vertical grain. All mortises and arm-rail tenons are laid out and cut while the leg blank is still square, before the cabriole shape is bandsawn.
- Porringer-Top Tea Table and Regal Queen Anne Lowboy: cabriole-leg case and table pieces with pad feet and applied moldings, in the same period style.

### Shaker designs (p.1761)

Elements of Shaker Style, by Chris Becksvoort (p.1761), is a proportion-and-detail reference rather than a single project, most useful for period-correct member sizing and profiles.

- Door frames and panels: early raised, fielded panels gave way over time to flat panels, with a "pillow" panel (planed on all four edges into the frame groove, with only a barely noticeable field) as a transitional form; frames usually carry a quarter-round thumbnail profile on the inside edge rather than a square shoulder (p.1763).
- Drawers: both flush and lipped types were built; lipped drawers carried the quarter-round or thumbnail profile on the top and two sides only, never the bottom edge, which was considered too fragile (p.1764).
- Crown and base moldings are treated as functional, not purely decorative: a rounded base molding or bracket resists splintering near the floor where brooms, mops, and boots strike it (p.1765 to 1766).
- Turned knobs follow a mushroom form; sizes range from small spindle-turned drawer knobs up to about 1 1/2 in dia to large face-turned built-in knobs up to about 2 1/2 in dia, the larger ones screwed on from inside rather than glued on a tenon (p.1767).
- Tabletop edges are often square or only slightly eased; drop-leaf tables use the rule joint (p.1767).
- Legs are most often a double-tapered square leg, tapered only on the two inside faces for a wider, sturdier stance; straight-turned tapered legs (common on drop-leaf tables) are often splayed a few degrees off perpendicular so they do not look "pigeon-toed"; swell-tapered legs narrow under the shoulder then swell to maximum diameter one-quarter to one-half of the way down; the shoulder transition to a turned leg is cut either square at 90 degrees or, more commonly, at 45 degrees for a rounded shoulder (p.1768).
- Two dated antique pieces are given with full dimensions: a cherry cupboard, Canterbury NH, circa 1850 to 1900, 80 in by 44 in by 19 in (p.1763), and a cherry single drop-leaf desk from the same shop and period, top 14 1/2 in by 30 in, leaf 12 in, carcase 12 1/2 in by 20 1/2 in (p.1766).

The chapter continues with two build projects, a Shaker dining table and a Shaker chest of drawers.

### Arts and Crafts designs (p.1792)

Elements of Arts and Crafts Style, by Graham Blackburn (p.1792), surveys the defining features of the style, also called Mission, Craftsman, Crafts, Cloister, or Quaint: exposed, straightforward joinery, quartersawn white oak as the signature timber (chosen for its ray-fleck figure), and an emphasis on visible structure over applied ornament, foursquare proportions rather than curved or carved forms. Three named build projects follow: a Craftsman-style Morris chair, a Limbert-style coffee table, and a Stickley-style cabinet, closing with a leaded-glass cabinet-door project (copper-foil or lead-came technique, soldering the joints, and cementing the glazing with a blackened whiting or plaster-of-paris compound, then burnishing with sawdust to clean and darken the lead came).

## Rules for modelling

This is a reference and technique compilation, not a procedural build guide, so the following are rules to apply rather than a fixed build sequence.

1. Treat lumber sizes as nominal versus actual: 4/4 rough stock finishes at less than 1 in; a stated S2S thickness is 3/16 to 1/4 in less than the rough-sawn figure it is based on (p.75). Model finished member sizes, not nominal ones, unless the task specifically calls for rough stock.
2. When sizing any solid-wood panel, apron, or slab that spans across the grain, budget movement using the shrinkage table and formula on p.101 to 104: expect roughly 4% radial and 8% tangential dimensional change from a fully dry to a fully green state, worse for oak, beech, and hickory, better for cherry, walnut, mahogany, and teak.
3. Isolate cross-grain movement with frame-and-panel construction wherever a wide flat member would otherwise be glued rigidly across its width; keep frame members quartersawn and under about 1 3/4 in wide if a rigid case back must also resist wood movement (p.116).
4. For any tabletop, breadboard end, or slab lid modelled as fixed to a base, leave clearance gaps and elongated fastener holes as in the quick reference (grooves, slots, and pin holes 1/32 to 1/4 in oversized in the direction of anticipated movement) rather than modelling a fully rigid attachment.
5. For frame joints (face frames, doors, chair and table frames), prefer half-lap or bridle geometry where maximum joint strength matters and the joint can be exposed; use mortise and tenon with a tenon at least 3/8 in thick (roughly one-third to one-half the stock thickness) where a hidden joint is required; avoid modelling a stub tenon or unreinforced miter as a load-bearing corner.
6. For dovetail geometry, use a slope of 1:6 for softwood parts and 1:8 for hardwood parts, half-pins at the outer edges, and interior tails visually larger than the pins between them.
7. For dado and rabbet joints, cut the dado to about one-third of the mating board's thickness.
8. For steam-bent or laminated curved members, size the bend around either a steam time of about 1 hour per inch of thickness (solid steam-bent stock) or a laminated ply count where a 1-in-thick member on a 12-in radius uses about 10 plies, each at least 3/16 in thick at its thinnest point.
9. For pegged mortise-and-tenon joints, size pegs at 3/16 to 3/8 in diameter depending on the scale of the piece, and keep peg centerlines clear of the tenon's edges to avoid splitting.
10. For drawer fronts, apply one pull under 14 in of width and two pulls spaced 18 to 22 in apart over 18 in of width, with drawer side clearance of 1/64 to 1/16 in total.
11. When a project needs a wedged, knockdown joint (a trestle base, a bench, a stretcher through a post), model the wedge mortise at about 8 degrees off the tenon's long axis, with the wedge mortise's inner wall buried inside the receiving member so only the outer end needs a clean, angled cut.
12. Size shop-fixture and jig members (benches, sawhorses, dado jigs) from the two worked examples in the source rather than inventing values: a working bench height around 35 in, a benchtop three layers of 1-in stock, dog holes on 4-in centers starting 5 in from the vise.
13. There is no timber-framing, span, or building-scale content in this source; do not use it for structural member sizing beyond individual furniture and shop-fixture parts, and do not infer nailing schedules, house-frame spacing, or code-driven dimensions from it.
