# Advanced Framing Construction Guide: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/APA-Advanced-Framing-Construction-Guide.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://www.apawood.org/guides-tools-training/technical-document-library/construction-guides/advanced-framing/ and save it under that name.

**Source:** *Advanced Framing Construction Guide*, Form No. M400A. APA, The Engineered Wood Association, Tacoma, Washington. 2014, revised September 2014. No ISBN. 24 pages as paginated in the PDF (page 1 cover, page 24 back cover; the PDF page index matches the printed page number throughout). Consultant credited: Tony Mainsbridge, advanced framing consultant. Note that apawood.org currently distributes a newer edition of the same guide, Form M400 (no "A" suffix), revised April 2016, under the same title; the copy in this repository is the September 2014 edition.

**Scope of source:** A short design guide to "advanced framing", also called optimum value engineering (OVE), for wood light frame residential construction under the 2012 International Residential Code (IRC), International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) and the American Wood Council's Wood Frame Construction Manual (WFCM). It covers 24 inch on center stud spacing, two-stud and insulated three-stud corners, interior to exterior wall intersection framing, single top plates and the vertical member alignment ("stack framing") they require, single-ply and wood structural panel box headers, minimal jack stud and cripple stud use, blocking, and continuous wood structural panel wall sheathing for bracing and siding attachment. It does not give full span tables for joists, rafters or headers (it refers the reader to the IRC and WFCM tables for those), does not cover foundation design, does not give a complete nailing schedule beyond the one wind pressure sheathing table it reproduces, does not cover engineered design for high wind or seismic regions (explicitly out of scope, referred to the International Building Code), and does not cover mass timber, CLT, or non-wood systems.

**Most useful for:** sizing and spacing platform-frame wall studs, corners, headers and top plates for an "advanced framing" (24 inch on center, single top plate) wood house; laying out interior to exterior wall intersections and rough openings so header and jack stud counts match the source's rules; wall sheathing and bracing geometry for wind resistance.

## Chapter by chapter notes

### 1. Front matter (pp. 1 to 3)

Cover, a one-page sustainability sidebar ("Wood, the Natural Choice", replanting statistics, a CORRIM life cycle study reference, an APA grade-stamp illustration) and the table of contents. None of it carries framing geometry.

### 2. Components of advanced framing (p. 4)

A labelled cutaway drawing of a two-story house identifies the six features the rest of the guide explains: single top plates (p. 17), single headers (p. 13), insulated three-stud corners or two-stud corners with ladder blocking (p. 10), in-line or stacked framing (p. 18), 2x6 studs at 24 in o.c. instead of 2x4 at 16 in o.c. (p. 9), continuous plywood or OSB wall sheathing (p. 18), and jack studs and cripples used only where required around openings (p. 15). Use this page as the index to the rest of the document when only one technique is needed.

### 3. Advanced framing defined (p. 5)

Advanced framing, also called optimum value engineering (OVE), was developed by the NAHB Research Center in the 1970s to reduce redundant framing while keeping structural performance. Conventional framing is defined here as 2x4 or 2x6 at 16 in o.c., double top plates, three-stud corners, multiple jack studs, double or triple headers, and unnecessary cripple studs. The most commonly adopted single advanced framing change is 2x6 studs at 24 in o.c.; other techniques (single top plates, two-stud corners, single headers, minimal jack studs and cripples) can be adopted independently or together, with the largest savings when applied holistically. The source recommends confirming 24 inch framing is accepted by the local building official before design.

### 4. Advantages of advanced framing (pp. 5 to 7)

Energy efficiency (p. 5 to 6): Figure 1 compares whole wall R-value across four wall systems meeting R20 (see quick reference table). Deeper 2x6 cavities at 24 in o.c. hold more insulation than 2x4 at 16 in o.c., and fewer framing members reduce thermal bridging and voids at corners and intersections. The ENERGY STAR Thermal Enclosure System Rater Checklist (version 3, revision 5, section 4.4.5, "Reduced Thermal Bridging") requires, in addition to the stud spacing table in quick reference: insulated corners (R6 minimum), insulated headers (R3 for 2x4, R5 for 2x6), window and door framing limited to one pair of king studs, one pair of jack studs and cripples only as needed for on-center spacing, and interior-exterior wall intersections insulated to the same R-value as the rest of the wall.

Cost effectiveness: the table of contents lists this as a subsection at page 6, but no distinct heading appears in the printed body text; its content is not separable from the energy efficiency discussion above (this looks like an editorial omission in the source, not a missing page).

Structural integrity (p. 7): aligning vertical framing members under roof trusses or rafters creates a direct load path (cross-referenced to Figure 13, p. 19) that transfers compression and tension loads straight through the studs, giving a stronger structure with fewer stressed members. Continuous wood structural panel sheathing is code-recognized as wall bracing up to 24 in stud spacing, while most other bracing methods (let-in bracing, some panel products) are limited to 16 in o.c. Continuously sheathed walls also serve as a nailbase for siding fastened between studs (for example vinyl siding needing 16 in fastener spacing) even at 24 in stud spacing, and support fiber-cement products up to 11 psf.

Sustainability (p. 7): frames the guide's techniques against the National Green Building Standard (ICC 700-2008) and LEED for Homes. ICC 700 section 601.2 awards 3 points per qualifying technique exceeding 80 percent usage in the building, up to 9 points maximum for full floor, wall and roof framing.

### 5. Incorporating advanced framing techniques (p. 8)

A four-step adoption sequence for builders converting gradually:
1. Switch to 2x6 studs to increase cavity depth and meet R20 (most important in colder zones).
2. Where structural code allows, move the wall framing module from 16 in to 24 in o.c.; keep double top plates at this stage to avoid triggering in-line/stack alignment requirements.
3. Add intersecting wall techniques (three-stud corners, ladder junctions) and energy-efficient headers with limited opening framing.
4. Eliminate double top plates last, since this needs full vertical alignment (including 24 in o.c. floor and roof framing) and non-standard stud lengths that may be hard to source. The source notes many builders never take this last step and keep double top plates.

The source stresses documenting whichever techniques are chosen on the architectural plans, and extra site supervision if the framing crew is new to these methods.

### 6. Floor framing (pp. 8 to 9)

Two rules for optimum resource efficiency: specify engineered wood floor joists (I-joists, structural composite lumber, or glulam) at 24 in o.c., and maximize member spans between supports. Engineered joists at 24 in o.c. cut joist count by about 30 percent versus conventional 16 in spacing, though deeper or larger joists may be needed and floor vibration serviceability should be checked (source points to APA Form E710 for that). I-joist depth ranges let plumbing, electrical and mechanical services run inside the floor cavity without dropped ceilings. Thicker floor sheathing is recommended at 24 in o.c. spacing to keep floor stiffness comparable to 16 in o.c. floors; the extra sheathing cost is offset by the lower framing cost.

### 7. Wall framing (pp. 9 to 16)

This is the geometric core of the guide.

Overview and Figure 2 (p. 9): a wall frame comparison drawing contrasts, in one elevation, a single top plate, wood structural panel box or single-ply header, single studs at the sides of an opening, and optional ladder blocking (all advanced framing) against redundant cripple studs and a three-stud/California corner (conventional or advanced/conventional shared). Conventional framing is again defined as 2x4/2x6 at 16 in o.c., double top plates, three-stud corners, multiple jack studs, double or triple headers, redundant cripples.

Stud spacing (p. 10): conventional 16 in o.c., advanced typically 24 in o.c.; fits standard sheathing, insulation and finish material modules.

Corners (p. 10, Fig. 3, Fig. 4): the 2012 IRC (Figure R602.3(2)) permits omitting the third corner stud or partition backing studs where wood backup cleats, drywall clips, or other approved devices back the facing material. Figure 3 shows the insulated three-stud corner ("California corner") from outside and inside, next to a conventional three-stud corner with an isolated, hard-to-insulate cavity, and an alternate three-stud corner using drywall clips. Figure 4 shows two-stud corners: an outside corner with 2x ladder blocking at 24 in o.c. or drywall clips, a two-stud corner using drywall clips, and a two-stud inside corner for wall panelization where the sheathing (plywood or OSB, per the siding manufacturer's ES report) is installed by the panel fabricator for siding attachment.

Interior wall intersections (p. 11, Fig. 5): the ladder junction method uses 2x blocking at 24 in o.c. vertical spacing, needing under 6 ft of blocking material in a typical 8 ft wall (versus 16 ft of stud lumber plus blocking for a conventional intersection with a stud each side). Install blocking with the wide face vertical for maximum backing and maximum insulation depth. A second detail shows a junction built for continuous drywall application, minimizing drywall joints; both details reference a 3 in x 6 in x 0.036 in galvanized steel plate at the top plate joint (see Figure 6). Drywall clips can replace ladder blocking; drywall is held by the adjacent wall's drywall, not fastened to the clip. At least one ladder block at mid-wall height is recommended to keep the adjacent stud straight.

Prescriptive and alternate connection details (p. 11 to 12, Fig. 6): where metal plates are not used for single top plate connections, framing cutoffs (sized to also support ceiling drywall) can substitute. Longitudinal top plate splice: prescriptive is a 3 in x 12 in x 0.036 in galvanized steel plate at the splice with (12) 8d (2-1/2 in x 0.113 in) nails each side (per 2012 IRC Table R602.3(1), Item 14, exceeding its minimum); alternate is a 2x scab across the splice joint with (8) 16d (3-1/2 in x 0.135 in) nails each side, no splitting of scab or plate permitted. Intersecting wall connection: prescriptive is a 3 in x 6 in x 0.036 in galvanized steel plate with (6) 8d (2-1/2 in x 0.113 in) nails each side (2012 IRC Section R602.3.2); alternate is a 2x6 (or 2x4/2x6) lumber splice with (2) 10d (3 in x 0.128 in) nails each side (2012 IRC Table R602.3(1), Item 19).

Headers (p. 13, Fig. 7, Fig. 8): single-ply headers give the most cavity insulation space of the header types shown. Single-ply lumber headers are sized per 2012 IRC Table R602.7.1; single-ply engineered wood headers are sized from tributary live and total load in pounds per linear foot against a published span table such as the AWC's WFCM. Figure 7 shows: a single-ply load-bearing header flush with the outer stud face, with cavity insulation space above the header to stud depth less header thickness, and a header bottom plate completing the rough opening (single studs at the sides may be adequate for many one-story buildings, per IRC Table R502.5(1)); a large-opening single header using a 3-1/8 in or 3-1/2 in glulam or multi-ply SCL/lumber with jack studs as required; and an approved framing connector option (header hanger or equivalent) usable with single- or double-ply headers, with a single stud at the sides of rough openings up to 48 in wide. Figure 8 shows openings in non-load-bearing walls: no header is required, a single opening top plate spans the opening (doubled only if wider than 8 ft), with cripple studs as required between the opening top plate and the wall top plate; these cripples can be omitted if the header-to-top-plate gap is under 24 in, unless needed for vertical seams in the wall finish.

Wood structural panel box headers (p. 14, Fig. 9): site-built load-bearing headers per 2012 IRC Figure R602.7.2 and Table R602.7.2, typically 15/32 in Performance Category sheathing or thicker over minimum 2x4 framing, panel installed on one side (usually the exterior, to avoid impairing interior finishes) or both sides. Header height is 9 in or 15 in overall; a 15-inch one-sided header spans 4 ft wide openings on homes up to 28 ft wide, or 3 ft or narrower openings on homes up to 32 ft wide, for single-story clear-span truss roofs or two-story construction with floor and roof on interior bearing walls. Openings up to 4 ft need only a single stud each side (no jack stud); jack studs are required for spans over 48 in. A 15 in overall header height allows 6 ft 8 in doors at 8 ft ceilings. Top and bottom plates of the box header must be continuous with no vertical joints in the panel over the opening; nail pattern shown at 3 in, 3 in, 3 in spacing along the header top.

Openings (p. 15 to 16, Fig. 10): opening placement and the framing layout above it drive header size. Figure 10 compares three 36-inch openings with 24 in, 24 in and 48 in wide tributary loads respectively, the narrowest tributary load being the "best placement"; the rough opening layout module is 24 in layout module + 3/4 in + (rough opening width / 2) for 23 in to 46-1/2 in wide openings, with continuous bearing from a load-bearing sill beam or foundation wall. Jack studs may be omitted where a single jack stud is prescribed by 2012 IRC Table R502.5(1)/(2), or where an approved connector (header hanger) ties the header to the full-height stud, or where wood structural panel headers are used (Figure 9 note). The number of full-height studs at each header end is not in the 2012 IRC itself but is in the 2012 WFCM: section 3.4.1.4.2 requires at least half the number of studs displaced by the opening (Table 3.23C), with an exception (Table 3.23D) permitting a single full-height stud at each end for openings up to 48 in wide at 24 in stud spacing, in design wind areas up to 150 mph (3 second gust), subject to stud length and grade.

Blocking (p. 16): minimized except where structurally needed (for example at braced wall panel joints) or to back fixtures/finish materials. 2x horizontal blocking between studs can be installed edge-out or wide-face-out against the sheathing/drywall; wide-face vertical gives maximum backing and minimal intrusion into the insulatable cavity.

Metal hardware (p. 16): straps and anchors resist uplift and lateral wind/seismic loads but create thermal bridges (metal conducts heat hundreds of times faster than wood). Where framing is vertically aligned for a direct load path (Figure 13), studs above the top plate should be strapped or anchored to the studs below; using continuous wood structural panel sheathing for this connection instead of metal connectors reduces thermal bridging and hardware cost.

### 8. Single top plates (pp. 17 to 18)

Single top plate construction requires all members above the plate to be stack framed (vertically aligned with the members below), with a maximum alignment offset tolerance of 1 in (Figure 11), applying to roof trusses, rafters and second-story floor joists. There is no offset limit for members bearing on double top plates with 2x6 at 24 in o.c. Ceiling and roof framing (p. 17 to 18, Fig. 12): spacing ceiling joists and rafters 24 in o.c. improves material efficiency; where attic storage is limited and no mechanical equipment is needed, ceiling joists over conditioned space can go to 24 in o.c. per IRC Table R802.4(1). Uplift resistance can come from anchoring trusses to the top plate via continuous wood structural panel sheathing nailing, or via metal connectors at the truss's raised heel provided the top plate itself is anchored to the members below through continuous sheathing (Figure 12, energy heel truss to wall detail, showing the sheathing lapped from wall to under the truss heel with an optional rafter-tie or tension strap). The 2012 IECC section 402.2.1 allows a reduced prescriptive attic insulation depth where an energy (raised) heel truss lets uncompressed insulation extend over the top plate: uncompressed R30 is deemed to satisfy R38 requirements, and uncompressed R38 satisfies R49, needing roughly 10 in and 12 in of insulation depth respectively.

Single top plates and framing member layout (p. 18): because stack framing needs one master layout for every framing level, the source recommends starting the layout from the roof framing (dictated by roof geometry) and working down, reversing the usual foundation-up sequence. Hip roofs typically lay out from the nominal centerline intersection of the hip(s) with the ridge; gable roofs typically lay out from one end wall. Truss roofs are stacked directly above the wall studs with no member offset.

### 9. Wall sheathing (pp. 18 to 20)

Widening stud spacing from 16 to 24 in o.c. increases the potential for panel buckling between studs, especially with jobsite moisture exposure; not a structural issue but a serviceability one, mitigated by 1/8 in panel end and edge gaps and moisture management (source points to APA Form X480).

Wind resistance and wall assemblies (p. 18 to 20, Fig. 13, Table 1): buildings must resist lateral racking forces, uplift forces separating roof from wall from floor from foundation, and inward/outward pressures on cladding and sheathing, per 2012 IRC Section R301.2.1. Figure 13 (stack framing and 24 in o.c. member spacing) is a full wall section axonometric showing, top to bottom: roof sheathing (omitted for clarity), energy heel roof trusses at 24 in o.c., single top plate, "stacked" framing members creating the direct load path, wall sheathing, 2x6 studs at 24 in o.c. supporting one floor, roof and ceiling (height per code), APA Sturd-I-Floor at minimum 24 in o.c., APA Rim Board, and APA wood I-joists or SCL at 24 in o.c. Table 1 (2012 IRC Table R602.3(3)) gives nail size, penetration, panel span rating, panel thickness, stud spacing, nail spacing and maximum wind speed by exposure category for wood structural panel wall sheathing resisting wind pressure (values in the quick reference table above); footnotes: panel strength axis parallel or perpendicular to supports, but three-ply plywood at more than 16 in stud spacing must run its strength axis perpendicular to supports; Wall-16/Wall-24 span-rated panels or 16/24 o.c. rated plywood siding may substitute for 24/0 or 24/16 rated panels respectively, with Wall-16 and 16 o.c. siding limited to 16 in stud spacing.

Wall bracing (p. 20): continuous wood structural panel sheathing installed per 2012 IRC Section R602.10 gives the most flexibility in meeting prescriptive bracing requirements (bracing method CS-WSP), and also minimizes metal strap use for uplift, further cutting thermal bridging and hardware cost. Rigid foam and other non-structural sheathing lack shear/racking strength and need extra bracing. Let-in bracing (LIB) and similar methods are limited to 16 in o.c. stud spacing or less, making them incompatible with 24 in o.c. advanced framing.

## Rules for modelling

This is a reference and design guide, not a build sequence, so the numbered items below are rules to apply when generating an advanced-framed wood wall or floor assembly, not construction steps.

1. Default stud size and spacing for an "advanced framed" wall: 2x6 (38 x 140 mm nominal) at 24 in (610 mm) on center. Conventional comparison case: 2x4 or 2x6 at 16 in (406 mm) on center.
2. Use a single top plate only if every member above it (studs, trusses, rafters, second-story floor joists) is vertically aligned with the stud below, within a 1 in (25 mm) offset tolerance. If that alignment cannot be guaranteed, model a double top plate instead, with no offset restriction.
3. When single top plates are used, build the framing layout top-down: lay out the roof framing first (hip roofs from the ridge/hip intersection, gable roofs from an end wall, trusses stacked directly over studs with zero offset), then propagate that grid down through the wall studs to the floor joists below.
4. Corners: model either an insulated three-stud (California) corner, or a two-stud corner with 2x ladder blocking at 24 in o.c. (or drywall clips) closing the back of the corner. Do not model a plain three-stud corner with a sealed inner cavity as the default; that is the conventional case the guide is contrasting against.
5. Interior-to-exterior wall intersections: model a ladder junction, 2x blocking between the intersecting wall's end stud and the wall behind it at 24 in o.c. vertical spacing, with at least one block at mid-wall height. Do not place a full stud on each side of the intersection (that is the conventional configuration).
6. Openings in load-bearing walls: use single-ply headers by default, sized to the actual tributary load (span table or engineering, not fixed here). Use a single jack stud at each side unless the tributary width or code table requires more; jack studs may be omitted entirely where an approved header-hanger connects the header to a full-height stud, or where a wood structural panel box header is used, for openings up to 48 in (1220 mm) wide.
7. Openings in non-load-bearing walls: no header. Model a single opening top plate over the rough opening (doubled only if the opening is wider than 8 ft / 2440 mm), with cripple studs between it and the wall top plate, omitted if that gap is under 24 in (610 mm).
8. Wood structural panel box header option: overall height 9 in or 15 in, 15/32 in (12 mm) or thicker structural panel on one or both faces, continuous top and bottom plates with no vertical panel joints over the opening, single stud at each side up to 48 in span, jack stud required beyond that.
9. Position rough openings on the 24 in layout module where possible: opening center offset = 24 in module + 3/4 in + (rough opening width / 2), valid for 23 in to 46.5 in wide openings, to minimize tributary load and wasted jack/cripple studs.
10. Blocking: only place it where structurally required (braced wall panel joints, fixture backing); orient horizontal blocking wide-face-out against the sheathing for maximum finish backing and minimum intrusion into the insulated cavity.
11. Wall sheathing: model continuous plywood or OSB sheathing over the whole wall face (not just at braced panel segments) as the default advanced-framing bracing method, valid up to 24 in stud spacing; treat let-in bracing or other non-continuous methods as limited to 16 in stud spacing.
12. Floor and ceiling framing follow the same 24 in o.c. module as the walls when stack framing is used; engineered joists (I-joist, SCL, glulam) are the default member type at that spacing, not dimension lumber.
13. Metal straps or anchors linking studs across a top plate should only be modelled where the load path is not already carried by continuous structural panel sheathing; the guide treats sheathing-based connections as the preferred, lower-thermal-bridging alternative to metal hardware.
