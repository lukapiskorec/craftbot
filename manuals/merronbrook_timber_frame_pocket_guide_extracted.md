# Timber frame construction: a useful pocket site guide: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Merronbrook - Timber Frame Pocket Guide.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://www.merronbrook.co.uk/assets/downloads/timber-frames/timberframepocketguide-aug2016.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** Timber frame construction, a useful pocket site guide. Version 2.0, August 2016 (content dated 2nd edition 2014, updated by Martin Milner). Published by the Structural Timber Association (STA), Alloa, Scotland, with technical support from NHBC and LABC. No ISBN. Reference STA/TFCPG/0816. 64 pages as paginated in the PDF.

**Scope of source:** A site quality assurance pocket book for low rise domestic timber frame buildings built by the open panel method, with timber floor joists and truss rafter roofs. It gives site checklists, defect warnings and setting out tolerances for the sequence from substructure to finished cladding. It explicitly puts design best practice and project specific member sizing outside its scope: it says the frame specification, nailing schedule and manufacturer's drawings take precedence, and it does not print stud sizes, spacing tables, span tables, panel layer buildups or truss designs. Treat this document as a source of erection tolerances, sequence and site details, not as a structural design or sizing reference.

**Most useful for:** setting realistic construction tolerances and erection sequence into a platform frame model, sole plate and packing details, cavity barrier and breather membrane layout, and the gaps needed for differential movement between a timber frame and masonry cladding.

## Notes on the coordination checklists (pages 8 to 9)

The contractor and design team checklist covers foundation drawings, architectural drawings, M and E drawings, timber frame erection drawings, timber frame detail books, NHBC chapter 6.2 or LABC technical manual as appropriate, finishes and material lists, and a reviewed fire safety plan. The erectors and contractors checklist adds agreed scope, risk assessments, health and safety plan, erection programme and delivery plan, craneage plan, and scaffolding plan. The erectors checklist adds material schedules, nailing schedules, erection sequence, site supervisor's checklist and special instructions. None of these carry numeric values, they are process gates before work starts.

## Notes on the build sequence, what to look for (pages 10 to 18)

The guide numbers 106 checklist items across nine stages. The geometrically relevant ones:

Before frame delivery: foundations must be constructed to the setting out drawings, top of substructure level and square within tolerance (see substructure tolerances above), foundations dimensionally correct, scaffolding and craneage agreed.

Storage: keep components off the ground and ventilated, store panels flat with sheathing side up, keep trusses vertical on bearers at node points or flat on adequate bearing.

During erection: temporary bracing fitted, floors not overloaded, work progressed systematically floor by floor, each level completed before moving on, scaffolding kept ahead of the work.

Upon completion, wall construction checks: DPC under all ground floor walls in contact with slab, panels right way up and plumb to tolerance, joints aligned and tight to tolerance, multiple studs present under beam loads, vertical DPCs at external openings, locating plate and head binders fitted.

Upon completion, floor construction checks: joists per design drawings with decking screwed or nailed (not adhesive alone), joists have adequate bearing (typically minimum 45 mm, more if hangers or supports require it), joists nogged or blocked per design (note that engineered joist designs usually omit mid span noggings, called dwangs in Scotland), joist hangers fully nailed and close fitting, no oversized hangers.

Upon completion, roof construction checks: trusses correctly spaced and plumb to tolerance, truss clips fitted or adequately nailed, trusses braced, roof watertight before internal work starts, girder trusses bolted or nailed per design, multiple studs under point loads, eaves plumb cuts straight and true, soffit supported with noggins, valley boarding fitted, ladder sections and roof bracing connected to spandrel panels, eaves vent ducts giving 50 mm airflow or an accredited breather membrane.

Before dry lining: frame moisture content below 20 percent and the building watertight before insulation and plasterboard, cavity barriers fitted at separating floors and walls, insulation to specification with no gaps, vapour control layer or vapour control plasterboard fitted to all external walls and lapped to floor junctions, services notched and drilled only per detail, noggins fitted, no timber contact with flues or chimneys (suitable clearance air gap required).

After external cladding: roof tiled and ventilated per manufacturer's requirements, cavity perpend vents fitted and unblocked, wall ties and fixings stainless steel at correct centres and fitted to the stud centre line sloping away from the frame, cavity width checked, settlement gaps fitted at eaves, sills, penetrations and verges filled with compressible filler, window and door apertures sealed.

## Chapter notes: substructure (pages 20 to 22)

The chapter opens with a defect warning: a badly laid and inaccurate substructure is the single biggest problem the timber frame erector faces on site, and faults must be rectified before the frame is erected.

Fig, p.20: two check diagrams. Measuring the diagonals of the base to confirm it is square (tolerance plus or minus 5 mm up to 10 m, plus or minus 10 mm over 10 m), and measuring wall lengths against the drawing (tolerance plus or minus 10 mm).

Fig, p.21: plan view of a slab edge measured against a straight datum line, tolerance plus or minus 10 mm maximum. Section view of a concrete slab checked against a level datum, individual points within plus or minus 5 mm, whole slab level variation not exceeding 10 mm.

Fig, p.22: two section views through a foundation wall showing level tolerance against a datum, 4 mm up to 150 mm wall width and 7 mm at 250 mm wall width. Repeated defect warning: if the permitted tolerances cannot be achieved, stop and consult.

## Chapter notes: sole plates (pages 23 to 31)

The sole plate is the first level of timber on the project and its setting out and fixing accuracy affects the whole building's performance. The same principles apply at storey lift level, where sole plates are sometimes called wall plates. Block up stands for the sole plate should be minimum 7.3 N/mm2 block. Sole plates are usually installed before the timber frame is delivered.

Sole plate checklist (p.24): DPC under the sole plate to specification, correct size and grade of timber, replace damaged or heavily fissured plates, sole plates are preservative treated and sometimes supplied in random lengths to be cut on site (cut ends must be brush treated), DPC joints lapped 100 mm.

Fig, p.25: accuracy of setting out. The structural frame engineer must be consulted if the sole plate extends or sets back by more than 10 mm from the design line. Horizontal tolerance checks shown in section (sole plate and DPC against a reference line) and vertical tolerance checks (plus or minus 2 mm over the entire area of the dwelling).

Three packing options for levelling the sole plate, each shown as an isometric detail:

1. Permanent structural packing under sole plate (fig, p.26): the sole plate is levelled on temporary spacers at no greater than 0.9 m centres. After the ground floor wall panels and the floor over, or roof structure, are erected, permanent packing is placed under the sole plate: either free flowing non shrinkable grout along the full length and width, or individual durable robust packers under the full area of each load point.

2. Bedding of sole plate (fig, p.27): the sole plate is laid on a continuous level bed of mortar extending the full length and width of the plate, checked for line and level with spacers.

3. Double sole plate sandwich (fig, p.28): a lower sole plate follows the contours of the supporting structure, an upper sole plate is fixed on top and levelled with temporary spacers between the two plates. After the first lift is erected, permanent timber or robust packing is inserted under each load point for the full area.

Depth of packing (fig, p.29): if the packing, bedding or spacer depth is above 10 mm, the structural timber frame engineer must be consulted to check fixing type and length. The packing or bed must support each stud, be durable, not deform under load, and maintain the DPC between packing and plate.

Fixing (fig, p.30): direct fixing with shot fired nails or screw and plug to design specification and centres, or sole plate brackets. Brackets within the internal envelope should be galvanised mild steel, minimum coating Z275, with matching corrosion protection fixings. Brackets in the cavity should be stainless steel. Defect warnings: ensure fixing length accounts for packing depth, avoid splitting the sole plate or damaging the substructure (which can spall masonry or slab edges).

Fixing checklist (p.31): consult and set out the wall plate or sole plate layout on the prepared substructure, check line, level and diagonals before fixing, use the correct fixing method at specified centres, agree fixing length and spacing with the frame engineer, use high load galvanised or stainless steel brackets and fixings as specified, use non-compressing shims and durable packing.

## Chapter notes: frame erection (pages 32 to 36)

Always check line, level and diagonals of the substructure before erection begins, and consult the site supervisor if they are outside tolerance. Check panels for storage damage or out of square panels, which cause poor fitting frames and out of tolerance buildings.

Nailing: panel to panel fixings at minimum 300 mm centres vertically, rail to sole plate fixings at minimum 600 mm centres, following the frame specification (ask for one if none exists). Head binders, where specified, are nailed separately to the top of wall panels, covering panel joints and overlapping by 600 mm. Specification should be checked for VCL membranes to be installed during panel erection (at internal to external wall junctions and floor to external wall junctions) and for sealant or compressible seal at panel and floor connections.

Floor to wall junctions need a nailing or screw fixing specification. Floors must not overhang into the cavity by more than 10 mm. Roof and floor members are fixed per specification, roofs tied down, and the frame built around chimneys and flues with a typical minimum 40 mm clearance air gap.

Fig, p.33: vertical tolerances. Maximum deviation from a vertical datum plumb line is 10 mm per storey, with a maximum cumulative value of plus or minus 10 mm from the datum over two storeys, assuming a maximum storey height of 3 m. Line of panels: plus or minus 3 mm on the line of the sole plate, plus or minus 5 mm deviation measured at wall panel mid height from an agreed datum line.

Fig, p.34: alignment. The internal horizontal building face of a panel on plan can step a maximum of 5 mm, averaging no more than 3 mm to any one panel. Vertical steps are reviewed for their impact on the floor or roof structure above. Gaps over 2 mm high and over 300 mm long require a rigid full bearing packer inserted. Maximum steps of plus or minus 5 mm trigger investigation and adjustment. Diagram shows a plan view (horizontal step) and elevation view (vertical step) through stacked panels, aiming for nil deviation, noting that the frame cannot be plumbed after the plasterboard is fixed.

Non-loadbearing walls on floor decking must be supported on joists or noggins below, with lateral restraint at the top rail or head binder at maximum 600 mm centres.

Breather membrane (p.35): often pre-fixed in the factory. If applied on site, laps are 100 mm horizontal and 150 mm vertical. A minimum 25 mm oversail at the base is required, 100 mm is good practice so the membrane sheds water into the cavity and accommodates tolerances and sole plate packing. Identification marks on the membrane pinpoint stud locations for the cladding trades. Damaged areas are repaired with surplus material lapped correctly below and over the existing material and stapled; no timber frame structure should be visible once the membrane is fixed and repaired.

Fig, p.36: patching tears in breather membrane, a three step isometric sequence. Step 1, the tear is located. Step 2, the area around the tear is squared off and cuts extended to the top by 150 mm. Step 3, a patch is slipped up from below with minimum 100 mm overlap to the bottom edge and minimum 150 mm overlap to both sides, stapled in position with stainless steel staples at 150 mm centres.

## Chapter notes: insulation (pages 37 to 41)

Insulation for walls and roofs is project specific; floor insulation requirement is checked against the design specification. Materials can be wood wool, mineral wool, natural wool, cellular fibres or rigid foam, all requiring CE marking. Insulation checklist: no wet or damp insulation, timber moisture content below 20 percent measured with a calibrated meter, building watertight or insulation protected, insulation fills the whole area between studs and plates with no gaps, and is not overfilled (which can cause nail popping).

Fig, p.38: a standard wall insulation detail, showing dry lining and VCL layers, with a defect warning that filling behind return studs is essential to avoid uninsulated zones.

Fig, p.39: truss ceiling tie level insulation, called a cold roof space (other roof types exist and the design specification should be consulted). Shows a minimum 50 mm gap at the eaves ventilator, and notes not to cut insulation short at that point.

Roof insulation checklist (p.40): placed below ceiling wind bracing, dressed around and over but not beneath water tanks, cross lapped in layers to required depth, rafter ventilator fixed to stop insulation blocking airflow, insulation continues over and below the wall head to prevent cold bridging.

Fully filled party walls (pp.40 to 41): a common current regulation requirement. For open timber frame panels, insulation between panels is installed once the building is dry and watertight; for sheathed party walls, check the method statement so insulation stays dry. Fig, p.41: fully filled party wall cavity, minimum 250 mm recommended width, maximum 600 mm, with a defect warning that the party wall cavity needs a minimum 50 mm clear gap and a minimum 240 mm distance between the internal faces of the plasterboard on each side.

## Chapter notes: vapour control layer and airtightness (pages 42 to 45)

Durability depends on a vented cavity between frame and cladding plus a breather membrane on the cold side of the insulation, applied to the outer face of the sheathing. The VCL, on the warm side of the insulation, can be an important part of the airtightness strategy.

VCL checklist (p.43): VCL is beneath or part of the plasterboard lining, may be a separate polythene sheet, vapour check plasterboard or an appropriate internal wood based board; not installed if framing moisture content is above 20 percent; for 500 gauge or 125 micron polythene sheet, lap 100 mm with joints on studs or rails, fixed with 9 mm by 9 mm by 18 gauge stainless steel staples at maximum 250 mm centres; airtightness specification may require specialist VCL tape; sheet VCLs overlap into separating walls and across to the floor airtight barrier, and return into reveals, heads and sills of openings; damaged VCL is repaired to maintain airtightness.

Thermal efficiency and airtightness (pp.44 to 45): continuity of the airtightness barrier matters most around windows, doors and other penetrations. At floor level the VCL must lap with the DPC (fig, p.44, shows VCL, DPC and DPM lapped together). At the floor to wall junction the VCL around the floor frame laps onto the wall VCL, and the airtight layer around the floor must be a vapour open material, not polythene (fig, p.45). At the wall to roof junction the VCL must lap through as well.

## Chapter notes: dry lining, fire stops, cavity barriers (pages 46 to 52)

Dry lining (p.46) is fixed only once the frame is watertight, with services tested first, and only on timber below 20 percent moisture content. Fire rated linings using two layers require both layers fully nailed or screwed to specification. General fixing centres: nails maximum 150 mm, drywall screws 230 mm on ceilings and 300 mm on walls; screws reduce nail popping from moisture movement.

Fire stops (p.47) are non-combustible board or mineral wool, installed at wall junctions between dwellings (such as roof to wall), at service penetrations through party walls and floors, and at other compartment separations. The page shows a typical fire stop layout at a spandrel and roof membrane junction and at boxed eaves, and notes floor level fire stops can be omitted in fully filled party walls.

Cavity barriers (pp.48 to 51) can be rigid, preservative treated timber battens or non-combustible board, or flexible, based on mineral wool or intumescent strips. They must be positioned against solid timber members (studs, rim beam). For dwellings, cavity barriers are required around all openings in external walls, at the top of the external wall cavity, at compartment wall or floor junctions with external walls, at party wall junctions between buildings, and for non-domestic properties in England and Wales at every 10 m. In Scotland and Northern Ireland they are additionally required at every floor to external wall junction, and at maximum 10 m centres in Scotland, 8 m in Northern Ireland. Fig, p.49: a typical wall section showing breather membrane lapped over a cavity tray by at least 100 mm, open perpend, cavity barrier, vapour open airtight barrier and dry lining. Fig, p.50: cavity barrier positions on a typical semi-detached or end terrace dwelling, separate diagrams for England and Wales versus Scotland and Northern Ireland requirements, noting a cavity barrier is needed at each floor level for flatted developments. Cavity barriers checklist (p.51): barriers must completely close the cavity, flexible barriers must be a tight fit of the correct thickness fixed with stainless steel staples at specified spacing, rigid barriers must be the exact width (made up with flexible material if undersized), protected with a DPC between barrier and cladding, no gaps at junctions, horizontal barriers overlapped by the breather membrane above, mineral wool in flexible barriers tightly butted or lapped 100 mm at junctions.

Cavity barriers and fire stops at party walls (p.52): a vertical party wall junction detail shows DPC, fire stop with edge seal, cavity insulation and cavity barrier, noting other options exist per specification. A horizontal party wall junction detail carries a defect warning that a fire stop must be installed at floor level if the wall is not detailed with fully filled party wall insulation (the fire stop material at that junction may otherwise form part of the fully filled solution).

## Chapter notes: masonry cladding (pages 53 to 56)

Masonry cladding is built using a coursing rod so levels line up at openings and eaves, with pre-planned coursing for clearance at sills, soffits and balconies for differential movement. Cavity width between the frame assembly and masonry cladding is typically minimum 50 mm, maximum 60 mm, or as specified. Before starting external masonry, plumb down from the eaves and gables of the timber frame to check the cavity width will fall within tolerance.

Vented cavity checklist (p.54): clear of mortar droppings, at the base of the wall one brick course below the DPC and sole plate, open perpends at maximum 1500 mm centres for a vented cavity (1200 mm in Scotland, plus perpend vents at eaves level), and above horizontal cavity trays (lintels, cavity barriers) open perpends at 900 mm centres and at least two per lintel. Vented underfloor construction needs vents built into the masonry per the drawings.

Fig, p.55: typical section through a vented cavity, clear cavity width shown between the frame sheathing and brick skin, with wall ties centrally nailed to stud locations. Typical clear cavity width stated here as minimum 50 mm, maximum 58 mm, check specification (note this is slightly narrower than the 60 mm maximum given on p.53, both figures are in the source).

Fixing wall ties (p.56): ties must suit the cavity width and be timber frame ties that accommodate movement, sized and spaced to the engineer's specification. Do not straighten pre-bent ties, bend points upward, place in line with the stud, bedded end flat against the brick. Drive the stainless steel nail home firmly into the stud location, not into sheathing alone unless the sheathing design specifically allows it. Typical tie spacing at maximum centres: horizontally 600 mm, vertically 450 mm or 375 mm depending on stud centres and design, at openings 225 mm, at eaves and verges 300 mm, at expansion joints 225 mm.

## Chapter notes: installing services (page 57)

Floor joists: only notch or drill solid timber joists within the designer's specified limits, never notch I-joists or open web joists (use their factory knockouts instead), and follow the manufacturer's recommendations for holes in engineered wood joists. Walls: studs and rails are drilled or notched only where stated on the drawings, service points and sockets are fully backed to meet fire resistance requirements, external service penetrations follow the fire stopping and cavity barrier specification. General: avoid plumbing in external walls where possible, provide access to pipe bends and stop valves, seal VCL service penetrations with PVC tape, use de-rated cabling within the stud framework per IEE guidelines, protect services from nail penetration with conduits.

## Chapter notes: differential movement (pages 58 to 61)

Timber is typically installed at up to 20 percent moisture content and dries to around 10 to 12 percent for internal walls in a heated building; as it dries the cross section shrinks and the structure settles. Cladding materials move differently: clay brick expands, blocks and calcium silicate brick shrink, neither in step with the timber. Good practice is to pre-load the frame with roof tiles and internal sheeting before installing masonry cladding, within structural limits. Any component fixed to the frame that overhangs or projects through the masonry needs a gap beneath it, filled with a compressible filler such as impregnated foam tape, to take up the movement without damage. Allowances are needed at window sills, roof verges and eaves, timber or boarding that overhangs brickwork, flues and chimneys, overflow pipes, and traditionally built stair cores. Reducing vertical shrinkage means using I-joists, open web joists or super dried timber, correct settlement detailing, and keeping timbers as dry as possible.

Fig, p.60: a section through a two storey brick clad house with 235 mm standard sawn timber joists at first and second floor, over a concrete ground floor. Gaps shown: 10 mm at ground floor level, 20 mm at first floor level, 35 mm at second floor level, with 5 mm added to the gap dimension at eaves level. A note explains these are generic gap values including allowances for brick expansion and filler compression, and that project specific STA member design values should be used in preference where available. Settlement at a chimney depends on the number of storeys and the roof span.

Fig, p.61: sills, a masonry corner detail showing the sill fixed to the timber frame but not built into the masonry, with a gap to allow movement below the brick sill support. Verges and eaves, two elevation and section sketches showing gaps to allow movement between a barge or fascia detail and the brick gable, and between an eaves soffit and the wall below.

## Fire, cost and other short chapters

Fire is covered only through the fire stops and cavity barriers chapters already summarised above (pages 47 to 52); there is no separate fire chapter. Cost, history and acoustics are not covered chapters in this guide beyond passing references to fire and acoustic performance of dry lining and insulation (p.46, p.16). The reference documents chapter (p.63) points to NHBC Standards Chapter 6.2, the LABC Warranty Technical Manual, TRADA guidance on timber frame construction, STA advice notes on timber frame tolerances and on cavity barrier best practice, STA erector training books, and the Energy Saving Trust website for airtightness and thermal efficiency background reading.

## Rules for modelling

This is a reference and site quality guide, not a procedural build script, so the rules below are the tolerances and setting out constraints a modelling agent should apply when generating a UK platform frame model, in roughly the order the building goes up.

1. Substructure must be square to within plus or minus 5 mm up to 10 m diagonal, or 10 mm over 10 m, and wall lengths must be within plus or minus 10 mm of drawn dimensions (p.20).
2. Slab or foundation wall level must not vary by more than 5 mm at any point or 10 mm across the whole slab (p.21), and foundation wall level tolerance is 4 mm up to 150 mm wall width or 7 mm at 250 mm (p.22).
3. Set the sole plate no more than 10 mm off its design line without engineer input, and hold it to plus or minus 2 mm horizontally over the whole dwelling (p.25).
4. Model the sole plate as sitting on one of three packing systems: permanent packing or grout under each stud after erection with 0.9 m temporary spacers meanwhile, a continuous mortar bed, or a double sole plate sandwich with temporary spacers between the two plates (pp.26 to 28). Any packing or bedding thicker than 10 mm needs an engineer specified fixing.
5. Space wall panel nailing at minimum 300 mm centres vertically and rail to sole plate fixings at minimum 600 mm centres, with head binders lapping panel joints by 600 mm (p.32).
6. Hold panel plumb to within 10 mm per storey and plus or minus 10 mm cumulative over two storeys at 3 m storey height, and hold panels to plus or minus 3 mm on the sole plate line, plus or minus 5 mm at mid height (p.33).
7. Keep the internal face step between adjacent panels to a maximum 5 mm, averaging 3 mm, and insert a rigid packer at any gap over 2 mm high and 300 mm long (p.34).
8. Lap breather membrane 100 mm horizontally and 150 mm vertically, with a minimum 25 mm oversail at the base, 100 mm recommended (p.35).
9. Do not let floors overhang the cavity by more than 10 mm, and keep a minimum 40 mm clearance air gap around chimneys and flues (p.33).
10. Model the roof as truss rafters over a cold roof space (insulation at ceiling tie level) with a minimum 50 mm ventilator gap at the eaves, unless a warm roof detail is specified (p.39).
11. Size masonry cladding cavity width to fall between 50 mm minimum and 60 mm maximum (the source also gives 58 mm as a typical maximum elsewhere, treat 50 to 58 mm as the safer default and check against project specification), with open perpends at maximum 1500 mm centres at the base (1200 mm in Scotland) and 900 mm centres above lintels (p.53 to p.55).
12. Space wall ties at maximum 600 mm horizontally and 375 to 450 mm vertically depending on stud centres, tightened to 225 mm at openings and expansion joints and 300 mm at eaves and verges (p.56).
13. Place cavity barriers around every external wall opening, at the top of the external wall cavity, and at every compartment wall or floor to external wall junction; add vertical barriers at 10 m centres in England, Wales and Scotland, 8 m in Northern Ireland (pp.48 to 49).
14. Fully filled party wall cavities should be modelled at 250 mm minimum to 600 mm maximum width, with a minimum 50 mm clear gap and 240 mm minimum between the internal wall faces (p.41).
15. Leave a compressible movement gap wherever a component crosses from the timber frame into masonry cladding: 10 mm at ground floor level, 20 mm at first floor level, 35 mm at second floor level, plus an extra 5 mm at eaves level, scaled from a 235 mm deep sawn timber joist reference case (p.60). Treat these as generic defaults, project specific STA design values take precedence where given.
16. Keep sill, verge and eaves details clear of the masonry below by the same kind of movement gap, with the sill fixed to the timber frame rather than built into the brick (p.61).
