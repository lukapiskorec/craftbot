# Canadian CLT Handbook, 2019 Edition: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Canadian CLT Handbook 2019.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://web.fpinnovations.ca/download/clt-handbook-2019-full-edition/ and save it under that name.

**Source:** Karacabeyli, Erol and Gagnon, Sylvain (editors), *Canadian CLT Handbook, 2019 Edition*, FPInnovations, Pointe-Claire, Quebec, Special Publication SP-532E, 2019. ISSN 1925-0517. ISBN 978-0-86488-590-6 (paperback, Volume I). Volume II's own print ISBN is not stated on the pages carried into this PDF; the digital format covering both volumes combined carries ISBN 978-0-86488-592-0. This single PDF contains both volumes bound together, 812 pages as counted by the Read tool and pdftotext. Chapter authors and peer reviewers are listed individually per chapter (over 30 named contributors from FPInnovations, APA, Canadian Wood Council, National Research Council Canada, universities and engineering firms).

**Scope of source:** The complete, current North American reference on cross laminated timber (CLT), covering the product itself (manufacturing, grades, dimensions), structural design of CLT elements and buildings (gravity, lateral, connections, duration of load, vibration), performance attributes (fire, acoustics, building enclosure, environmental) and construction (lifting, handling, transportation), finished with a full worked design example of an 8-storey mass timber building. It is a design and specification guide, not a shop-drawing catalogue: it gives generic CLT stress grades (E1-E4, V1-V3), generic connection types and capacities, and one detailed worked example, but it does not give a single manufacturer's actual panel catalogue, does not give prescriptive span tables for light constructions (that is the Wood Design Manual's job, referenced throughout), and its numbers assume Canadian code context (CSA O86, NBCC 2015) rather than a European or US-only code. Fire, acoustics, moisture and environmental chapters are performance-attribute chapters written for code compliance, not construction detail catalogues.

**Most useful for:** sizing and detailing CLT floor, roof and wall panels (thickness, span, layer build-up), designing and drawing panel-to-panel, wall-to-floor, wall-to-roof and wall-to-foundation connections (splines, half-laps, brackets, hold-downs, screw and lag-screw patterns), laying out CLT shear walls and their aspect ratios, sequencing lifting and erection of CLT panels including rigging geometry and transport envelopes, and reproducing a complete 8-storey platform-frame mass timber building (grid, member sizes, connection counts) as a worked example.

## Chapter 1: Introduction to cross-laminated timber (PDF pp. 8-54)

CLT is defined as an engineered panel of at least three layers of dimension lumber boards glued face to face in alternating (typically 90 degree) orientation, with an odd number of layers (three to seven common, more possible). Key baseline facts from section 1.3-1.4 (PDF p.21-23):
- Lumber thickness ranges 16 to 51 mm, width about 60 to 240 mm; boards are finger-jointed, kiln dried and either visually or machine stress graded.
- Panel widths in the market are typically 0.6, 1.2, 2.4 or 3 m, lengths up to 18 m, and thickness typically 105, 175 or 245 mm in buildings though up to 508 mm is possible in special cases.
- Wall panels are normally oriented with outer layers vertical (parallel to gravity load); floor and roof panels have outer layers parallel to the main span.
- Figure 1 (panel configuration) and Figure 2 (example cross-sections), both PDF p.21, and Figure 3 (5-layer panel cross-section with fibre direction of top layers, PDF p.22) are the baseline diagrams for how a CLT panel is assembled.
- Figure 4 (PDF p.23) contrasts CLT with glulam: CLT's cross lamination gives it two-way, plate-like structural action (like a reinforced concrete slab) rather than glulam's one-way beam action.
- Figure 5 (PDF p.23) shows the same floor built either from four narrower one-way panels or one CLT panel spanning both directions, with the note that "distance a" between supports for two-way action can reach 3 m depending on manufacturer.

Section 1.2 (introduction of CLT in North America, PDF p.19-20) traces the line from the first (2011) Canadian CLT Handbook, through its use as the base document for the 2013 US CLT Handbook, to the bi-national CLT product standard that both countries now recognise; Section 1.8 (CLT in construction, PDF p.21) is a short pointer forward to Chapters 2-13 for the actual design and construction content.

Section 1.6 (PDF p.25-28) covers the two governing standards: ANSI/APA PRG 320 (manufacturing, qualification, QA, bi-national for Canada and the US) and CSA O86 (structural, fire, vibration design provisions, updated to include CLT in Update 1, 2016). Section 1.6.2 sketches three regulatory paths in Canada: the Acceptable Solution path (code-prescribed heights/areas), the Alternative Solution path (performance-based, used for the tall wood demonstration buildings), and a Site-Specific Regulation path unique to British Columbia.

Section 1.7 (p.14-20 of the chapter) is a one-paragraph preview of every later chapter's scope, useful as a fast cross-check that a topic is covered somewhere in the book before diving into pdftotext searches: lateral design (1.7.1), connections and construction (1.7.2), duration of load and creep (1.7.3), vibration of floors and tall buildings (1.7.4), fire performance (1.7.5), sound insulation (1.7.6), building enclosure (1.7.7) and environmental performance (1.7.8).

Section 1.9 (p.21 of the chapter) introduces the 8-storey mass timber design example that is worked in full in Chapter 13. Section 1.10 (p.22-25) gives Canadian market context with data through 2016-2017: housing starts shifting toward multi-family construction, taller wood buildings becoming more common, and early applications identified as the most promising near-term CLT niches: elevator/stair cores and shafts (Figure 9, the Arbora elevator shaft under construction, p.23), composite wood-hybrid floor plates, podium levels under taller towers, buildings above six storeys (needing an alternative-solution path), and non-residential low-rise construction generally (one-to-four storey non-residential floor area chart, Figure 11, and construction cost comparison, Figure 12, both p.25).

## Chapter 2: Cross-laminated timber manufacturing (PDF pp. 55-106)

This is the chapter to open for panel product and dimension questions. Section 2.2 (component requirements, PDF p.60-69 approx.) covers what goes into a panel before it is even pressed:
- 2.2.1 Laminations (PDF p.60-71): lumber selection criteria (moisture content, characteristics affecting adhesive bond quality), and structural composite lumber (SCL, e.g. LVL or LSL) as an alternative lamination material for outer or inner layers. Sections 2.2.1.1-2.2.1.2 give the theoretical basis for choosing sawn-lumber versus SCL laminations (grade, stiffness sorting, and how each behaves once cross-laminated), and 2.2.1.3 covers practical lamination requirements specific to CLT manufacturing.
- 2.2.2 Adhesives (PDF p.74-77): adhesive requirements are set by durability under the same in-service conditions as the finished CLT panel, since the finger joints inside individual boards and the panel's face-to-face glue lines both have to survive the panel's service life; the chapter covers adhesive families acceptable for CLT, the same durability class of structural adhesives already used for glulam and finger-jointing, tested per the qualification protocol in section 2.5.
- 2.2.3 Lamination joints (PDF p.77): edge-joint types between adjacent boards in the same layer, with or without edge gluing. Unglued edges are common in North American CLT and explicitly affect connection design, rolling shear behaviour and gap-crossing detailing in Chapter 5, where fasteners installed directly over an unglued gap can lose capacity.

Section 2.3 (PDF p.77-82) is the core dimensional-data section:
- Maximum CLT thickness 508 mm (20 in), a practical limit for handling and transport rather than a structural one.
- Dimensional tolerances at time of manufacture (see quick reference table above) differ slightly between the bi-national ANSI/APA PRG 320 standard and the Canadian CCMC Technical Guide; both are given because a designer may encounter either.
- Panel classification is based on lay-up: seven generic layups are defined in PRG 320, four "E" grades (MSR/E-rated lumber in parallel layers, No.3 grade in perpendicular layers) using Spruce-Pine-Fir, Douglas Fir-Larch, Eastern Softwoods/Northern/Western species, or Southern Pine, and three "V" grades (visually graded lumber) using Douglas Fir-Larch, Spruce-Pine-Fir or Southern Pine. Table 1 (p.79) gives required characteristic strengths and moduli for these layups (bending, tension, compression, shear, rolling shear) in both parallel and perpendicular layers. Table 3 (p.80), "LSD stiffness and unfactored resistance values for CLT," is the single most useful table in the chapter for a modelling agent: for 105, 175 and 245 mm panels (built from 35 mm laminations, i.e. 3, 5 and 7 layers) it lists effective bending stiffness (EI)eff, shear rigidity (GA)eff and shear resistance vz in both major and minor strength directions, for each of the E1, E2, E3, V1, V2 grades.
- Appearance classification (p.81-82) has no mandatory standard; PRG 320 offers non-mandatory "Architectural" (knot holes over 19 mm filled, exposed faces free of loose knots, smooth finish) and "Industrial" (unfilled voids, no fill requirement, concealed-use) classifications, with the note that appearance does not affect structural capacity.

Section 2.4 (manufacturing process, PDF p.83-87) lists nine steps: (1) primary lumber selection, (2) lumber grouping (sorting by grade/stiffness into the layers they will occupy), (3) lumber planing (to a consistent thickness before layup), (4) cutting layers to the desired panel length, (5) adhesive application, (6) CLT panel layup (boards placed in the alternating orthogonal pattern), (7) assembly pressing (Figure 10, p.83, the schematic of this whole sequence; a companion figure shows a lumber shrinkage relief detail used between boards in the same layer), (8) in-line quality control plus surface sanding and cutting to final size, and (9) product marking, packaging and shipping. It notes CLT can be built from layers up to 51 mm thick combined to reach any total thickness up to the 508 mm cap, that the vast majority of panels are made to a specific project's layup, size, shape and machined features (recesses, holes, slots) rather than as a generic stocked commodity, and that panels are cut and CNC-routed for openings (windows, doors, service channels, connections) after pressing.

Section 2.5 (qualification and QA, PDF p.88-91) covers plant pre-qualification (initial testing before a plant is permitted to sell certified CLT), mechanical-properties qualification (proving a specific layup meets its published design values) and re-qualification after any process change (adhesive, species, press parameters), and the two mandatory ongoing QA tests: delamination tests (checking the glue bond survives accelerated wet/dry or freeze/thaw cycling) and block shear tests (checking bond-line shear strength on small specimens), plus visual quality checks for gaps, checks and surface defects. Figure 12 (p.90) shows where delamination ("D") and block shear ("B") test specimens are cut from within a pre-qualification panel per ANSI/APA PRG 320 and the CCMC Technical Guide.

Section 2.6 (standardization, PDF p.92) and 2.7 (conclusions, PDF p.93) close the chapter by noting that as more manufacturers enter the North American market, further standardization of layups, thicknesses and appearance grading is expected, and that designers should always confirm the actual published properties of the specific manufacturer's product being used rather than assuming the generic PRG 320 grade values apply unmodified.

## Chapter 3: Structural design of cross-laminated timber elements (PDF pp. 107-172)

This chapter is the CSA O86-aligned engineering method for sizing CLT floor, roof and wall panels; a modelling agent needs it mainly for its numeric outputs (thicknesses, spans, deflections) rather than the derivations, since Chapter 13's worked examples apply the same equations with numbers filled in. Section 3.2 (material properties, PDF p.115-117) restates Chapter 2's Table 1 stress-grade values as the input data for every equation in the rest of the chapter. Section 3.3 (analytical methods, PDF p.117-119) previews the three stiffness/strength models detailed in Appendix 3A (Gamma method, k-method, shear analogy) and notes that general finite-element software can also model CLT panels directly as orthotropic plates once (EI)eff and (GA)eff are known in both directions.

Section 3.4 (modification factors, PDF p.121) sets KD per CSA O86 Clause 5.3.2 (short/standard/long-term categories, same 1.15/1.00/0.65 values as in Chapter 6), KS = 1.0 for all properties because CLT is dry-service-only under the current standards, KT = 1.0 for untreated panels, and KH = 1.0 (no system factor benefit currently recognised for CLT plates).

Section 3.5 (flatwise bending and shear, PDF p.122-129) is the equation set behind every floor/roof panel design in the book:
- Bending resistance, major (longitudinal) axis: Mr,y = phi x Fb x Seff,y x Krb,y, with phi = 0.9 and Krb,y = 0.85 (a conservative reduction applied specifically to major-axis bending). Minor axis: same form with Krb,x = 1.0.
- The effective section modulus and effective bending stiffness (EI)eff are derived by the "shear analogy" (Kreuzinger) method, summing each layer's own bending stiffness plus its parallel-axis term; Figures 6 and 7 (PDF p.123, p.125) show the cross-section geometry for the major and minor directions of a 5-ply panel.
- Shear resistance uses the rolling-shear strength of the transverse layers: Vr = phi x Fs x (2/3) x Ag.
- Section 3.5.3 gives rules for cantilevers (use 2x the cantilever length as "l" when computing (EI)eff) and continuous multi-span beams (use 0.8l in a simplified procedure, or iterate to find the points of inflection and recompute).
- Section 3.5.4 (two-way slabs, PDF p.129) gives the concrete-slab-derived geometric criteria for when two-way action may be assumed: span ratio b/a not exceeding 2.0, relative stiffness of supporting beams/walls between 0.2 and 5.0, column offsets under 20% of span; if b/a exceeds 2.0 the panel should be designed one-way in the shorter direction a instead. It recommends at least 5 layers if two-way action is to be used, and notes finite-element torsional stiffness for CLT plates should be reduced to 50% (3-ply) or 25% (5-ply) of the calculated value, since the raw calculated values run high.

Section 3.6 (walls, PDF p.130-132) covers CLT panels loaded as walls:
- 3.6.1 Pure axial loads: only the layers whose laminations are parallel to an applied in-plane axial load contribute to the effective compression area Aeff (Figure 9, PDF p.130, "axially loaded 3-ply CLT panel," makes this explicit: the middle transverse layer of a 3-ply wall carries none of the vertical load). Factored compressive resistance Pr = phi x Fc x Aeff x KZc x KC, where KZc is a size factor and KC a slenderness factor; the slenderness ratio Cc is capped at 43 (equivalently, Le/reff capped at 150).
- 3.6.2 Combined axial and out-of-plane bending: an interaction equation checks Pf/Pr plus Mf/(Mr x (1 - Pf/PE,v)) against 1.0, where PE,v is the Euler buckling load adjusted for shear deformation using (GA)eff. This is the equation applied directly, with real numbers, in the Chapter 13 wall design example.

Section 3.7 (compression resistance perpendicular to the panel, PDF p.132) covers bearing at supports where a wall lands on a floor slab or a beam bears on a wall, i.e. load applied perpendicular to the panel's face rather than in-plane; Figure 11 ("compression failure perpendicular to the CLT panel at a support," p.132) shows the local crushing failure mode this section checks. This is the same perpendicular-to-grain bearing check flagged again in Chapter 4 wherever a rocking shear wall bears on a CLT floor slab beneath it.

Section 3.8 (CLT as a beam or lintel, PDF p.132-141) covers three shear failure modes when CLT is loaded in-plane as a beam: Mode I and II are conventional shear failure, Mode III is a distinctive CLT failure mode at the "crossing areas" between longitudinal and transverse laminations where shear stress concentrates (Figures 13-15, p.133-138, show the assumed stress distribution and a composite-beam model used to derive it). This matters for lintels over openings and deep beam-like CLT elements, distinct from the flatwise panel bending of section 3.5. Section 3.8.3 lists the K-factors specific to this beam/lintel use: KL (lateral stability factor, same concept as for sawn beams), KZb (size factor for bending) and KX/KR (curvature and radial resistance factors, relevant only if the CLT beam is curved). Section 3.8.4 notes creep in bending is treated the same way as for flatwise panels (Chapter 6).

Section 3.9 (deflections and vibration, PDF p.142) is a short cross-reference rather than a full method: it confirms that CLT floor and roof deflection is calculated with the (EI)eff/(GA)eff combined formula shown in the section 3.10 worked example (a bending term plus a shear term), and it refers vibration serviceability entirely to Chapter 7's vibration-controlled span method rather than repeating it.

Section 3.10 (simple design examples, PDF p.146-152) is a compact worked example using a single 175 mm, 5-ply, grade E1 panel (layer thicknesses 34/30/34/30/34 mm) run through every check in the chapter:
- Bending and shear stiffness (3.10.1-3.10.2, for a 1 m wide strip): (EI)eff,y = 3.4x10^12 N-mm2; bending resistance Mr,y = 7.7x10^7 N-mm (major axis) and Mr,x = 8.9x10^6 N-mm (minor axis); shear resistance Vr,zy = 4.9x10^4 N (major) and Vr,zx = 2.8x10^4 N (minor).
- Wall compression (3.10.3, same panel used as a 3 m tall, 1 m wide wall): effective thickness heff = 102 mm, effective area Aeff = 1.0x10^5 mm2, radius of gyration reff = 53.2 mm, slenderness ratio Cc = 16.3 (well under the 43 cap), and compressive resistance Pr = 1.4x10^6 N.
- Combined axial and bending (3.10.4): the same wall under Pf = 7.5x10^5 N axial load plus Mf,y = 5.0x10^6 N-mm out-of-plane moment gives an interaction ratio of 0.83, comfortably under 1.0.
- Deflection (3.10.5): the same panel as a 4.5 m span floor under 1.9 kPa live load deflects 3.3 mm total (bending term plus shear term).
- Gamma-method cross-check (3.10.6): reworking the same panel with the Gamma method for a 6 m span finds bending resistance 15.6% higher than the shear-analogy result, a difference attributed entirely to the 0.85 strength modification factor Krb,y applied only in the shear-analogy method.

Appendix 3A (PDF p.155-172) documents three alternative analytical models for effective stiffness and strength, in order of increasing complexity and generality:
- 3A.1 Gamma method (mechanically jointed beams theory, PDF p.157-162): treats the CLT panel as mechanically jointed layers with a connection-efficiency factor gamma per layer, most useful for unbalanced or unsymmetrical cross-sections where the shear analogy method's assumptions do not hold cleanly.
- 3A.2 k-method (composite theory, PDF p.163-165): Table 3A.1 gives composition factors "k" and Table 3A.2 gives effective strength/stiffness values for standard solid-wood cross-layer panels, a simpler but less flexible alternative to the Gamma method.
- 3A.3 Shear analogy (Kreuzinger) method (PDF p.166-172): the method used as the primary approach throughout the rest of the chapter and in the ANSI/APA PRG 320 standard's own published resistance tables, splitting the panel into two coupled "beams" (bending-only and shear-only components) whose results are superimposed to get the final stress distribution (Figures 3A.4-3A.7 illustrate this superposition).

Section 3.11 (concluding remarks, PDF p.152) notes CLT has been a structural product in Canada for less than a decade at the time of writing, with several editions of ANSI/APA PRG 320 published since and CLT design provisions now embedded in CSA O86; it flags Appendix 3A's alternative analytical models as the tool to reach for whenever a project's actual CLT layup falls outside what PRG 320 and CSA O86 directly cover.

## Chapter 4: Lateral design of cross-laminated timber buildings (PDF pp. 173-222)

Covers how CLT buildings resist wind and seismic loads, focused on platform-type construction (walls landing on the floor below at every storey) since that is where most research exists; Figure 1 (p.175) shows typical platform-type construction and Figure 2 (p.176) the balloon-type system used in the 13-storey Origine building in Quebec City, where wall panels run continuous over several storeys.

Section 4.3.3 (capacity design principles, PDF p.182-187) sets out which connections must be energy-dissipative (ductile, expected to yield) versus non-dissipative (capacity-protected, designed to remain elastic): vertical joints between shear wall segments and the connections from walls to the floor/foundation below in uplift should yield; perpendicular-wall connections, floor-panel connections and roof/floor-to-wall-below connections should not. Non-dissipative connections are sized to the 95th-percentile ultimate resistance of the dissipative connections, or to forces computed with RdRo = 1.3.

Section 4.3.4.1 (aspect ratio, PDF p.188) is one of the most load-bearing numeric rules in the book for shear wall geometry: CSA O86 requires shear wall (or wall segment) aspect ratios between 1:1 and 4:1 to use the ductility/over-strength factors RdRo = 3.0, but the handbook recommends raising the lower bound to 2:1 to favour rocking over sliding; walls with aspect ratio under 2:1, or over 4:1 (which should not be relied on for lateral resistance at all), must be designed with RdRo = 1.3. Figure 4 (p.189) illustrates the distinction between a "segment" (a length/height ratio unit for design purposes) and a "panel": two vertical CLT panels connected by a flexible joint (e.g. half-lap with widely spaced screws) count as two independent-rocking segments, while the same two panels joined by closely spaced, angled self-tapping screws act as a single rigid segment.

Section 4.3.4 (other design aspects, PDF p.190-206) covers the remaining geometric and behavioural rules for a lateral system:
- 4.3.4.2 Brackets and hold-downs (PDF p.190-192): angle brackets resist both horizontal shear and vertical uplift simultaneously (shear-uplift interaction, governed by a circular-domain interaction equation, Figure 5, p.191), while hold-downs are assumed to carry negligible shear and resist uplift only. Discrete hold-downs are recommended NOT to be part of the primary energy-dissipating mechanism (contrary to what CSA O86-14 Update 2 allows); they should still be designed to fail in a yielding mode but sized with extra over-strength.
- 4.3.4.3 Perpendicular walls (PDF p.192): ignore any stiffening contribution from walls perpendicular to the direction of loading, even though testing shows they can act somewhat like extra hold-downs, because the effect is not yet fully quantified; their connections should still be treated as non-dissipative.
- 4.3.4.4 Irregularities (PDF p.192-193): NBCC irregularity types 4 (in-plane discontinuity) and 5 (out-of-plane offset) are prohibited in a CLT seismic-force-resisting system; it is also prudent to avoid types 6, 8 and 9 (weak-storey, non-orthogonal, gravity-induced lateral-demand) until more research exists.
- 4.3.4.5 Deflections (PDF p.193-194): total lateral deflection of a shear wall is the sum of panel rocking, global rotation, sliding (to be minimised), connection deformation and in-plane wall bending (Figure 7, p.194); the gravity system must be checked to ensure it can tolerate the resulting drift.
- 4.3.4.6 CLT as a gravity system with a non-wood SFRS (PDF p.194-195): where CLT carries only gravity load alongside a steel or concrete lateral system, the CLT elements and connections must either be isolated from the seismic-force-resisting system's drift or be designed to accommodate that drift elastically.
- 4.3.4.7 CLT diaphragms (PDF p.195) and 4.3.4.8 balloon-type structures (PDF p.195-196) extend the same capacity-design and rocking principles to floor/roof diaphragms and to continuous multi-storey wall panels respectively.

Section 4.4 (methods of analysis, PDF p.192-193) allows both the equivalent static force procedure and dynamic (modal response spectrum or time-history) analysis per NBCC, matching the choice later made in Chapter 13's design example. Section 4.5 (research on seismic behaviour, PDF p.193-206) surveys full-scale shake-table testing that underpins the rocking-based design philosophy: European research (Dujic et al., Ceccotti, Popovski and Gavric) established the rocking/sliding response mechanisms; North American research includes a two-storey CLT house tested at FPInnovations (Figure 10, PDF p.204); and Japanese/New Zealand research includes a three-storey CLT house tested at NIED, Tsukuba (Figure 8, PDF p.198), a seven-storey house tested at the Hyogo Earthquake Engineering Research Center, Miki (Figure 9, PDF p.199), and a five-storey house tested at the E-Defense laboratory, also in Miki (Figure 11, PDF p.207).

## Chapter 5: Connections in cross-laminated timber buildings (PDF pp. 223-298)

The core connections chapter, closely relevant to any modelling agent drawing CLT joint geometry. Section 5.1.1 (fastener types, PDF p.228-231) surveys self-tapping screws (4-12 mm diameter, up to 600 mm long, usually installed without predrilling), nails/spikes/timber rivets (not permitted for withdrawal in end grain unless driven into lamination side grain), bolts and dowels (mainly hold-downs), and split ring/shear plate connectors (rare in CLT, more common with glulam).

Section 5.2.1 (in-plane panel-to-panel connections, "Detail A," PDF p.233-238) is a catalogue of five joint types, each with a labelled diagram:
- Internal spline (Fig.4, p.233): a plywood, LVL or lumber strip inserted into a slot machined in both panel edges, fastened with screws/nails on both faces; fasteners load in double shear, giving high lateral resistance, but requires precise plant profiling.
- Single surface spline (Fig.5, p.234): a strip fastened to one face across the joint, single shear, simple and fast on site, commonly used for floors.
- Double surface spline (Fig.6, p.235): splines on both faces, double the shear planes, can be designed for out-of-plane moment if plywood splines are used.
- Half-lap joint (Fig.7, p.236): panel edges rabbeted at the plant, joined on site with long self-tapping screws; simple but carries a splitting risk at the notch and a loss of panel width compared with splines.
- Proprietary connectors (p.237-238): the tube connector (slotted steel tubes with threaded rods, Fig.8), shear keys (steel plates in compression, developed by Nordic Structures for shear walls, Fig.9), and the Knapp clip-on male/female connector (Fig.10).

Section 5.2.2 (corner connections, "Detail B," PDF p.239-245) covers perpendicular-wall junctions:
- Self-tapping screws driven from the exterior (Fig.11-12), or toe-screwed at an angle from the interior to avoid end-grain installation (Fig.13); toe-screwing needs care to avoid chipping the panel corner in tension perpendicular to grain.
- Interior metal brackets (Fig.14): simple and strong but exposed, which reduces fire resistance unless the wall panel is recessed to hide the bracket and cap it with finishing material.
- Proprietary connectors: the X-RAD from Rothoblaas (pre-assembled steel-to-steel system, also usable as a lifting point, Fig.15), dovetail brackets sliding into place (Fig.16), Knapp clip-on brackets (Fig.17) and hook joints (Fig.18), all requiring tight fabrication tolerances for on-site fit.
- Concealed metal plates (6 to 12 mm thick, Fig.19-20), fastened with power-driven nails or self-tapping screws into precise CNC-machined recesses, sometimes paired with SFS Intec self-drilling screws that penetrate steel and wood without predrilling (Fig.21).

Section 5.2.3 (wall-to-floor connections, "Detail C," PDF p.246-257) covers platform construction:
- 5.2.3.1 Platform construction (PDF p.246-249): long self-tapping screws driven down through the floor into the wall edge below, or toe-screwed at an angle (Fig.22); metal brackets (Fig.23-24), sometimes with vertical slots to permit rocking; the "shear key" bracket with profiled holes that stay in contact with the bolt/dowel through the full rocking motion (Fig.25); proprietary Knapp connectors (Fig.26) and the Resilient Slip-Friction Joint (RSFJ), a self-centring friction hold-down providing up to 20% damping without needing post-earthquake replacement (Fig.27); concealed T-shaped metal plates (Fig.28).

- 5.2.3.3 Balloon construction (PDF p.253-257): continuous wall panels with a floor attached to the wall's side face via a wood ledger (glulam, LVL, LSL or PSL, Fig.37) or a metal bracket (Fig.38), plus metal plates that maintain continuity across the shear wall at each floor level (Fig.36).

Section 5.2.4 (wall-to-roof, "Detail D," PDF p.258) and 5.2.5 (wall-to-foundation, "Detail E," PDF p.259-262) repeat the same self-tapping-screw/metal-bracket/concealed-plate vocabulary:
- Roof connections (Fig.39-41): self-tapping screws or metal brackets, elevation views essentially identical to the wall-to-floor details above.
- Foundation connections, visible/exposed plates (5.2.5.1): exposed steel brackets and anchor bolts (Fig.42-43), with a damp-proof membrane recommended between CLT and concrete.
- Foundation connections, concealed hardware (5.2.5.2): concealed plates with tight-fit dowels or bolts set into machined grooves (Fig.44), better fire performance and appearance at the cost of more precise CNC machining.

Section 5.3 (mixed CLT construction, PDF p.263-265) covers CLT combined with other structural materials:
- 5.3.1.1 Platform construction with mixed wood systems: CLT walls carrying a conventional joisted floor (sawn lumber, glulam, SCL or wood I-joist) via a continuous rimboard and blocking between joists (Fig.45-46).
- 5.3.1.2 Balloon construction with mixed wood systems: joists attached to the side of a continuous CLT wall via metal hangers or ledgers (Fig.47).
- 5.3.2 CLT mixed with steel or concrete: open-web steel joists bearing directly on CLT walls through cut openings, common in non-residential roofs (Fig.48).

Section 5.4 (analysis and design of connections, PDF p.266-278) sets the capacity-design framework:
- Sec.5.4.1-5.4.2 (lateral and withdrawal resistance, PDF p.266-269): dowel-type fasteners are checked separately depending on whether they are driven perpendicular to the panel face or into the panel edge, because unglued cross-layer gaps and the CLT-specific "JX" embedment adjustment factor change the effective strength versus an equivalent solid-sawn member.
- Dissipative connections must fail in a yielding mode (per CSA O86 yielding modes (d), (e) or (g)), be at least moderately ductile (ductility ratio 3.0 or more, EEEP method per ASTM E2126), and possess enough deformation capacity for the intended rocking; using many small-diameter fasteners rather than few large ones is recommended specifically to force the desired yielding failure mode rather than a brittle wood-crushing failure.
- Sec.5.4.6 (fastener placement, PDF p.275-277): Table 2 (bolts/dowels in the panel edge) and Table 3 (nails/screws in the panel edge) give minimum fastener spacing, end distance and edge distance as multiples of fastener diameter dF (see quick reference table above for the bolt/dowel values); Figure 55 (PDF p.276) shows the geometry these symbols refer to.
- Sec.5.4.7 (detailing, PDF p.278): shrinkage/swelling of CLT with seasonal humidity changes must be accounted for at connections, especially where sealants or membranes are also present (Fig.56, acoustic membrane between wall and floor plates; Fig.57, ratchet/hook devices used to pull panels tight on site, cross-referenced to Chapter 12).

Section 5.4.5 (ductility, PDF p.273-277) defines the ductility ratio as the displacement at ultimate (or peak) load divided by the displacement at yield (Figure 52) per the ASTM E2126 Equivalent Energy Elastic-Plastic (EEEP) method, and shows measured hysteresis loops for a light-gauge steel bracket with 18 spiral nails (d=4.2 mm, L=89 mm) loaded in uplift (Figure 53) and in sliding (Figure 54).

## Chapter 6: Duration of load and creep factors for cross-laminated timber panels (PDF pp. 299-314)

Short chapter explaining why CLT needs its own duration-of-load and creep treatment: its cross-laminated, glued construction makes it more prone to time-dependent (creep) deformation under sustained load than glulam, because of the orthogonal layer arrangement and the added deformation contributed by rolling shear in the transverse layers. CSA O86 handles duration of load with the same KD factor used elsewhere in wood design (short term up to 7 days, 1.15; standard term such as snow or occupancy load, 1.00; long term, essentially continuous, 0.65, Table 1, PDF p.304), with an interpolation equation, KD = 1.0 - 0.50 log(PL/PS) (not below 0.65), for cases where the specified long-term load PL exceeds the specified standard-term load PS. Creep itself is folded into the total out-of-plane deflection calculation via a creep factor (Kcreep = 2.0 for dry service conditions in the Chapter 13 worked examples) applied to the permanent-load component of deflection, separately from the live/snow-load component which is not multiplied up. The chapter discusses parameters that influence how much a given CLT panel creeps: adhesive type and stiffness, whether lamination edges are glued (edge-gluing reduces gap-related extra deformation) and the presence of manufacturing relief grooves. It flags that mechanically fastened (non-adhesive) CLT is outside the scope of both CSA O86 and this Handbook, and that such products may deflect and creep significantly more than glued CLT, so this Handbook's numbers should not be applied to nailed-only or dowelled-only CLT products.

## Chapter 7: Vibration performance of cross-laminated timber floors and tall wood buildings (PDF pp. 315-362)

Presents FPInnovations' vibration-controlled design method for bare CLT floors, since lightweight-joist vibration methods do not transfer to CLT's different mass and stiffness (CLT floors are heavier than joisted wood floors but lighter than concrete slabs). The method (now in CSA O86, developed from the 2011 first-edition method with revisions) calculates a maximum vibration-controlled span directly from the panel's effective bending stiffness in the major direction and its mass, without iteration (an improvement on the first-edition method, which needed iteration), then applies an empirical approach to account for multiple spans and for non-structural elements (toppings, ceilings, partition walls, finishes) that add damping and stiffness beyond the bare panel. Chapter 13's design example uses the specific numeric form of this rule: a maximum vibration-controlled span read directly off the Wood Design Manual's panel selection tables (5.46 m for 175 mm 5-ply E1, 6.82 m for 245 mm 7-ply E1), with a 20% span increase allowed for multi-span floors with partitions (capped at 8 m span) and a 10% span reduction if a concrete topping's area density is not at least double the CLT's own area density. A parallel design method is given for timber-concrete-composite (TCC) floors (developed from separate FPInnovations research), and a stiffness requirement for the beams that support CLT floors, more demanding than in the chapter's previous edition. Preliminary guidance for tall-building wind-induced vibration gives simple equations for the first two transverse natural frequencies of a wood building and recommended damping ratios; these equations reappear, applied numerically, in Chapter 13 section 13.9. The chapter deliberately keeps its main text to design procedures, scope statements and worked examples only, pushing the underlying science to two appendices (Appendix I, background theory; Appendix II, development and CSA O86 adoption history of the CLT floor method).

## Chapter 8: Fire performance of cross-laminated timber assemblies (PDF pp. 363-472)

Covers fire-resistance testing and design of CLT floor, wall and stair-shaft assemblies, both bare/exposed and gypsum-protected, conforming to the bi-national ANSI/APA PRG 320 manufacturing standard (CLT made outside that standard's species/density/adhesive requirements is explicitly out of scope for this chapter's numbers). Testing and methods covered:
- Component-level and system-level fire-resistance tests: bare panels, gypsum-clad panels, and full compartment (system-level) tests, showing CLT elements can achieve fire-resistance ratings beyond 3 hours in some cases, with or without gypsum board protection.
- The informative calculation method in Annex B of CSA O86 Update 1: a reduced-cross-section method based on a notional charring rate, plus a "zero-strength zone" beyond the visible char layer that has lost strength from heat but has not yet fully charred; and a refined stepped-charring model, first developed in the 2014 revision of this chapter and validated against test data.
- Surface flame spread testing, showing reduced ignition risk for mass timber versus conventional interior wood finish products.
- Fire-stopping details adapted from concrete construction, shown to be suitable for CLT with adequate detailing.
- Use of CLT for vertical exit stair shafts as an alternative to noncombustible construction, and guidance on incorporating CLT into a performance-based (alternative-solution) fire design.
- Fire safety measures during construction, before sprinklers and finishes are in place, when a building is at its most vulnerable to fire spread.
Chapter 13's fire-design worked examples (charring at 0.7 to 0.8 mm/min depending on member and exposure, giving 91 to 103 mm of lost cross-section per exposed face for a 2-hour rating, plus a fixed 7 mm zero-strength zone) apply this chapter's method directly to the glulam beams, glulam column, CLT floor and CLT wall of the design-example building, and are the clearest place to see the method's numbers actually plugged in.

## Chapter 9: Acoustic performance of cross-laminated timber assemblies (PDF pp. 473-520)

Covers noise control for CLT separating walls and floor/ceiling assemblies between dwelling units and between units and public corridors, stairs, elevator or service areas (exterior CLT walls and roofs are explicitly out of scope, and stepped-storey CLT buildings are only briefly touched due to lack of data). Explains Sound Transmission Class (STC, a single-number rating of direct transmission through the separating element only) versus Apparent Sound Transmission Class (ASTC, which also accounts for flanking transmission through the surrounding structure as actually perceived by occupants), noting the National Building Code replaced STC with ASTC as its rating metric in 2015; the chapter's first (2011) edition necessarily drew on European products and data since no Canadian CLT existed yet, while this updated edition is built on measured performance from actual Canadian CLT buildings. Gives a four-step systematic approach:
1. Understand the fundamentals of building acoustics (what sound is, how it travels through a solid medium, why it cannot cross a vacuum).
2. Know the principles of noise control (source, path, receiver).
3. Understand how construction details affect sound insulation performance (mass, decoupling, damping, absorption at each assembly).
4. Develop a strategy for a given project's noise-control targets.
It also gives a library of design examples for CLT walls and floor/ceiling assemblies meeting a range of targets from minimum code to high-demand occupancies, along with a summary of how various design parameters and construction details generally affect sound insulation; trade names of finish, membrane, topping and sound-absorptive products are used in the examples purely for accuracy, without endorsement, since no generic classification system yet exists for them. It notes that mid- to high-rise CLT wall design (which must also carry higher axial and lateral structural loads) differs meaningfully from low-rise wall design, whereas CLT floor acoustic design does not change much with building height. Chapter 13's design example targets ASTC 47 / STC 50 / IIC less than or equal to 55 for a student-residence occupancy, with a floor build-up of carpet or luxury vinyl tile, a sound membrane, 38 mm concrete topping, an acoustic underlayment mat and 175 mm 5-ply CLT with an exposed ceiling.

## Chapter 10: Building enclosure design of cross-laminated timber construction (PDF pp. 521-585)

Covers heat, air and moisture control for the building enclosure (envelope) of CLT construction in Canadian climates, drawing on research, laboratory and field testing and years of project experience with CLT in wall, roof and floor applications across Canada and the US. The building enclosure is defined as the system of materials, components and assemblies that physically separates conditioned interior space from unconditioned exterior space, controlling heat flow, airflow and moisture (both water vapour and liquid water) in addition to its fire and acoustic separation roles covered elsewhere in the Handbook; a CLT panel may itself be part of that enclosure system, or may sit entirely inside it, protected by a separate envelope. The chapter gives a primer on relevant building code requirements and the building science of heat, vapour, air and moisture control specific to CLT walls and roofs, then covers CLT wall and roof enclosure design and detailing options, and finishes with strategies for addressing construction moisture (CLT is manufactured dry but is exposed to weather on site before the envelope closes it in), in-service moisture, and preservative treatment where required for long-term durability. Illustrated with the Wood Innovation and Design Centre (Prince George, BC, the first tall mass timber building in North America, designed by Michael Green Architecture) as a built example of CLT beams integrated into roof and floor assemblies (Figure 1, PDF p.526).

## Chapter 11: Environmental performance of cross-laminated timber (PDF pp. 586-638)

Organised in three sections. Section 1 (comparative life cycle assessment, PDF p.591 onward) presents a life cycle assessment (LCA) case study comparing a 4-storey CLT apartment building against a functionally equivalent building with a concrete slab-and-column structure and light-gauge steel stud walls, using the international ISO 14040/14044 LCA framework to evaluate global warming, acidification and smog potential across the product life cycle (manufacture, use, disposal); the CLT building shows a reduction in life cycle greenhouse gas emissions, consistent with other published case studies, and the section closes with a discussion of climate effects of wood use (carbon storage duration, substitution effects) not always addressed in current LCA practice. Section 2 addresses Canadian wood supply: the gap between the annual allowable cut and current harvesting levels, the availability of certified wood products, and a mass-balance framework for exploring where the wood supply for expanded CLT production could come from (the forest itself, other wood-product markets, or resource efficiency gains in forest management, processing, manufacturing, consumption, reuse and recycling). Section 3 summarises indoor air emissions test results from CLT samples, showing CLT panels can meet the most stringent indoor air quality standards. The chapter cross-references FPInnovations' separate "Technical Guide for the Design and Construction of Tall Wood Buildings in Canada" for broader building-level environmental and occupant-health guidance beyond CLT specifically.

## Chapter 12: Lifting and handling of CLT elements (PDF pp. 639-708)

The construction-systems and erection chapter, closely relevant to sequencing and rigging a CLT model. Section 12.1.1 (PDF p.644) frames CLT erection as directly analogous to precast concrete panel erection (Figure 1, p.644, lifting and handling of precast concrete elements), and states that much of the technical guidance in the chapter is adapted from precast-concrete lifting-accessory manufacturers. Section 12.1.2 (PDF p.645-650) shows photographs of CLT elements actually being lifted by cableway, by helicopter, and by mobile crane on projects in Norway, Montreal, Quebec City, Lac Etchemin (Quebec), Vaxjo (Sweden) and Vancouver (Figures 2-10), establishing that no single lifting method dominates in practice.

Section 12.2 (slinging and fastening systems, PDF p.651-666) catalogues three families of lifting hardware with figures for each: contact lifting systems (slings with or without a support block, through drilled holes or around the panel, Fig.11-23); screw hoist systems (screwed anchors for wall or floor use, Fig.24-25; screwed plate and lifting ring, Fig.26; double-threaded socket with eyelet bolt, Fig.27; an innovative wood-screw-and-eyelet-bolt system with or without a recessed pocket, Fig.28); and lifting systems using an inserted steel rod with a soft sling (Fig.29), a lifting hook (Fig.30), or a friction system (Fig.31).

Section 12.3 (general principles, PDF p.671-683) is the calculation section: weight is found from W = V x rho with rho taken as 4 to 6 kN/m3 for softwood CLT (density range 320-720 kg/m3 depending on species and moisture, versus about 2400 kg/m3 for precast concrete); a worked example gives a 2.4 x 16 x 0.3 m slab a weight of about 6000 kg (60 kN) (Fig.32, PDF p.673). Table 1 (dynamic acceleration factors, PDF p.674) ranges from 1.1-1.3 for a fixed crane up to 3-4+ for lifting and moving on rugged ground; wind loading on the lifted panel and use of tag/guide ropes to prevent rotation are also flagged, and it is recommended that each lift be performed in a single operation as the engineer intended. Table 2 (coefficient of lifting angle, PDF p.676) tabulates the 1/cos(alpha) multiplier for sling inclination from 0 to 60 degrees, with a strong recommendation to keep the cable angle from vertical under 30 degrees. Section 12.3.4 (asymmetrical load distribution, PDF p.675) gives the two-anchor force-splitting equations used when the centre of gravity is not centred between the anchors. Section 12.3.6 (number of effective anchors, PDF p.677-683) recommends designing two-anchor lifts (N=2) as the default even when three or four slings are physically used, because load distribution among more than two points cannot be guaranteed exactly symmetric, and recommends N=1 (every anchor sized for the full load) when the load or its centre of gravity is not precisely known; Figures 34-41 (PDF p.678-681) show good- and bad-practice sling layouts for walls and slabs with symmetric or asymmetric centres of gravity, with and without spreader beams.

Section 12.4 (other accessories, PDF p.684-692) covers site hardware beyond the lifting slings themselves:
- Fire-resistant fibrous caulking rope and joint-sealing tapes at floor/wall/connector joints (Fig.47-50), also relevant to Chapter 9's acoustic sealing between panels.
- Adjustable steel shoring for temporary wall/column support before permanent connections are complete (Fig.51).
- Beam-grip ratchet-and-hook or ratchet-and-screw-plate devices used to pull panel joints tight during erection (Fig.52-53).
- Manual winches with soft slings for fine positioning (Fig.54).
- Steel shims with non-shrink cement grout at the wall-to-foundation junction, closing the gap between a level concrete slab tolerance and the CLT wall base (Fig.55).

Section 12.5 (transportation, PDF p.693-700) gives road transport envelopes for a CLT load:
- Dimension limits (12.5.1.1): US road limits of 4.11 m height, 2.6 m width and 16.15 m (53 ft) semi-trailer length (Fig.57); a flatbed's deck sits about 1.51 m off the ground at its highest (front) point, so a load box of 2.6 x 2.6 x 16.15 m is the practical maximum on a standard flatbed. Drop-deck and double-drop-deck trailers allow taller loads (up to 3.0 m and 3.56 m load height respectively, Fig.58-59) at the cost of a non-continuous deck.
- Weight limits (12.5.1.2-12.5.1.3): vary by Canadian province and by axle configuration; typical tare weights are 13.0 t for a 5-axle unit, 14.5 t for 6-axle, 16 t for a tri-drive 7-axle unit, with Table 3 (PDF p.697) giving maximum payloads by jurisdiction. An 8-axle B-train combination can run at 62.5 t GVW but its two articulated trailers cannot share a single panel, so the longest panel it can carry is about 9.75 m, the length of its lead trailer deck (Fig.56).
- Oversize/overweight permitting (12.5.2): limited to indivisible loads, generally restricted to daylight-hours travel, requiring special markings/banners and, on some routes, an escort vehicle.
- Construction-site access (12.5.3): off-tracking of long trailers on tight turns is a function of the sum of the squares of the vehicle's wheelbases, so an extra-long trailer needs proportionally more turning clearance than a short one; on-site space for storage and manoeuvring should be checked before ordering oversized loads.

Section 12.6 (positioning on site, PDF p.700-706) covers storing panels on skids at least 150 mm above grade, wrapping bundles on all six faces with a slit at the bottom to let trapped moisture escape, stacking to match the planned erection sequence to minimise re-handling, and temporary weatherproof tarping/tenting during construction (Fig.61-64). Section 12.7 (references, PDF p.707-708) lists the precast-concrete and CLT-specific lifting literature the chapter draws on, useful if a modelling agent needs to trace a specific hardware detail back to its manufacturer source.

## Chapter 13: Design example (PDF pp. 709-812)

A complete worked example: an 8-storey residential mass timber building in Ottawa, Ontario, concrete at the first storey with seven storeys of CLT and glulam above. Section 13.1 (PDF p.714-716) gives the building's geometry: floor plate 15.2 m x 50 m, repeated on every level, nine structural grid bays at 6.1 m on-centre (short direction) by 7.0 m (long direction, from the column example in 13.6.7), two stair cores and one elevator core, storey height 3.6 m for the ground (concrete) storey and 3.0 m floor-to-floor for the seven timber storeys, total height 24.6 m, building oriented north-south along its short axis. The gravity system is CLT floor/roof panels spanning one-way between glulam beam lines on a post-and-beam glulam frame (double continuous beams in the north-south direction), with the elevator/stair core walls and three additional CLT walls (two east-west, one north-south) also carrying gravity load. The lateral system is CLT core and shear walls resisting seismic and wind loads through rocking, floor/roof diaphragms modelled as semi-rigid, continuous steel rod or discrete hold-down tie-downs, and a concrete podium with conventional concrete shear walls. Soil is assumed bedrock (500 kPa SLS / 1000 kPa ULS bearing), Site Class C. Materials: CLT grade E1 (PRG 320 / CSA O86-14 Update 2), glulam beams D.Fir-L 24f-EX, glulam columns D.Fir-L 16c-E.

Section 13.4 (loads, PDF p.717-722) tabulates dead, live and snow loads floor by floor:
- Ground floor (concrete storey, retail/wholesale): dead load 4.8 kPa (finishes, partitions, 150 mm concrete slab-on-grade), live load 8.4 kPa (retail) plus 0.25 kPa exits, totalling 9.85 kPa.
- Second floor (concrete transfer slab): dead load 4.8 kPa (350 mm concrete suspended slab plus finishes, partitions, mechanical/electrical allowance), live load 1.9 kPa residential plus 4.8 kPa exits.
- Floors 3 to 8 (CLT floor): dead load 3.44 kPa, made up of 0.20 kPa finishes and sound insulation, 1.00 kPa partition load, 0.91 kPa 38 mm concrete topping, 0.88 kPa 175 mm CLT floor slab at 515 kg/m3 density, 0.20 kPa mechanical/electrical allowance; live load 1.9 kPa residential plus 4.8 kPa exits.
- Roof: dead load 1.76 kPa (0.43 kPa roofing/insulation/finishes, 0.88 kPa 175 mm CLT roof slab, 0.20 kPa glulam post-and-beam self-weight, 0.25 kPa mechanical/electrical allowance); snow load S = Is[Ss(Cb x Cw x Cs x Ca) + Sr] = 1.0[2.4(0.8x1.0x1.0x1.0) + 0.4] = 2.32 kPa (snow drifting ignored in this example); minimum roof live load 1.0 kPa or a 1.3 kN point load over a 200 x 200 mm area, whichever governs.
Wind loads (section 13.4.2) use hourly reference wind pressure q50 = 0.41 kPa for Ottawa and the NBC's frequency-dependent choice of static, dynamic or wind-tunnel procedure (the static procedure applies above 1 Hz fundamental frequency, estimated here by Rayleigh's method); the resulting per-floor wind loading is tabulated separately for the north-south and east-west directions. Earthquake loads (section 13.4.3) use Ottawa's design spectral acceleration values (Sa(0.2)=0.439, Sa(0.5)=0.237, Sa(1.0)=0.118, Sa(2.0)=0.056, PGA=0.281, PGV=0.196), a calculated seismic weight per floor, and an equivalent-static-force-procedure load distribution, with torsional sensitivity checked separately for each orthogonal direction.

Section 13.6 (gravity design, PDF p.732-750) sizes each element in turn, each example annotated with the governing code clause and a utilisation ratio:
- Roof CLT panel, simply supported single span 6.1 m: 175 mm 5-ply E1 satisfies bending (29% utilised), shear (32%) and deflection.
- Roof CLT panel, two-span continuous 12.2 m: same 175 mm panel, 28-38% utilised.
- Floor CLT panel, simply supported single span 6.1 m: the 175 mm panel fails the total-deflection check (span/150 actual versus span/180 limit), so the handbook recommends floors be built two-span continuous if 175 mm CLT is used.
- Floor CLT panel, two-span continuous 12.2 m: 175 mm panel passes strength (37% bending, 51% shear) and, after checking the CSA O86 vibration-controlled span method (Chapter 7) with the 20% multi-span/partition bonus, passes vibration too, provided the concrete topping area density stays under twice the CLT's own area density.
- Glulam roof beam, two-span continuous, 15.2 m long, 6.1 m tributary width: 175 x 570 mm D.Fir-L 24f-EX.
- Glulam floor beam: sized similarly (13.6.6, not detailed further here).
- Glulam column at grid D/2, second storey: 365 x 342 mm D.Fir-L 16c-E, tributary area 42.7 m2 with a live-load reduction factor of 0.78, factored load 1914 kN against a factored resistance of 2237 kN (86% utilised).
- CLT wall at the stair core (grid G+), first CLT storey: a 245 mm 7-ply E1 wall (Aeff = 140x10^3 mm2/m) carries 77 kN/m factored axial load against 2140 kN/m capacity, combined axial-plus-eccentric-moment interaction ratio 0.10; the handbook notes that a thinner 105 mm 3-ply V2 wall (Cr=423 kN, Mr=16 kNm at 3 m unsupported height) would suffice for gravity alone but not for the wall's other (lateral/fire) duties.

Section 13.7 (fire design, PDF p.751-760) reduces each member's cross-section for a notional charring rate (0.70 to 0.80 mm/min depending on species and exposure) plus a fixed 7 mm zero-strength zone, checked against a 2-hour fire-resistance requirement:
- Glulam roof/floor beam (13.7.2, exposed on three sides): needs 315 x 646 mm bare, versus 175 x 570 mm sized for strength alone; a smaller beam is possible if protected by the CLT deck above and a gypsum membrane below.
- Glulam column (13.7.3, exposed on four sides): the largest catalogue column size, 365 x 456 mm, still fails the 2-hour check; a custom 365 x 532 mm section is needed.
- CLT floor (13.7.4, exposed on the underside): the 175 mm 5-ply panel needs one layer of 15.9 mm Type X gypsum board to reach 2 hours; without gypsum, a 245 mm 7-ply panel is required for an exposed ceiling.
- CLT wall (13.7.5, exposed on one side): a 245 mm 7-ply panel is required with no gypsum protection; neither a 105 mm nor a 175 mm panel can resist any compression force at a 3000 mm effective height once charred for 2 hours.
Section 13.7.6 (discussion) draws out the general lesson: charring has a major impact on the section size needed for a 2-hour rating with no added protection, connections supporting gravity loads need the same fire rating as the elements they support (steel located inside the reduced wood cross-section is considered adequately protected), and gypsum board encapsulation is usually more economical than oversizing the wood section.

Section 13.8 (lateral design, PDF p.761-770) confirms Rd less than or equal to 2.0 and Ro = 1.5 for platform-type CLT construction per CSA O86-14 Update 2, describes the two-stage analysis approach used because the concrete podium is roughly ten times stiffer than the wood storeys above (period ratio check per ASCE 7-16 Clause 12.2.3.3), and works a linear dynamic (modal response spectrum) analysis of the wood portion. ## Rules for modelling

This is a reference/design guide, not a step-by-step procedural source, so the rules below are organised as a checklist for sizing and detailing a CLT building rather than a build sequence.

1. Build every CLT panel from an odd number of layers (3, 5 or 7 typical), each layer 16 to 51 mm thick, laid orthogonally; keep the two outer layers on the same axis to define the panel's major strength direction (Ch.1, PDF p.21).
2. Default to 105 mm (3-ply), 175 mm (5-ply) or 245 mm (7-ply) total thickness for buildings; do not exceed 508 mm without checking the manufacturer can produce it (Ch.1/2, PDF p.21, 77).
3. Orient wall panels with the major (outer-layer) axis vertical, parallel to gravity load; orient floor and roof panels with the major axis parallel to the main span (Ch.1, PDF p.21).
4. Keep panel widths to a manufacturer's standard (0.6, 1.2, 2.4 or 3 m) and lengths under about 18 m; check the tighter of production and highway transport limits (2.6 m width, 16.15 m length for a standard semi-trailer) before committing to a panel size (Ch.1/12, PDF p.21, 696).
5. Design floor and roof panels one-way by default; only model two-way action if the panel is at least 5-ply, the span ratio is 2.0 or less, and it is genuinely supported on multiple sides (Ch.3, PDF p.129).
6. Size wall panels for axial load using only the layers whose lamination runs parallel to the load; a 3-ply wall effectively wastes its middle layer for vertical load (Ch.3, PDF p.130).
7. Keep CLT shear wall (or wall segment) aspect ratio between 2:1 and 4:1 if the model wants to show full ductile/rocking behaviour; below 2:1 or above 4:1, treat the wall as a lower-ductility, higher-force element (Ch.4, PDF p.188).
8. Model shear wall segments as jointed at their vertical edges (spline, half-lap or screwed connection) so each segment can rock independently; model hold-downs at the wall base carrying uplift only, and angle brackets carrying both shear and uplift (Ch.4/5, PDF p.189-192).
9. Choose a connection type by application: splines (internal, single or double surface) or half-laps for panel-to-panel in-plane joints; self-tapping screws or metal brackets for perpendicular corners; self-tapping screws, brackets or proprietary clip systems for wall-to-floor; brackets or exposed/concealed plates with anchor bolts for wall-to-foundation (Ch.5, PDF p.233-262).
10. Space bolts/dowels in a CLT panel edge at least 3 dF apart (both directions), with end distance up to max(4 dF, 50 mm) and edge distance 1.5 dF (5 dF on a loaded edge); use finer spacing multiples for nails/screws per Table 3 (Ch.5, PDF p.276-277).
11. Build a platform-type storey stack as: concrete podium or foundation, then repeating wall-panel-plus-floor-panel storeys (wall height equal to one storey), with hold-down/tie-rod continuity carried down to the podium; use balloon framing (continuous multi-storey wall panels with floors ledger- or bracket-hung off the side) only where tall, uninterrupted walls are wanted, e.g. cores (Ch.4/5, PDF p.176, 253-257).
12. When rigging a lift, use two effective anchor points by default even if more slings are shown, keep sling angle from vertical under 30 degrees, and size for a CLT density of 4-6 kN/m3 (400-600 kg/m3) times panel volume (Ch.12, PDF p.672-674).
13. Stage panel delivery and storage to match the erection sequence; store on skids at least 150 mm off the ground, oriented to the same face-up direction they will be installed (Ch.12, PDF p.700).
14. When laying out a full building, reproduce the Chapter 13 pattern as a reasonable default: concrete first storey (taller, e.g. 3.6 m), then repeating 3.0 m timber storeys on a roughly 6 to 7 m structural grid, CLT floor/roof panels one-way on a glulam post-and-beam frame, CLT core and perimeter shear walls sized 175 to 245 mm, with wall thickness stepping down at upper storeys (Ch.13, PDF p.714, 746-750, drawing S102).
15. For fire-rated exposed timber, reduce every exposed face of a member by a notional char depth (rate around 0.7 to 0.8 mm/min for a 2-hour rating) plus a 7 mm zero-strength zone before checking the reduced section for strength; gypsum board encapsulation can substitute for extra wood thickness (Ch.8/13, PDF p.751-760).
16. Classify every connection as either dissipative (ductile, expected to yield: vertical joints between shear wall segments, wall-to-floor/foundation uplift connections) or non-dissipative (capacity-protected, must stay elastic: perpendicular-wall joints, floor-panel joints, floor/roof-to-wall-below joints), and size the non-dissipative ones to the 95th-percentile resistance of the dissipative ones, or to RdRo = 1.3 (Ch.4/5/13, PDF p.182-187, 771).
17. Where two panels can be joined either loosely (independent rocking segments) or rigidly (angled, closely spaced screws, one combined segment), pick based on the aspect ratio the assembled wall needs to hit; a loose joint keeps each narrower sub-panel's own aspect ratio, a rigid joint combines them into one wider, lower-aspect-ratio segment (Ch.4, PDF p.189).
18. Step down wall and floor panel thickness with height in a tall building rather than using one thickness throughout: the design example uses 245 mm CLT walls at its lower CLT storeys and 175 mm at upper storeys, and 175 mm floor/roof panels throughout with a 245 mm panel substituted only where fire or vibration governs (Ch.13, drawing S102, PDF p.793).
