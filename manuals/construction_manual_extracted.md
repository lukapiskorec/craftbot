# Construction Manual of Prefabricated Timber House: extracted notes

**Original PDF:** `manuals/Construction Manual Of Prefabricated Timber House.pdf` (gitignored, not in the repo). If the file is missing, download it from https://www.itto.int/files/user/pdf/publications/PD12%2087/pd-12-87%20e.pdf and save it under that name.

**Source:** Construction Manual of Prefabricated Timber House, FRIM Technical Information Handbook No. 5, by Mohd Shukari Midon, Chu Yue Pun, Hilmi Md Tahir and Nor Azian Mohd Kasby. Forest Research Institute Malaysia (FRIM), produced under the ITTO funded project "Research on the Utilization of Tropical Timber in Construction", 1996. ISSN 0127-9793, ISBN 983-9592-55-6. 60 pages as counted by the PDF, comprising front matter, preface and acknowledgements, 7 chapters, a references list and a 9 sheet technical drawing appendix.

**Scope of source:** step by step construction of a prefabricated, low cost, single storey timber framed house built by the modular panel system, for Malaysia and climates like it (not suitable for very strong winds or hurricanes). Covers fabrication systems, the design reasoning behind the platform frame method, material specification, a full construction sequence from footings to painting, and maintenance. Does not give structural design calculations or load tables, span tables beyond the one worked house, alternative roof shapes (only the single gable truss design is drawn), or nailing schedules beyond what is shown on the drawings.

**Most useful for:** sizing and sequencing a small platform frame timber house built from prefabricated wall, floor and roof panels: wall panel and truss member sizes, panel erection order, and the appendix drawings for exact truss and panel geometry.

## Chapter 1: Introduction

There are already a number of Malaysian factories making prefabricated timber houses using proprietary systems. This manual proposes an open, non-proprietary method instead, where parts may be assembled in the factory or on site in a do-it-yourself manner. It is the result of FRIM research into timber strength, timber joints, wall panels and roof trusses, and it names the system used here the Modular Panel System.

Not all component dimensions are given in the running text. Detailed dimensions are in the drawings, in Appendix I. The house is designed for Malaysia and similar climates, but not for places with very strong winds or hurricanes. Room partitions can be rearranged to suit individual preferences without affecting structural strength, because they are designed as non-load-bearing walls.

---

## Chapter 2: Fabrication systems (overview)

1. Pre-cut system, the oldest: timber cut, notched and drilled at the factory, marked, bundled and assembled on site. More accurate than site cutting, minimal waste.
2. Modular panel system (adopted in this manual, fig. 1): components built in a factory by 2 to 3 men without cranes. Uniform wall panels sized on module M = 1.22 m (4 ft) to tally with Malaysian cladding sheets (plywood, hardboard, chipboard, cement board). Exploded view: floor joists, then floor panels, then wall panels, then ceiling panels, then roof truss.
3. Large size panel system (fig. 2): panels up to 10 to 12 m, a dwelling length, factory-assembled. Good for mass building many small houses, fewer site joints, but higher transport and handling cost and cranes needed.
4. Volume element system (fig. 3): dwellings completely finished at the factory on foundation beams, buyer sees the finished house beforehand, fastest site erection but needs trailers and cranes.

---

## Chapter 3: Design of the house

- Traditional Malaysian construction is post and beam (beams 2 to 4 m apart on posts, non-load-bearing infill panels). The manual's house is instead timber-framed (wood-framed), traditional in North America and Scandinavia, comparatively new in Malaysia.
- Two timber-frame methods:
  - Balloon frame (fig. 4): studs run the full building height, intermediate floor joists side-fixed to studs. Only for 2 to 4 storey buildings.
  - Platform frame (fig. 5), used here: shell built one storey at a time with storey-height wall panels, each floor becomes the construction platform for the next storey or roof.
- Wall panel make-up (fig. 6): top and bottom horizontal plates spaced for the designed height (2745 mm, 9 ft), vertical studs nailed between them at 610 mm centres (the text's "0.16 m centres" at this point is a misprint, 610 mm is used everywhere else), noggings added to strengthen sheathing edges, sheets fixed one or both sides. All framework butt-jointed and nailed. Panels no more than 3000 mm long for man-handling. Panels erected over the prepared platform, roof placed over to complete the building.
- Advantages of timber-framed construction: simple transport of pre-assembled panels; less site labour, more saved if factory-assembled; panels erected manually by 2 to 3 men, no cranes; speed, all wall panels of a house up in 2 to 3 days, once roofed, plumbing and electrical proceed in the dry; low cost with high thermal insulation; flexible design, modification and extension.

## Chapter 4: Basic principles

- Modular dimensioning gives the best use of sheet materials and eases later extension. Local sheets are 1220 by 2440 mm, so planning uses multiples of 610 mm, a 610 mm grid governs plan layout and the positioning and fixing of partitions, roofs and joists (fig. 7).
- Example plan (fig. 7): proposed house 50 sq m, 7320 mm frontage by 5490 mm, with a kitchen, WC and bath wing 4270 mm wide by 2440 mm deep at the rear. A future extension of 3050 mm (bedroom 3) brings it to 63 sq m. Rooms: BR1, BR2, living or dining, verandah, kitchen, bath, WC.
- Load path (fig. 8): unlike post and beam, the wall panels are load-bearing. Roof trusses (at 1220 mm centres), studs (at 610 mm centres) and floor joists (at 610 mm centres) are kept vertically in line for efficient load transfer from roof to studs to joists to ground. The section shows, top to bottom: roof truss, head binder, top plate, vertical stud, nogging, lintel, cripple stud, door, bottom plate, base plate, floor board, floor joist, bearer.
- Load-bearing versus non-load-bearing walls (fig. 9): load-bearing walls support the floor or roof above. Non-load-bearing walls are partitions only, made shorter than load-bearing walls in the same storey so the roof or floor joists above do not rest on them, leaving a clearance at the top.
- Openings: doors and windows need a lintel spanning the opening, with the ends carried on cripple studs at each side (fig. 8). Small openings need no lintel, they are simply framed between studs.
- Panel-making tolerances:
  1. Stud depth must be uniform throughout so the finished panel-with-sheathing width stays constant (for example, for 47 by 97 studs, the 97 mm dimension must be accurate or regularised).
  2. A 1 to 3 mm vertical gap is left between plywood sheathings for expansion, covered with beading.
  3. The finished length of each prefabricated panel is 1 to 3 mm less than the drawing size.
- Detached or linked houses: houses may be linked by a separating wall (fig. 10, grid 305 mm, minimum 305 mm separating wall zone). The empty space between neighbouring houses acts as a firebreak, with minimum spacing set by the Uniform Building By-laws 1984.

### Sequence of construction (summary from Ch. 4)

- Main building at the front on a raised platform 700 mm above ground, kitchen at the back on ground level concrete. Floor to ceiling 2745 mm (9 ft). Perspective in fig. 11.
- Order: concrete footings, then posts on footings, then bearers bolted across posts, then floor joists with stiffening cross pieces on bearers, then tongue and groove floor boards conceal-nailed, platform now ready. Then pre-assembled wall panels are erected over the platform (kitchen, on ground level and not connected to the platform, can proceed at the same time), then head binders or wall plates bind panel tops and support trusses, then panels are fixed sideways with nailed iron plates or angles, concealed with beading, then prefabricated trussed rafter roofs go on the wall plates, rafters braced diagonally, purlins nailed, then roofing sheets (building now weathertight), then doors and windows, then plumbing, wiring and sanitation, then paint, then occupy.

## Chapter 5: General requirements and specification of materials

### Timber

- Strength Group B per Malaysian Standard M.S. 544:1978 (SIRIM).
- Grade: Standard structural grade per Malaysian Grading Rules (MGR) Part III Section J.
- Sizes: as drawings. Exposed surfaces planed, unexposed sawn unless regularised (for example the 97 mm of 47 by 97 studs must be fixed and accurate).
- Moisture content at installation: floor boards 18 percent or less, all other timber 22 percent or less.
- Preservative treatment: pressure impregnated with CCA to a dry salt retention of 5.6 kg per cubic metre, CCA penetration at least 12 mm.

### Panels and boards

- Plywood: from a reputable mill, water-boil-proof (WBP) or moisture-resistant (MR) type depending on position in the plan.
- Cement-bonded particleboard (cemboard) for the kitchen, fixed per manufacturer's instructions.
- All other materials per good construction practice.

## Chapter 6: Details of construction

### Site access

Check or construct vehicle access, especially in remote areas. Electric power (mains or generator) and water (mains, well, river, or transported) are still needed on site. Allocate storage space for prefabricated components away from the building area.

### Preparation of site

Examine the soil (a timber house is light, so most normal soils are fine). If termite-infested, apply anti-termite soil treatment before construction.

Clear vegetation, remove roots and cellulosic material during grading, ensure good drainage. The frontal (platform) part may sit on level ground or a gentle slope, level the platform by adjusting footing level or post length (fig. 12). Floor at least 700 mm above ground for ventilation. Kitchen area at the rear levelled by cut and fill with proper ramming.

### Setting out

Do not build too close to the boundary (for circulation, and because a gap to neighbours acts as a firebreak, minimum spacing per Uniform Building By-laws 1984). Locate near one end of a large plot to allow future extension (fig. 13).

Drive temporary pegs at the four platform corners, then set out with batter boards, 2 per corner, 8 total, each of two vertical stakes 50 by 100 mm by about 1 m driven at least 1 m beyond the footing lines, with a horizontal board 25 by 150 mm nailed on. All board tops are levelled, using a surveyor's level or a transparent water-filled hose (fig. 15), the highest corner is the datum.

Strings are stretched between boards, positions marked with a nail or saw kerf so strings can be replaced. Two adjoining sides are set with steel tapes, squareness checked with the diagonal (sides 7230 and 5400 mm give a diagonal of the square root of 7230 squared plus 5400 squared, that is 9096 mm), or by the 3-4-5 rule (fig. 16, plumb bob to a nail in a stake at the first corner). Fix three corners, establish the fourth, check both diagonals.

Intermediate footing points are marked with steel tape and plumb bob, always measuring from corners along the string. All 12 footing positions set (fig. 14, grid of 3 by 2440 by 2 by 2700 centre lines of footings).

### Footings

Remove strings, keep batter boards for later alignment and levelling.

Excavate 12 holes, 600 by 600 mm, 400 mm deep, ram hardcore to 150 mm thick, blind with a level of sand.

Set up formwork for concrete, footing depth at least 300 mm (more on sloping contours if all footing tops are kept level, or vary post lengths instead so footing tops need not be level). Footing top at least 50 mm above ground.

Before pouring: 12 mm mild steel bar reinforcement near the bottom, mild steel U-straps for posts set at the top, aligned and levelled from batter-board strings and plumb bobs (fig. 12(i)).

Concrete mix 1 to 2 or 3 to 4 (cement to sand to stone, sand quantity depends on wetness), preferably machine-mixed (thorough hand mixing is acceptable). Pour and rod or vibrate to a dense concrete without disturbing the reinforcement.

### Platform

Posts: a mild steel bearing plate 120 by 120 by 6 mm is placed inside the U-strap, posts 120 by 120 by 499 mm are dropped in, each fixed to the U-strap with 2 bolts through predrilled holes (fig. 17).

Bearers: one on each side of the post tops (2/60 by 194, that is, paired), 4 bolts through both bearers and the post, washers under bolt heads and nuts bearing on timber. Lengthened with splice pieces 2/25 by 194 by 300 between posts. Keep posts truly vertical and all bearer tops level and horizontal.

Joists: 47 by 145 mm at 610 mm centres on bearers, slant-nailed with 75 mm nails, one each side (fig. 18). Stiffening cross pieces against tilting: 50 by 50 at each end, 50 by 75 at the centre, seated in notches cut in the joist tops (fig. 19).

Floor boards: tongue and groove strip flooring laid across joists (Ch. 6 text says 30 by 145 mm, Table 1 and the drawings say 22 by 145 mm), groove fitted over tongue snugly and blind-nailed through the tongue at 40 to 50 degrees into the joist (fig. 20). Header joist 20 by 194 mm nailed to joist ends, top level with the floor boards, concealing the joist spaces (fig. 21).

Platform complete, kitchen construction can start separately or at the same time.

### Wall panels

Panels are pre-assembled at the factory (more accurate) or on site (pre-cut pieces save labour, assembled on a level surface such as a concrete slab or trestles).

Base or sill plates are fixed over the platform first: 47 by 112 (exterior), 47 by 84 (interior), to match panel widths (fig. 22 shows base plate layout with joints). Check positions and corner squareness, then nail to floor boards with 76 mm nails at 300 mm centres in a zigzag pattern.

Erection (fig. 23): the first panel is corner panel No. 1, skew-nail its bottom to the base plate, hold it upright with temporary diagonal braces (removed only once panels are permanently fixed). Then subsequent panels. 21 panels over the platform, 6 over the kitchen.

Panels are 2745 mm high, longest 2550 mm, so they are man-handled without cranes. Four temporary lifting handles are fixed about 1000 mm high near the ends of each double-sheathed panel, removed once in position.

Wall plates or head binders go over the panel tops: 47 by 112 on thicker exterior walls, 22 by 84 on thinner interior walls (the thin 22 mm plates keep the tops of non-load-bearing walls clear of the trusses). Nailed with 76 mm nails at 300 mm centres, zigzag.

Panel-to-base-plate fixing: Pryda knuckle nail plates 2N10 (89 mm wide by 63 mm deep, 2 rows of 10 punched-out nails) or similar, at 610 mm centres on the exterior side, interior side skew-nailed with 76 mm nails to the base plate. Metal plates and nails are covered by plywood skirting 9 by 100 mm.

Panel-to-panel (side) fixing: 3 Pryda 2N10 plates, evenly spaced. Corner (right-angle) fixing: 3 Pryda angle plates 3NA (44 by 44 by 95) on the interior angle. All plates covered with plywood beading (2/9 by 72, fig. 24 shows length-wise panels and corner panels, nail plates 63 by 89 at 610 mm spacing).

Erect kitchen walls before roof trusses, because the extended trusses of the main building rest on the rear kitchen wall.

### Kitchen

Area about 3.1 by 5.1 m behind the main building. Excavate to level, hardcore rammed to 100 mm, blind top with sand and wet liberally, pour 100 mm concrete floor, mix 1 to 2 to 4. Strip footings 150 mm wide by 50 mm thick additionally under wall-panel lines (fig. 25 drawing labels: concrete curb 50 by 115 for wall panels, concrete floor 100 mm, strip footing 200 by 100, slab 2443 by 4500).

Concrete curbs replace timber sole plates, because of the wet nature of the kitchen: 50 mm thick by 115 mm wide for thicker walls, 50 by 84 for thinner. Bolts 12 by 100 mm cast vertically into the curbs, 38 mm embedded, threads exposed, about 2 bolts per panel (fig. 26, anchor bolt at 1 m centres).

Kitchen panels are sheathed both sides with cement-bonded particleboard, 10 mm outside, 8 mm inside. Pre-assembly covers only the 10 mm side, elongated holes drilled in the bottom rail to match curb bolts, panel dropped over the bolts and tightened with nut and washer, the 8 mm inside sheet fixed in situ (predrill nail holes in the cemboard).

Wall plates on top, side and corner attachments with Pryda plates or equivalent, as for plywood panels.

### Roof trusses

Trussed rafters are preferably factory pre-assembled. Joints: nailed 9 mm WBP plywood gusset plates on both sides (per plan) or proprietary punched metal plates (then fabricated by the licensed factory, which also sizes the plates, timber member sizes unchanged). All members 35 by 72 mm.

Erection: the first truss goes up at one end, nailed to the wall plate, temporarily braced to the ground at two places with 50 by 100 mm timber (fig. 27), it must be firm, because all other trusses fasten back to it. The second truss is placed upside down with ends on opposite wall plates, peak hanging down, then tilted up with a long Y-ended pole (fig. 28), nailed to the wall plate, braced to the first truss, and the process repeats. Attachment: skew-nailed with 4 nails of 100 mm per truss-to-plate joint, two each side (fig. 29).

Gable ends (platform area) are closed with 6 mm plywood cut to size, roof-space ventilation is through holes drilled in these gable sheets.

Kitchen end: four trusses have extended rafters covering the kitchen, resting on the kitchen wall plate, two additional 35 by 72 mm beams between each pair of trusses stiffen the kitchen roof.

Manual erection uses 4 workers (one on each wall nailing, one at truss centre fixing the top, one general or lifting), a crane is faster if available.

Permanent bracing against wind sway: horizontal braces at right angles to trusses, diagonal braces at 45 degrees from the top of the gable-end truss to the bottom of the fourth truss (fig. 30, braces 22 by 97 mm). Temporary bracing is then removed.

Purlins 35 by 72 mm go on top of trusses, skew-nailed to each rafter with two 76 mm nails, one per side (fig. 31). Fascia and barge boards are fixed to rafter and purlin ends.

Roofing: corrugated asbestos-free fibre cement sheets are laid over purlins per manufacturer's instructions. Building now dry regardless of weather.

### Doors and windows

Openings are pre-formed in wall panels. Door openings keep a full-length bottom rail during handling (this prevents twisting), after erection the exposed rail portion is cut out together with the base plate at the opening (fig. 32).

Doors and windows are factory-assembled and delivered for installation. Door sizes: 700 mm (WC or bath), 770 mm (kitchen or rooms), 840 mm (main entrance), all 2100 mm high from floor level. Door and window dimensions must match the panel openings exactly. All doors open inwards.

Flush doors: the framework is assembled square, plywood glued and nailed on both sides.

Windows: frames are made on site in the panel openings. Adjustable aluminium louvre frames with 8 leaves are fixed to the wooden window frame, glass sheets cut to size slide into the louvre slots, glass sheets are also used as fixed glass louvres above the adjustable ones.

### Railings and stairs

Railings: rails 47 by 97 mm, vertical balustrades 20 by 20 mm, rail top at 1000 mm above floor.

Stairs, 2 off (entrance and kitchen): rise 170 mm, run 230 mm. Treads 47 by 250 mm rest both ends on ledgers 50 by 50 by 180 mm fixed to stringers 60 by 219 mm. Stringers lean against the floor joist at the top and fix to the concrete landing with angle-iron plates 63 by 63 by 200 mm at the bottom (fig. 33).

### Ceiling

Noggings 38 by 50 mm at 610 mm centres are fixed between roof trusses first (fig. 34). Ceiling is 6 mm plywood or 6 mm cement-bonded particleboard (Table 1 lists 4 mm MR plywood, 2 by 8 ft), nailed to noggings and trussed rafters in a zigzag pattern. Ceiling is in the main building only, not the kitchen.

### Services

Plumbing, electrical and sewerage work can start as soon as roofing sheets are on.

Wiring holes: 25 mm diameter, drilled at the centre of the section of wall frames and floor joists, at least 300 mm from the ends of studs or joists and from any other hole (fig. 35, electric socket fixed to 50 by 50 mm blocking).

Notches in joists are allowed only if: in the top edge; located between one tenth and one fifth of the span from the support; depth no more than 21 mm for a 145 mm joist. Otherwise drill holes for piping (fig. 36, holes 300 to 550 mm from the support, centred with 50, 25, 50 mm spacing on a 145 mm joist).

Fixtures fix to the panel framing timbers, add extra noggings for heavy fixtures such as wash basins or boilers.

### Finishing touches

Plywood skirtings 9 by 100 mm between wall and floor (main building), plywood beadings 9 by 72 mm cover all plywood joints.

Paint the whole building, for protection, cleaning and appearance, unfinished exposed wood weathers quickly. Floors: natural colour and grain are attractive, apply a floor finish against wear and abrasion. Building ready for occupation.

## Chapter 7: Maintenance and repair

Two enemies of the tropical timber house: termite attack and moisture or damp decay.

Raised platform: keep underfloor space well ventilated, no blocking by grasses, shrubs or stored items. No standing water under the building, soil grades away from the house. Keep tops of all concrete (footings, stair landing, kitchen floor) 50 to 100 mm above ground as designed. Inspect regularly for termite activity, break earthen termite tunnels (subterranean termites need soil-to-building contact), treat soil with insecticide if possible.

Exterior walls: no part should collect water (moisture leads to fungi and decay, common on door and window frames), seal shrinkage or workmanship cracks with putty, set rust spots at exposed non-corrosive nail heads below the surface and putty over, repaint deteriorated finishes.

Interior: fill cracks with putty and paint, do not let moisture linger on timber or plywood, especially in the kitchen. Sheathing damage: small holes are puttied and sanded, larger holes are cut out and a new section fitted. Floor-board shrinkage gaps (boards laid at high moisture content): glue matching timber strips into the gaps, or replace floor sections in severe cases.

Roof: ceiling stains indicate leaks, locate and repair. Maintain roof-space air circulation, keep gable-end ventilation openings provided and unblocked.

## Build sequence for procedural modelling

1. Grid: everything on a 610 mm module (M equals 1220 mm), studs and joists at 610 mm centres, trusses at 1220 mm centres, all vertically aligned.
2. Set out 12 footing positions (3 by 2440 by 2 by 2700 grid) with batter boards, square by diagonals.
3. Footings: 12 pads, hole 600 by 600 by 400, 150 mm hardcore, 300 mm or more concrete (1 to 2 to 4) topped 50 mm or more above ground, U-strap cast in.
4. Platform: posts 120 by 120 by 499 in U-straps, then paired bearers 2/60 by 194 bolted both sides of posts, then joists 47 by 145 at 610 mm slant-nailed, stiffeners 50 by 50 (ends) or 50 by 75 (centre) in notches, then tongue and groove floor 22 by 145 blind-nailed, then header joist 20 by 194. Platform level 700 mm above ground.
5. Base plates 47 by 112 (exterior) or 47 by 84 (interior) nailed to the floor, kitchen uses cast concrete curbs 50 by 115 or 50 by 84 with 12 by 100 mm anchor bolts instead.
6. Wall panels: 27 prefabricated panels (2745 mm high, 2550 mm or less long, studs 47 by 97 or 47 by 72, plywood 9 or 6 mm or cemboard 10 or 8 mm sheathing), erected corner first with temporary props, fixed with Pryda 2N10 nail plates at 610 mm and 3NA corner angles, wall plates 47 by 112 or 22 by 84 bind the tops. Non-load-bearing partitions are shorter, leaving clearance under the trusses.
7. Roof: gable trusses (35 by 72 mm members, 9 mm plywood gussets, span about 5770 mm, apex 1195 mm) at 1220 mm centres, 4 trusses extend 2415 plus 520 mm over the kitchen, diagonal and horizontal braces 22 by 97 mm, purlins 35 by 72 mm, fascia 20 by 145 mm, corrugated fibre-cement sheets, 6 mm plywood gables with vent holes.
8. Doors and windows: fitted into pre-framed openings (lintels 97 by 145 on cripple studs), doors 700, 770 or 840 mm wide by 2100 mm high, louvre windows typically 1055 by 1587 mm.
9. Stairs and railings: 2 stairs (rise 170 mm, run 230 mm, treads 47 by 250 mm, stringers 60 by 219 mm), railings 1000 mm high, balusters 20 by 20 mm.
10. Ceiling: noggings 38 by 50 mm at 610 mm between trusses, then 6 mm boards, main building only.
11. Services in drilled holes or notches per the rules, then skirtings 9 by 100 mm, beadings 9 by 72 mm, paint.
